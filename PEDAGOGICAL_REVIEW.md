## Methodology

All 270 core lessons (Modules 01-31) were read in full by eight independent
reviewers, each assigned a module cluster, each blind to the others' work.
For every lesson — exhaustively, not selectively — each reviewer answered
eight questions and converted the answer into a single 1-10 score:

1. Is the explanation intuitive (does Intuition/Mental Model build genuine
   understanding, or just restate the definition)?
2. Are examples sufficient (do the Indian/US/Historical examples actually
   illuminate the concept)?
3. Are analogies effective (does the Real World Analogy genuinely map onto
   the concept, or feel forced)?
4. Is the lesson too long (given its estimated time, does it pad or repeat)?
5. Is the lesson too short (does it leave real gaps or rush nuance)?
6. Does it overload beginners (too many new concepts/terms at once)?
7. Are quizzes appropriate (do they test understanding, not just recall)?
8. Is the homework meaningful (does it require genuine application)?

**Score rubric:** 9-10 = exemplary, a template for other lessons · 7-8 =
solid, minor polish possible · 5-6 = adequate but with one genuine,
specific weakness · 3-4 = a learner would likely come away confused ·
1-2 = fails its basic teaching function. Reviewers were explicitly told
not to rubber-stamp every lesson as 8-9 and to find genuine differences —
the resulting distribution below shows they did (nothing below a 5, but a
real spread from 5 to 9, not a flat wall of 8s).

## Cross-Cutting Patterns

Because each reviewer worked blind to the others, the following patterns
are especially significant — they were independently rediscovered by
multiple reviewers who never saw each other's work, not artifacts of one
reviewer's house style.

1. **Quizzes are the single most consistent weakness in the entire
   curriculum — every one of the 8 reviewers flagged it independently,
   without exception.** Across all 270 lessons, quizzes overwhelmingly test
   definitional recall ("what is X?") rather than application, calculation,
   or scenario judgment — even in lessons whose *homework* requires genuine
   math or chart analysis. This is the single highest-leverage, most
   consistent fix available: adding one scenario-based or calculation-based
   question per quiz would raise the curriculum's weakest dimension without
   touching its strongest (homework, analogies).
2. **Worked numerical examples are the biggest differentiator in
   math-heavy content.** Lessons that show a full worked calculation
   (`[08.03]` DuPont, `[20.04]` leverage, `[21.05]`-`[21.08]` payoffs,
   `[24.01]` expectancy) consistently score 8-9; lessons on comparably
   quantitative topics that describe a formula without computing it
   (`[09.03]` DCF, `[09.04]` DDM, `[20.06]`/`[20.09]` Futures,
   `[23.01]` Volatility, `[25.06]` Correlation Risk, `[27.03]` MPT,
   `[30.02]` Statistical Foundations) are almost exactly the curriculum's
   lowest-scoring lessons — and several of these assign homework requiring
   the very calculation the lesson body never demonstrates.
3. **Real World Analogies are the curriculum's greatest strength and its
   most variable dimension.** The best analogies do genuine explanatory
   work, not decoration — `[02.06]`'s parked bicycles for liquidity,
   `[04.06]`'s boats in a storm for crisis correlation, `[30.03]`'s two dogs
   on an elastic leash for cointegration, `[08.03]`'s race-with-a-shortcut
   for leverage-inflated ROE. Weaker lessons use analogies that are generic,
   drawn from an unfamiliar domain (corporate structures, engineering
   firms), or map only one dimension of a multi-part concept.
4. **Real, named historical events outperform hypothetical examples.**
   Module 31 (Case Studies, real events throughout) averages 8.0 — the
   highest of any content cluster except Trading Psychology — while
   Modules 29-30 (Algorithmic/Quantitative Trading, more often hypothetical
   Indian/US examples) average 7.4-7.5. The same pattern shows up within
   clusters: `[19.07]`'s real circuit-limit example outperforms other
   lessons' generic "Bank Nifty traders watch for X" template-fills.
5. **A recurring redundancy pattern at lesson-pair boundaries.** Several
   of the lowest-scoring lessons are a sub-variant of an immediately
   preceding lesson that doesn't earn its own full lesson's worth of new
   content: `[02.02]` vs. `[02.01]`, `[12.03]` Triple Top vs. `[12.02]`
   Double Top, `[14.03]` vs. `[12.04]`/`[13.04]`, `[16.02]` vs. `[15.02]`,
   and `[26.01]`/`[26.02]` vs. `[25.01]`/`[25.02]` (the same 1% rule taught
   three times across two modules).
6. **Beginner-overload risk is concentrated in specific, identifiable
   lessons, not spread diffusely across the curriculum.** `[01.07]` stacks
   TWAP/VWAP and mental-vs-hard stops onto lesson 7 of Module 01;
   `[07.08]` introduces 7+ dense terms in 30 minutes; `[11.02]` names five
   candlestick patterns at once; `[17.03]`/`[17.04]` each introduce seven
   Wyckoff acronyms; `[22.03]` uses "second derivative" without explaining
   what a derivative is to a no-calculus audience; `[30.02]` compresses
   four statistics concepts with no worked example. These are precise,
   fixable targets, not a systemic pacing problem.
7. **Trading Psychology (Module 28) defied the a-priori risk hypothesis.**
   Reviewers were briefed to watch for "motivational-poster-ish" content
   with low pedagogical value — instead it is the single highest-scoring
   module in the curriculum (8.63/10), consistently grounded in real
   research (Kahneman & Tversky, Pfeffer & Sutton) with homework requiring
   genuine personal application (journaling a real trade, designing an
   actual circuit-breaker rule), not abstract agreement with principles.
8. **Homework quality is a broad curriculum strength.** The large majority
   of lessons require real application — pulling and calculating from a
   real filing, marking up a real chart, computing an actual position size,
   personal journaling. The exceptions are isolated lookup-only tasks
   (finding a central bank Governor's name, converting time zones) that
   stand out precisely because they're inconsistent with the module's own
   otherwise-higher standard.
