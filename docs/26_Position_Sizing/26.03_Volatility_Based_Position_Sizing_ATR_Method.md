## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Volatility-based (ATR) position sizing** combines `[26.02]`'s fixed
fractional risk percentage with `[25.03]`'s ATR-based stop distance,
producing a sizing method that automatically adjusts for each specific
instrument's typical volatility — a highly volatile stock naturally
receives a smaller position size (wider stop in currency terms, same
percentage risk), while a calmer instrument receives a larger position
size (tighter stop in currency terms, same percentage risk), holding
the percentage-of-equity risk constant across both.

### Intuition
Two instruments — one calm, one volatile — using the *same fixed
currency stop distance* would produce very different real-world risk:
the volatile instrument's stop would be hit far more often by normal
noise, while the calm instrument's stop would rarely be tested at all.
Sizing based on each instrument's own ATR (`[25.03]`) instead ensures
the stop distance reflects that instrument's actual behavior, and the
position size formula then ensures the *percentage risk* stays
consistent regardless of which instrument is being traded.

### Mental Model
```
VOLATILITY-BASED (ATR) POSITION SIZING FORMULA:

  Stop Distance = N x ATR   (commonly N = 1.5-2.5, per [25.03])
  Risk Per Trade (currency) = Current Equity x Fixed % (e.g., 1%, [26.02])
  Position Size = Risk Per Trade (currency) / Stop Distance (currency)

  COMPARISON ACROSS TWO INSTRUMENTS (same account, same 1% risk):

  CALM Stock:    ATR = Rs 5.  Stop Distance (2x ATR) = Rs 10.
                 Rs 10,000 risk / Rs 10 = 1,000 shares (LARGER position)

  VOLATILE Stock: ATR = Rs 25. Stop Distance (2x ATR) = Rs 50.
                 Rs 10,000 risk / Rs 50 = 200 shares (SMALLER position)

  BOTH trades risk the SAME Rs 10,000 (1% of equity) if stopped out --
  despite very different position SIZES and very different per-share
  stop distances
```

### Visual Explanation
Charting position size against instrument ATR across several different
stocks typically shows an inverse relationship — higher-ATR (more
volatile) instruments receive proportionally smaller position sizes,
while lower-ATR (calmer) instruments receive proportionally larger
ones — with the *currency risk* held constant across all of them.

## Real World Analogy
ATR-based sizing is like a delivery company adjusting how much cargo
each truck carries based on the specific road conditions of its
assigned route — a truck on a smooth highway (low volatility) can
safely carry a fuller load, while a truck on a rough, unpredictable
mountain road (high volatility) carries less, even though both trucks
represent the same "acceptable risk" to the company's overall delivery
schedule.

## Indian Market Example
A trader compares two Nifty-listed stocks for a swing trade (`[24.06]`):
a large, stable FMCG stock with a low ATR, and a smaller, more volatile
mid-cap stock with a much higher ATR. Applying ATR-based sizing with a
consistent 1% account risk, the trader ends up with a much larger share
count in the FMCG stock and a much smaller share count in the mid-cap —
both trades still risking the same ₹ amount if stopped out.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader compares SPY (a broad, relatively lower-volatility index ETF)
against a smaller, higher-beta growth stock, applying the same ATR-
based sizing formula with identical 1% risk — arriving at a much larger
position size in SPY and a much smaller position size in the growth
stock, holding currency risk constant despite the underlying instruments'
very different typical daily ranges. **Hypothetical Example** for
illustration only.

## Historical Example
J. Welles Wilder Jr.'s introduction of ATR in 1978 (`[25.03]`'s
historical example) directly enabled this lesson's sizing method — prior
to ATR's availability, comparing "typical volatility" fairly across
instruments with very different price scales and behavior was
considerably more difficult, making consistent, volatility-adjusted
position sizing across a diverse instrument universe far less
systematic than it has since become.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional systematic strategies frequently use volatility-based
(sometimes called "vol-targeting") position sizing as their default
method precisely because it allows a single, consistent sizing formula
to be applied across large, diverse portfolios spanning very different
asset classes and volatility regimes (`[23.05]`) without requiring
instrument-by-instrument manual adjustment.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders using a flat, instrument-agnostic sizing rule (e.g.,
always the same share count) frequently end up taking dramatically
different real-world risk across their various positions without
realizing it — ATR-based sizing directly corrects this by tying
position size explicitly to each instrument's own behavior.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders often recalculate ATR periodically (not just at
entry) for longer-held positions, since an instrument's volatility
regime (`[23.05]`) itself can shift meaningfully during a multi-week
hold — a static ATR-based stop and size calculated only at entry can
become stale if the instrument's volatility character changes materially.

## Common Mistakes
- Using a flat, fixed share count or fixed currency stop across
  instruments with very different volatility characteristics.
- Calculating ATR once and never updating it for longer-held positions,
  even as the instrument's volatility regime shifts.
- Confusing ATR-based *stop placement* (`[25.03]`, where) with ATR-based
  *position sizing* (this lesson, how much) — the two work together but
  answer different questions.

## Misconceptions
- **"ATR-based sizing means every trade has the same position size."**
  It specifically produces *different* position sizes across
  instruments with different volatility, while holding currency risk constant.
- **"Volatility-based sizing is only relevant for professional/
  institutional traders."** Any retail trader comparing setups across
  multiple, differently-volatile instruments benefits from this method's
  consistency.
- **"Once calculated at entry, ATR-based position size never needs
  revisiting."** For longer holds, ATR can be recalculated periodically
  as the instrument's volatility character evolves.

## Key Takeaways
- ATR-based sizing combines `[25.03]`'s stop distance with `[26.02]`'s
  fixed fractional risk percentage to hold currency risk constant across
  instruments with different volatility.
- Higher-ATR (more volatile) instruments automatically receive smaller
  position sizes; lower-ATR (calmer) instruments receive larger ones.
- Enabled by J. Welles Wilder Jr.'s 1978 introduction of ATR, allowing
  fair volatility comparison across diverse instruments.
- Institutional "vol-targeting" strategies use essentially this same
  method at scale across diverse portfolios.

## Summary
Volatility-based (ATR) position sizing combines `[25.03]`'s stop
placement methodology with `[26.02]`'s fixed fractional formula to hold
currency risk consistent across instruments with very different typical
volatility, automatically producing smaller positions in volatile
instruments and larger positions in calmer ones — a method enabled by
Wilder's 1978 ATR indicator and widely used at institutional scale as
"vol-targeting." The next lesson turns to the Kelly Criterion — a more
mathematically sophisticated sizing approach that incorporates a
strategy's actual win rate and payoff ratio directly into the sizing formula.

4. Why might ATR need to be recalculated during a longer holding period?
   a) It never changes  b) The instrument's volatility regime can shift over time  c) Only for options  d) ATR is fixed at entry forever

5. True or False: ATR-based sizing produces the same position size regardless of an instrument's volatility.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Currency risk (percentage of equity) is held constant.
2. **b)** — Smaller, since its wider stop distance requires fewer units for the same risk.
3. **b)** — `[25.03]`'s ATR stop placement and `[26.02]`'s fixed fractional formula.
4. **b)** — The instrument's volatility regime can shift during a longer hold.
5. **b) False** — It specifically produces different position sizes based on each instrument's volatility.
