## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Defined-risk** options positions — long calls/puts (`[21.05]`),
spreads (`[21.08]`), iron condors/butterflies (`[21.10]`) — have a
mathematically fixed maximum loss known at entry, making this module's
sizing formulas (`[26.02]`–`[26.04]`) directly applicable using that
maximum loss as the "stop distance" equivalent. **Undefined-risk**
positions — naked short calls (`[21.06]`) and, to a lesser degree, naked
short puts (bounded only by the underlying reaching zero) — have no
fixed maximum loss, requiring a fundamentally different sizing approach
based on margin requirements, worst-case scenario modeling, and often
much smaller position sizes relative to account capital.

### Intuition
This module's core formula — Position Size = Risk Per Trade ÷ Stop
Distance — assumes a knowable, fixed maximum loss. For defined-risk
options structures, that maximum loss is simply the position's known
maximum loss (from Module 21's payoff formulas), making the formula
apply directly. For undefined-risk positions, there is no such fixed
number to divide by — sizing instead requires reasoning about a
worst-case, tail-risk scenario (`[25.07]`) rather than a precise formula.

### Mental Model
```
DEFINED-RISK OPTIONS SIZING (formula applies DIRECTLY):

  "Stop Distance" equivalent = Position's MAXIMUM LOSS (known at entry)
    Long Call/Put, [21.05]:  Max Loss = Premium Paid
    Vertical Spread, [21.08]: Max Loss = Net Premium Paid
    Iron Condor, [21.10]:     Max Loss = Wing Width - Net Credit

  Position Size (# of contracts) = Risk Per Trade (currency, [25.02])
                                    / Max Loss PER CONTRACT

UNDEFINED-RISK OPTIONS SIZING (formula does NOT apply directly):

  Naked Short Call, [21.06]: Max Loss = THEORETICALLY UNLIMITED
  Naked Short Put, [21.06]:  Max Loss = LARGE but capped (underlying to 0)

  -> Sizing instead requires: worst-case scenario modeling
     (e.g., "what if the underlying moves 3 standard deviations
     against me overnight?"), MARGIN requirement awareness, and
     typically MUCH smaller position sizes relative to account
     capital than defined-risk equivalents would suggest
```

### Visual Explanation
A defined-risk position's sizing calculation resembles any other
strategy's straightforward division — max loss per contract divides
cleanly into the risk-per-trade budget. An undefined-risk position's
sizing instead requires drawing out a range of hypothetical adverse
scenarios and checking that even a severe, low-probability outcome
remains survivable — a fundamentally different, scenario-based process
rather than a single clean formula.

```
                    v
```


## Real World Analogy
Sizing a defined-risk options position is like buying a fixed-price
package tour where the total cost is known upfront regardless of what
happens during the trip — straightforward budgeting. Sizing an
undefined-risk position is like agreeing to cover *any* costs a trip
might incur, with no upper limit specified — requiring the traveler to
think through worst-case scenarios (a medical emergency, an extended
delay) rather than simply budgeting a known, fixed number.

## Indian Market Example
A trader sizing a Nifty iron condor (`[21.10]`) with a known maximum
loss of ₹4,000 per lot and a ₹12,000 risk-per-trade budget can directly
calculate 3 lots using this module's standard formula. The same trader
considering a naked short Nifty call instead must model a severe
overnight gap scenario (e.g., driven by an unexpected macro announcement,
`[03.10]`) and size dramatically smaller — often to a single lot or
avoiding the naked structure entirely in favor of the defined-risk
alternative. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader sizing a defined-risk SPX credit spread (`[21.08]`) applies
this module's formula directly using the spread's known maximum loss.
The same trader considering a naked short put on a smaller-cap, more
volatile stock must instead account for the possibility of a severe
single-day decline (e.g., following an unexpected negative catalyst),
sizing the position at a small fraction of what the formula-based
defined-risk approach would otherwise suggest. **Hypothetical Example**
for illustration only.

## Historical Example
The Volmageddon event of February 2018 (`[22.08]`, `[23.06]`) is a
direct, real-world illustration of undefined-risk sizing failure —
several traders and even institutional products running short-
volatility positions without adequately modeling severe tail scenarios
suffered losses far exceeding what defined-risk position sizing
discipline would have permitted, a concrete historical case supporting
this lesson's caution around undefined-risk structures.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional risk desks typically impose much stricter capital
requirements and margin buffers on undefined-risk options books
specifically because of this sizing challenge — regulatory margin
frameworks (like SPAN margining, used by NSE and other exchanges)
attempt to formalize worst-case-scenario-based margin requirements
precisely because a clean, formula-based maximum loss doesn't exist for
these structures.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders are frequently drawn to naked option selling for its
apparently attractive premium income (`[24.09]`'s theta-selling
discussion), without fully internalizing that standard position sizing
formulas from earlier in this module don't cleanly apply — favoring
defined-risk structures (spreads, iron condors) specifically for their
formula-compatible, calculable maximum loss is a common and reasonable
risk-management preference for this reason.

## Professional Insights
Professional options traders who do run undefined-risk positions
typically combine strict margin discipline, continuous Greeks
monitoring (`[22.07]`), and predefined adjustment triggers (`[22.08]`)
specifically because static position sizing alone cannot fully capture
undefined-risk exposure — ongoing, active management substitutes for
the clean, one-time formula defined-risk structures allow.

## Common Mistakes
- Applying this module's standard Position Size = Risk ÷ Stop Distance
  formula directly to an undefined-risk naked option position, where no
  fixed "stop distance" genuinely exists.
- Sizing naked option positions based on margin availability alone,
  without independently modeling a severe adverse scenario.
- Assuming a defined-risk structure's maximum loss calculation
  automatically accounts for early assignment or other structural
  nuances beyond this lesson's introductory scope.

## Misconceptions
- **"Options sizing works exactly like stock sizing, using premium as
  the stop distance."** Only true for defined-risk structures — naked
  short options have no comparable fixed maximum loss to divide by.
- **"Margin availability is a sufficient sizing check for undefined-
  risk positions."** Margin availability reflects what a broker
  currently requires, not necessarily what a severe, low-probability
  scenario could actually cost.
- **"Defined-risk structures eliminate the need for careful sizing
  altogether."** They make sizing calculable and formulaic, but
  disciplined risk-per-trade limits (Module 25) still apply fully.

## Key Takeaways
- Defined-risk options structures (long options, spreads, iron condors)
  have a known maximum loss — this module's standard sizing formula
  applies directly.
- Undefined-risk structures (naked short options) require scenario-
  based, worst-case sizing rather than a clean formula, typically
  resulting in much smaller position sizes.
- Volmageddon (February 2018) is a real historical illustration of
  undefined-risk sizing failure at scale.
- Favoring defined-risk structures is a reasonable, common risk-
  management preference specifically because they remain compatible
  with this module's formula-based sizing approach.

## Summary
Position sizing for options requires distinguishing defined-risk
structures, where Module 21's known maximum loss formulas plug directly
into this module's standard sizing formula, from undefined-risk naked
option positions, where no comparable fixed maximum loss exists and
sizing instead requires scenario-based, worst-case modeling alongside
margin discipline and active Greeks management — a distinction
Volmageddon illustrated concretely and severely. The final lesson in
this module turns to scaling in and scaling out — adjusting position
size dynamically after entry, rather than only at the initial sizing decision.
