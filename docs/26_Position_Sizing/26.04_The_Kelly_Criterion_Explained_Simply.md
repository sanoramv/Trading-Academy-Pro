## Core Concepts

### Definition
The **Kelly Criterion**, developed by John L. Kelly Jr. at Bell Labs in
1956, is a mathematical formula for the position size that maximizes
long-run geometric growth rate of capital, given a strategy's win
probability and payoff ratio. Unlike `[26.02]`'s fixed fractional
method (which uses an arbitrary, chosen percentage like 1%), Kelly
*derives* a specific optimal percentage directly from the strategy's own
statistics. In practice, traders very rarely use "full Kelly" directly,
instead using **fractional Kelly** (commonly half-Kelly or quarter-
Kelly) to reduce sensitivity to estimation error and moderate the
sizing volatility full Kelly produces.

### Intuition
Kelly answers a precise mathematical question: "given exactly this win
rate and this payoff ratio, what fraction of capital, risked
repeatedly, grows the account fastest over the long run?" But the
formula assumes the win rate and payoff ratio inputs are known with
certainty — in real trading, these are only *estimates* from a
necessarily limited, journaled sample (`[24.12]`), and Kelly's output is
highly sensitive to errors in those estimates, which is precisely why
practitioners scale the raw output down substantially before applying it.
If the calculated Kelly percentage is zero or negative, this signals the
strategy has no genuine edge (or a negative one) at the estimated inputs
— the formula is telling you not to bet at all, not to bet a small or
negative amount. Because Kelly's inputs are only ever estimates, a
strategy that appears to have a small positive edge from a limited sample
may, in reality, have zero or negative edge; sizing positions — even
conservatively — against a strategy whose true edge is negative
guarantees eventual capital loss with certainty over a long enough series
of trades, not merely a lower growth rate. This is distinct from, and in
addition to, the danger of over-betting a genuinely positive edge
(covered next).

### Mental Model
```
KELLY CRITERION FORMULA:

  Kelly % = W - [(1 - W) / R]

  Where:
    W = Win Rate (as a decimal, e.g., 0.40 for 40%)
    R = Payoff Ratio (Average Win / Average Loss)

  EXAMPLE:
    W = 0.40 (40% win rate)
    R = 3.0  (average win is 3x average loss)

    Kelly % = 0.40 - [(1 - 0.40) / 3.0]
            = 0.40 - [0.60 / 3.0]
            = 0.40 - 0.20 = 0.20  ->  FULL KELLY suggests 20% risk

  FRACTIONAL KELLY (commonly used in PRACTICE):
    Half-Kelly    = 20% / 2 = 10%   (still likely too aggressive
                                       for most traders' comfort)
    Quarter-Kelly = 20% / 4 = 5%    (more commonly seen in practice,
                                       still often reduced FURTHER
                                       toward Module 25's 1-2% range)
```

### Visual Explanation
Plotting long-run compound growth rate against position size (as a
percentage of capital) produces a curve that rises, peaks exactly at the
full Kelly percentage, then falls sharply for sizes beyond it — this
falling-off-a-cliff behavior beyond the peak is precisely why
overestimating Kelly's inputs (and thus sizing too aggressively) is
particularly dangerous, motivating the fractional Kelly practice.

## Real World Analogy
The Kelly Criterion is like a precisely calculated recipe that assumes
perfectly accurate ingredient measurements — mathematically optimal *if*
every input is exactly correct, but a small measurement error (an
overestimated win rate, say) can meaningfully spoil the result;
experienced cooks (traders) deliberately use less than the "optimal"
calculated amount specifically to build in a margin for the inevitable
imprecision in their own measurements.

## Indian Market Example
A trader journals 150 trades of a Nifty theta-selling strategy
(`[24.09]`) and calculates an actual win rate of 65% with a payoff
ratio of 0.5 (small, frequent wins, occasional larger losses — this
strategy's typical shape, `[24.09]`). Applying the Kelly formula
directly might suggest an aggressive position size; the trader instead
applies quarter-Kelly, further reduced to align with the account's
overall 1-2% risk-per-trade rulebook (`[25.08]`) rather than using the
raw Kelly output. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader backtests a breakout strategy (`[24.04]`) with a 35% win rate
and a 4:1 payoff ratio, calculating a full Kelly percentage, then
deliberately applies only an eighth of that calculated value given the
relatively limited historical sample size available and known
sensitivity of Kelly's output to estimation error in win rate and
payoff ratio. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
The Kelly Criterion, published by John L. Kelly Jr. in a 1956 Bell
Labs paper originally addressing signal transmission and gambling
problems, was later popularized in investment contexts by figures
including Edward Thorp, who applied Kelly-based sizing principles to
both blackjack card counting and, subsequently, quantitative investment
strategies — a direct historical link between the formula's
mathematical origins and its modern trading applications.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional quant funds that use Kelly-informed sizing almost
universally apply heavily fractionalized versions, often incorporating
additional constraints (maximum position limits, correlation
adjustments per `[25.06]`) on top of the raw Kelly calculation, treating
the formula as one input among several rather than a standalone,
directly-applied sizing rule.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders who discover the Kelly Criterion sometimes apply it too
literally and too aggressively, using a limited backtest's win rate and
payoff ratio as if they were certain, permanent facts rather than
noisy, evolving estimates — a well-documented cause of Kelly-based
retail account blowups, motivating this lesson's strong fractional-Kelly caution.

## Professional Insights
Professional practitioners emphasize that Kelly's inputs (win rate,
payoff ratio) are themselves subject to the same regime-shift and edge-
decay concerns `[24.12]`'s journaling discipline addresses — a Kelly
calculation from last year's data may no longer reflect the strategy's
current, true statistics, reinforcing the need for periodic
recalculation alongside fractional application.

## Common Mistakes
- Applying full Kelly directly in live trading without fractionalizing.
- Calculating Kelly from a small, statistically insufficient sample and
  treating the result as precise and permanent.
- Ignoring that Kelly assumes independent, identically distributed
  trade outcomes — an assumption real markets don't perfectly satisfy.

## Misconceptions
- **"The Kelly Criterion gives the safest position size."** It gives the
  size that maximizes long-run *growth rate*, which is often far more
  aggressive (and volatile) than what most traders would consider "safe."
- **"A single backtest is enough to calculate a reliable Kelly
  percentage."** Kelly's inputs require a statistically meaningful,
  ideally out-of-sample-validated dataset, and remain estimates subject
  to revision.
- **"Fractional Kelly defeats the purpose of using Kelly at all."**
  Fractional Kelly specifically exists to manage input estimation
  error — a well-understood, standard practical refinement, not a
  compromise that abandons the method's value.

## Key Takeaways
- Kelly % = W − [(1−W)/R], where W is win rate and R is payoff ratio —
  mathematically optimal for long-run growth given certain inputs.
- Full Kelly is rarely used directly; fractional Kelly (commonly 1/4 to
  1/2) manages sensitivity to input estimation error.
- Developed by John L. Kelly Jr. (1956), popularized in investing by
  Edward Thorp.
- Kelly should be recalculated periodically from journaled data
  (`[24.12]`), not treated as a static, one-time figure.

## Summary
The Kelly Criterion mathematically derives the position size that
maximizes long-run compound growth from a strategy's win rate and
payoff ratio, but its extreme sensitivity to estimation error means
practitioners almost universally apply only a fraction of the raw
calculated value — a caution tracing to John L. Kelly Jr.'s original
1956 work and Edward Thorp's subsequent investment applications. Having
now covered three sizing methods — fixed fractional, ATR-based, and
Kelly — the next lesson turns to the special position sizing
considerations options introduce, given their defined-versus-undefined
risk structures from Module 21.

   a) W × R  b) W − [(1−W)/R]  c) R − W  d) W + R

2. Is full Kelly commonly applied directly in live trading?
   a) Yes, always  b) Rarely — fractional Kelly is standard practice due to estimation error  c) Only for options  d) Only for beginners

3. Who developed the Kelly Criterion?
   a) Van Tharp  b) John L. Kelly Jr.  c) Ralph Vince  d) J. Welles Wilder

4. Who is credited with popularizing Kelly-based sizing in investing?
   a) Warren Buffett  b) Edward Thorp  c) Benjamin Graham  d) Harry Markowitz

5. True or False: The Kelly Criterion gives the "safest" position size for any strategy.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — W − [(1−W)/R].
2. **b)** — Rarely; fractional Kelly is standard practice due to estimation error sensitivity.
3. **b)** — John L. Kelly Jr., in 1956.
4. **b)** — Edward Thorp.
5. **b) False** — Kelly maximizes long-run growth rate, which is often more aggressive/volatile than what most traders consider "safe."
