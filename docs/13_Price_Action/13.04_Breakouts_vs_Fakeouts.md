## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **breakout** is a genuine, sustained price move beyond a significant
level (support/resistance, `[02.04]`; trendline, `[10.05]`; supply/demand
zone, `[13.03]`) with follow-through. A **fakeout** (this academy's
recurring "false breakout" concept from `[02.06]`, `[10.08]`, and
`[12.10]`, now synthesized into a specific price-action checklist) is a
brief penetration that quickly reverses. This lesson consolidates the
specific **candle-level signatures** that help distinguish the two in
real time: breakouts typically show strong-bodied candles with volume
expansion (`[10.08]`) and minimal opposing wicks; fakeouts typically show
a long wick penetrating the level with a close back inside, often on
unremarkable volume.

### Intuition
This lesson doesn't introduce a new concept — it's the practical, candle-
level synthesis of a theme this academy has built since `[02.06]`'s
liquidity discussion: distinguishing genuine directional conviction from
a brief, liquidity-driven spike requires reading the *specific candle
signature* at the moment of the break, not just noting that a level was
touched.

### Mental Model
```
BREAKOUT SIGNATURE (favors genuine follow-through):
  - Strong-bodied candle CLOSING beyond the level (not just
    wicking beyond it)
  - MINIMAL opposing wick (little rejection back inside)
  - EXPANDING volume ([10.08])
  - Often followed by a "RETEST" that HOLDS (see below)

FAKEOUT SIGNATURE (favors false/reversing move):
  - LONG WICK penetrating the level, but CLOSE back INSIDE
    (the level "rejected" the attempt, per [11.02]'s wick-
    rejection concept)
  - Unremarkable or DECLINING volume
  - Often followed by a sharp reversal AWAY from the
    breakout direction
```

### Visual Explanation
The **retest** — price returning to the broken level shortly after a
breakout — offers a lower-risk confirmation approach: a genuine breakout
typically sees the old resistance now act as new support (`[02.04]`'s
polarity concept) on the retest, while a fakeout typically sees price
fail to hold and fall back through.

### Flowchart
```
[Price approaches/breaks a significant level]
        |
        v
[Check the CANDLE SIGNATURE: strong body closing beyond
 the level (favors breakout) or long wick rejecting back
 inside (favors fakeout)?]
        |
        v
[Check VOLUME ([10.08]): expanding (favors breakout) or
 unremarkable/declining (favors fakeout)?]
        |
        v
[For LOWER-RISK entry: WAIT for a RETEST of the broken
 level -- does old resistance now hold as new support

```

## Real World Analogy
A genuine breakout is like a crowd decisively pushing through and past a
barrier, then confidently regrouping just beyond it without any urge to
retreat — the barrier's old position (resistance) now serves as a
natural rallying point (new support) for the crowd's continued advance.
A fakeout is like a crowd surging against the barrier, briefly spilling
past it, but immediately having second thoughts and retreating back
behind it — the brief penetration reflected momentary pressure, not a
genuine, lasting shift in position.

## Indian Market Example
Nifty 50 and Bank Nifty breakouts above significant resistance levels are
routinely evaluated by Indian price action traders using exactly this
candle-signature and retest framework — a breakout candle with a strong
body and expanding volume, followed by a retest that holds above the old
resistance (now support), is treated as considerably higher-confidence
than an immediate, un-retested chase of the initial breakout candle.

## US Market Example
US swing traders analyzing individual stock breakouts above key
resistance specifically watch for the retest-and-hold pattern before
committing full position size — a practice directly reducing exposure to
the false-breakout risk this academy has repeatedly emphasized since
`[02.06]`, at the cost of a slightly less favorable average entry price
compared to buying the initial breakout candle immediately.

## Historical Example
The distinction between genuine breakouts and fakeouts became
particularly systematized in price action trading education following
widely-studied historical false-breakout episodes (including aspects of
the 2010 Flash Crash, `[01.07]`, `[02.06]`, `[10.08]`, `[12.10]`) —
this specific event has been referenced throughout this academy
precisely because it offers such a clear, extreme, historically
significant illustration of the genuine-versus-false-breakout distinction
this lesson formalizes into a practical checklist.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional execution algorithms (Module 29) specifically incorporate
retest-confirmation logic before committing full position size to a
breakout-based strategy — treating the initial breakout as a signal
worth monitoring closely, but not immediately, fully actionable without
the kind of candle-signature and volume confirmation this lesson
describes.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders are particularly vulnerable to fakeouts because the
psychological pull to immediately chase an exciting, fast-moving
breakout candle is strong — building the discipline to check candle
signature, volume, and ideally wait for a retest, consistent with this
academy's repeated confirmation theme, meaningfully reduces this common,
costly vulnerability.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders acknowledge a genuine trade-off in the retest
approach: waiting for confirmation reduces false-breakout risk but also
means missing the most explosive, immediate portion of genuine breakouts
that never retest before continuing — professional practice generally
accepts this trade-off, favoring the improved win-rate and reduced risk
per trade over capturing every single point of a genuine move's initial
thrust.

## Common Mistakes
- Chasing every breakout candle immediately without checking its
  signature or waiting for volume confirmation.
- Assuming every breakout will retest, and refusing to ever enter without
  one (some genuine breakouts never look back, requiring a size-adjusted,
  probabilistic approach rather than a rigid rule).
- Ignoring the specific wick-versus-body signature at the moment of the
  break, missing an obvious fakeout warning sign.

## Misconceptions
- **"Every breakout should be entered immediately upon the first close
  beyond the level."** As this lesson and `[12.10]` emphasize, immediate,
  unconfirmed entry carries meaningfully higher false-breakout risk than
  waiting for candle-signature and volume confirmation, or a retest.
- **"Every genuine breakout retests the broken level before
  continuing."** Many genuine breakouts never retest and simply continue
  — waiting for a retest is a risk-reducing choice with a real trade-off,
  not a guaranteed feature of every genuine move.
- **"Fakeouts are rare, unusual events not worth specifically checking
  for."** As this academy has repeatedly emphasized (`[02.06]`, `[10.08]`,
  `[12.10]`), fakeouts are a well-documented, relatively common
  phenomenon warranting specific, routine checking.

## Key Takeaways
- Genuine breakouts show strong-bodied candles, minimal opposing wicks,
  and expanding volume; fakeouts show long rejecting wicks and
  unremarkable volume.
- The retest — old resistance holding as new support — offers a lower-
  risk confirmation approach, at the cost of a less favorable average
  entry price and the risk of missing non-retesting genuine breakouts.
- This lesson synthesizes this academy's recurring genuine-vs-false-
  breakout theme (`[02.06]`, `[10.08]`, `[12.10]`) into a practical,
  candle-level checklist.
- Institutional execution practice and disciplined retail traders both
  favor confirmation over immediate, unconfirmed chasing.

## Quiz
1. What is a breakout's typical candle signature?
   a) A long wick with a close back inside the level
   b) A strong body closing beyond the level, with expanding volume
   c) Zero volume and a tiny body
   d) A dividend announcement

2. What is a fakeout's typical candle signature?
   a) A strong body with expanding volume
   b) A long wick penetrating the level, with the close back inside
   c) Identical to a genuine breakout
   d) A type of moving average

3. What is a "retest" in this context?
   a) A completely unrelated concept
   b) Price returning to the broken level shortly after breaking it, checking if it holds
   c) A type of dividend
   d) A government regulation

4. What trade-off does waiting for a retest involve?
   a) No trade-off exists
   b) Reduced false-breakout risk, at the cost of a less favorable entry and possibly missing non-retesting moves
   c) Guaranteed higher profits with no downside
   d) It eliminates all risk entirely

5. True or False: Every genuine breakout retests the broken level before
   continuing.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — A strong body closing beyond the level with expanding volume signals a genuine breakout.
2. **b)** — A long wick penetrating the level with a close back inside signals a fakeout.
3. **b)** — A retest is price returning to the broken level to check if it holds.
4. **b)** — Waiting for a retest reduces false-breakout risk but costs a less favorable entry and may miss non-retesting moves.
5. **b) False** — Many genuine breakouts never retest and simply continue.
