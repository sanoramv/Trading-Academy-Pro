## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Backtesting** is the process of testing a systematic trading
strategy's rules against historical price data to estimate how it
would have performed, providing the empirical basis for `[24.01]`'s
expectancy calculation before risking real capital. A trustworthy
backtest requires care around several well-documented pitfalls:
**look-ahead bias** (accidentally using information that wouldn't have
been available at the time of the simulated trade), **survivorship
bias** (testing only on instruments that still exist today, excluding
those that failed or were delisted), and unrealistic **transaction
cost modeling** (ignoring spread, brokerage, and slippage that would
have applied to real trades).

### Intuition
A backtest's entire value depends on how faithfully it replicates what
a trader would have *actually* experienced trading the strategy live,
in real time, with real constraints — any gap between the backtest's
assumptions and that reality produces a result that looks better (or
occasionally worse) than genuine live performance would be, and each of
this lesson's named pitfalls represents a specific, well-documented way
that gap commonly occurs.

### Mental Model
```
BACKTESTING PITFALLS CHECKLIST:

  1. LOOK-AHEAD BIAS: Does the backtest use ANY information that
     would NOT have been known at the actual moment of the simulated
     trade? (e.g., using a full day's closing data to trigger a
     trade that would have needed to be placed INTRADAY)

  2. SURVIVORSHIP BIAS: Does the test universe include ONLY
     currently-existing instruments, excluding companies that were
     delisted, went bankrupt, or merged during the test period?
     (artificially inflates results by excluding failures)

  3. TRANSACTION COSTS: Does the backtest account for REALISTIC
     spread, brokerage, and SLIPPAGE (the difference between
     expected and actual execution price)?

  4. OUT-OF-SAMPLE TESTING: Was ANY portion of the data held back
     and NOT used during strategy DEVELOPMENT, to test on genuinely
     unseen data? ([29.04]'s overfitting concern, next lesson)

  A backtest failing ANY of these checks may produce results that
  look FAR better than genuine live performance would deliver
```

### Visual Explanation
Comparing a naive backtest's equity curve (ignoring costs, using
survivorship-biased data) against a rigorous backtest's equity curve
(realistic costs, full historical universe including delisted
instruments) on the same underlying strategy typically shows the naive
version producing a meaningfully smoother, higher-return curve — the
gap between the two curves represents the cumulative effect of these pitfalls.

## Real World Analogy
A flawed backtest is like evaluating a diet plan's effectiveness by
only studying people who successfully lost weight and kept it off
(survivorship bias), assuming perfect availability of exactly the right
foods at exactly the right times with no cost or inconvenience
(unrealistic transaction costs), and secretly knowing in advance which
weeks would be stressful and adjusting the plan retroactively
(look-ahead bias) — the resulting "success rate" would look
dramatically better than what a real person, facing real constraints
and unknown future stress, would actually experience.

## Indian Market Example
A trader backtests a Nifty stock-picking strategy using only currently-
listed NSE stocks, unknowingly excluding companies that were delisted
or went bankrupt during the test period (survivorship bias) — producing
an artificially inflated historical win rate and return figure that
would not have been achievable by a trader who genuinely couldn't have
known in advance which companies would survive. **Hypothetical
Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader backtests an intraday strategy using end-of-day closing prices
to trigger trades that, in reality, would have needed to be placed
based on intraday price action unavailable until later in the actual
trading session (look-ahead bias) — producing backtest results that
assume perfect, impossible foreknowledge of how each day would unfold.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
Backtesting pitfalls, particularly survivorship bias, gained
significant academic and industry attention following research in the
1990s (including work examining mutual fund performance databases,
which frequently excluded closed/failed funds) demonstrating that
survivorship bias alone could substantially inflate apparent historical
returns across an entire research field — a cautionary body of work
directly informing modern backtesting best practices, including the
checklist this lesson provides.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional quant research desks maintain point-in-time historical
databases specifically engineered to avoid look-ahead bias (data
reflecting exactly what was known and available at each historical
moment, not revised with hindsight) and dedicated survivorship-bias-
free universes including delisted securities — infrastructure
investments individual retail backtesters typically lack access to.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders using accessible backtesting platforms should
specifically verify whether the platform's historical data includes
delisted/failed instruments and whether transaction costs can be
realistically modeled — many free or low-cost retail backtesting tools
default to survivorship-biased, cost-free assumptions unless explicitly configured otherwise.

## Professional Insights
Professional quant researchers treat a strategy's out-of-sample
backtest performance (data never used during development) as
meaningfully more trustworthy than in-sample performance (data used to
design and tune the strategy), since in-sample results can be
artificially inflated through the overfitting process `[29.04]` examines
next, even without any of this lesson's other named pitfalls being present.

## Common Mistakes
- Backtesting using only currently-surviving instruments, ignoring
  delisted or failed ones.
- Assuming zero or unrealistically low transaction costs in backtest calculations.
- Using data that wouldn't have been genuinely available at the
  simulated trade moment (look-ahead bias).
- Never reserving genuinely unseen, out-of-sample data for final validation.

## Misconceptions
- **"A backtest with an impressive historical return is sufficient
  evidence to trade a strategy live."** Without checking for the
  pitfalls this lesson describes, an impressive backtest may simply
  reflect biased or unrealistic assumptions rather than a genuine, tradeable edge.
- **"Transaction costs are a minor detail that doesn't meaningfully
  affect backtest results."** For higher-frequency strategies
  particularly, realistic transaction costs can substantially or
  entirely erode an otherwise apparently profitable backtest.
- **"Survivorship bias is a minor, theoretical concern."** Documented
  research has shown it can substantially inflate apparent historical
  performance across entire research fields, not just isolated cases.

## Key Takeaways
- Backtesting estimates a strategy's historical performance, providing
  the empirical basis for `[24.01]`'s expectancy calculation.
- Look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, and unrealistic transaction cost
  modeling are the primary, well-documented pitfalls producing
  misleadingly optimistic results.
- Out-of-sample testing (data never used during development) provides a
  meaningfully more trustworthy performance indicator than in-sample results.
- Retail backtesting platforms often default to biased assumptions
  unless explicitly configured to avoid them.

## Summary
Backtesting provides the empirical foundation for validating a
systematic strategy's expectancy before live deployment, but requires
careful attention to look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, and realistic
transaction cost modeling — each a well-documented, common way a
backtest's results can diverge substantially from genuine live
performance. The next lesson turns to overfitting and curve-fitting — a
related but distinct pitfall specifically arising from excessive
strategy-tuning against historical data, even when this lesson's other
pitfalls have been carefully avoided.

## Quiz
1. What is look-ahead bias?
   a) Using future price data to make a decision correctly  b) Using information that wouldn't have been available at the simulated trade moment  c) A margin requirement  d) A stop-loss method

2. What is survivorship bias in backtesting?
   a) Testing on all historical instruments equally  b) Testing only currently-existing instruments, excluding delisted/failed ones  c) A type of stop-loss  d) A tax rule

3. Why does transaction cost modeling matter in backtesting?
   a) It doesn't  b) Ignoring costs can make an unprofitable strategy look profitable  c) Costs always improve returns  d) Only relevant for bonds

4. What is out-of-sample testing?
   a) Testing on data used during strategy development  b) Testing on data genuinely never used during development  c) Not testing at all  d) Testing only on delisted stocks

5. True or False: An impressive backtest return is sufficient evidence to trade a strategy live without further checks.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Using information that wouldn't have been available at the actual simulated trade moment.
2. **b)** — Testing only currently-existing instruments, excluding delisted or failed ones.
3. **b)** — Ignoring costs can make an unprofitable strategy look profitable.
4. **b)** — Testing on data genuinely never used during strategy development.
5. **b) False** — The backtest must be checked for look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, and realistic costs first.
