## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **trading bot's architecture** is the structural blueprint connecting
data, decision logic, execution, and monitoring into one automated
system. At minimum, a simple bot requires: a **data feed module**
(receiving live price/quote data), a **strategy/signal module**
(applying the coded rules from `[29.02]` to generate buy/sell signals), a
**risk management module** (enforcing Modules 25-26's sizing and stop-
loss rules independently of the signal logic), an **execution module**
(placing orders via a broker API, potentially using `[29.05]`'s
execution algorithms for larger orders), and a **logging/monitoring
module** (recording every action for later review, `[24.12]`'s
journaling discipline automated).

### Intuition
Every component in a trading bot maps directly onto a concept this
academy has already covered conceptually — the architecture simply
formalizes each into a distinct, testable software component.
Critically, the risk management module should be architecturally
*independent* from the strategy/signal module — this separation of
concerns ensures a bug or edge case in signal logic cannot bypass risk
limits, directly implementing `[25.08]`'s rulebook as a structural,
enforced safeguard rather than a document a human might forget to consult.

### Mental Model
```
SIMPLE TRADING BOT ARCHITECTURE (5 core components):

  1. DATA FEED MODULE
     Receives live price/quote/volume data from broker API/exchange
        |
        v
  2. STRATEGY/SIGNAL MODULE
     Applies CODED rules (Module 24's framework, [29.02]'s
     systematized logic) to generate BUY/SELL/HOLD signals
        |
        v
  3. RISK MANAGEMENT MODULE (ARCHITECTURALLY INDEPENDENT --
     critical design principle)
     Enforces Module 25/26's rules REGARDLESS of what the signal
     module outputs -- position size limits, max daily loss,
     correlation checks, [25.06]
        |
        v
  4. EXECUTION MODULE
     Places the ACTUAL order via broker API -- potentially using
     TWAP/VWAP/Iceberg logic, [29.05], for larger orders
        |
        v
  5. LOGGING/MONITORING MODULE
     Records EVERY signal, decision, and execution -- automated
     version of [24.12]'s journaling discipline; alerts a human
     if anomalies occur
```

bypassable steps but a mandatory architectural checkpoint.

## Real World Analogy
A trading bot's architecture is like a factory assembly line with a
mandatory quality-control checkpoint built into the physical layout —
no product can reach the shipping dock without physically passing
through the inspection station, regardless of how confident the earlier
production stages were; the risk management module plays the identical
structural role, architecturally impossible to skip.

## Indian Market Example
A trader builds a simple bot automating the Nifty theta-selling
strategy from `[24.09]`: a data feed module pulling live option chain
data via a broker's API, a signal module checking Delta-based strike
selection criteria, an independent risk module enforcing a maximum
₹15,000 daily loss limit and position-count cap, an execution module
placing the iron condor legs, and a logging module recording every
signal and decision to a database for later journaling review.

## US Market Example
A trader builds a simple bot automating a trend-following strategy
(`[24.02]`) on a basket of liquid ETFs: a data feed pulling daily
closing prices, a signal module calculating moving average crossovers,
an independent risk module enforcing `[26.02]`'s fixed fractional

## Historical Example
The architectural principle of separating risk management from
strategy logic gained particular prominence following several
well-documented algorithmic trading incidents, including Knight
Capital's August 2012 trading error, where a software deployment issue
caused a runaway automated system to execute erroneous trades over
roughly 45 minutes, resulting in a loss exceeding $440 million and
Knight Capital's eventual acquisition — a widely cited case study in
algorithmic trading education for the critical importance of robust
risk gates, kill switches, and thorough testing before live deployment.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional algorithmic trading systems implement multiple redundant
safeguards beyond this lesson's simplified architecture — including
hardware and software "kill switches" that can instantly halt all
trading activity, mandatory pre-trade risk checks enforced at the
exchange connectivity level (not just within the bot's own code), and
extensive testing environments that mirror live conditions before any
new code reaches production.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders building their own bots should specifically resist the
temptation to skip or weaken the risk management module for simplicity
— the Knight Capital incident, though occurring at institutional scale,
illustrates a universal principle: automated systems can fail in ways
that compound losses far faster than a human would ever allow, making
robust, architecturally-independent risk gates non-negotiable regardless of scale.

## Professional Insights
Professional algorithmic trading developers emphasize extensive testing
in a paper-trading or simulation environment (using live data but no
real capital) before any live deployment, and recommend deploying with
minimal position sizes initially even after successful testing, since
live market conditions (latency, slippage, edge cases) frequently
reveal issues that backtesting and simulation alone don't catch.

## Common Mistakes
- Combining strategy/signal logic and risk management into a single,
  non-separated code module, allowing a signal-logic bug to bypass risk limits.
- Deploying a bot to live trading without adequate simulation/paper-
  trading testing first.
- Neglecting logging and monitoring, leaving no way to diagnose issues
  after an unexpected loss or system failure.

## Misconceptions
- **"A trading bot only needs the strategy logic — risk management can
  be added later."** Risk management should be architecturally built in
  from the start, independent of strategy logic, per this lesson's
  core design principle.
- **"Once a bot passes backtesting, it's ready for full-scale live
  deployment."** Professional practice recommends simulation/paper
  trading and minimal initial live position sizes, since live
  conditions reveal issues backtesting alone doesn't catch.
- **"Automated systems can't fail as badly as human traders."** The
  Knight Capital incident demonstrates automated systems can compound
  losses far faster than human oversight would typically allow, absent
  robust safeguards.

## Key Takeaways
- A simple trading bot requires five core components: data feed,
  strategy/signal, risk management, execution, and logging/monitoring.
- Risk management should be architecturally independent from strategy
  logic — a mandatory gate, not an optional check.
- The Knight Capital incident (2012, ~$440 million loss) is a real,
  widely cited case study for the necessity of robust risk gates and
  thorough testing before live deployment.
- Professional practice recommends simulation/paper trading and minimal
  initial live sizing even after successful backtesting.
