# [29.01] What Is Algorithmic Trading

- Recognize algo trading's substantial and growing share of both Indian and global market volume.

## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Algorithmic trading** (algo trading) is the use of computer programs
to execute trading decisions — entry, exit, and order routing —
according to a predefined set of rules, without requiring a human to
manually place each order. It ranges from simple automation of a single
strategy's entry/exit signals (Module 24's frameworks, coded into
rules) to sophisticated execution algorithms managing large order
placement (`[29.05]`) to high-frequency trading operating at
microsecond speeds (`[29.08]`). All forms share the same core property:
a written, precise, testable rule set (`[24.01]`'s validity criteria)
executed mechanically rather than through in-the-moment human judgment.

### Intuition
Every psychological failure Module 28 documented — tilt, FOMO,
confirmation bias, loss aversion — occurs at the specific moment a
human makes a discretionary decision under live market pressure.
Algorithmic trading removes that moment entirely for the automated
portion of the process: a strategy coded into precise rules executes
identically regardless of the programmer's emotional state at the time
the code runs, structurally closing `[28.01]`'s knowing-doing gap for
whatever portion of the process is automated.

### Mental Model
```
CATEGORIES OF ALGORITHMIC TRADING (by purpose):

  1. STRATEGY AUTOMATION: coding a Module 24 strategy's exact
     entry/exit rules so a computer executes them automatically
     (e.g., automating [24.02]'s trend-following signal generation)

  2. EXECUTION ALGORITHMS: managing HOW a large order is placed
     into the market to minimize price impact, [29.05] (TWAP, VWAP,
     Iceberg) -- used regardless of WHY the trade was decided

  3. MARKET MAKING: continuously quoting both buy and sell prices,
     profiting from the bid-ask spread and Theta-like mechanisms,
     [21.06]/[22.04] -- typically institutional/professional scale

  4. HIGH-FREQUENCY TRADING (HFT): operating at microsecond speeds,
     [29.08] -- a distinct, extreme end of the algo trading spectrum,
     NOT synonymous with algo trading as a whole
```

## Real World Analogy
Algorithmic trading is like an airplane's autopilot system: it executes
precise, predefined flight parameters mechanically and consistently,
free from the pilot's momentary fatigue or emotional state, but it
still requires careful initial programming, ongoing monitoring, and a
human ultimately responsible for oversight — autopilot doesn't
eliminate the need for skilled aviation judgment, it changes where and
how that judgment is applied.

## Indian Market Example
An Indian retail trader who has developed and journaled a Nifty theta-
selling strategy (`[24.09]`) over many months codes the exact entry
criteria (strike selection by Delta, `[22.02]`; IV regime check,
`[23.05]`) into an automated system connected via a broker's API,
removing the in-the-moment discretion that previously introduced
inconsistency into their manual execution. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A US-based systematic trader automates a trend-following strategy
(`[24.02]`) across a basket of liquid futures contracts, coding the
moving-average trend filter, pullback entry trigger, and trailing stop
logic precisely so the system executes identically regardless of market
conditions or the trader's own emotional state on any given day.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
Algorithmic trading grew substantially in both US and Indian markets
following the broader shift to electronic trading infrastructure —
NSE's electronic transition beginning in 1994 (`[01.04]`) laid the
groundwork, with SEBI formally permitting direct market access (DMA)
and algorithmic trading for institutional participants starting around
2008, a regulatory milestone `[29.07]` examines in detail.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional trading desks now route a substantial majority of order
flow through some form of algorithmic execution — even fundamentally
discretionary investment decisions (which stock to buy, based on
`[08.10]`'s analysis) are typically executed via algorithmic order-
placement systems (`[29.05]`) to minimize market impact, illustrating
that algo trading and discretionary decision-making are not mutually exclusive.

## Retail Perspective
Retail algorithmic trading has become increasingly accessible in India
through broker-provided APIs (subject to SEBI's regulatory framework,
`[29.07]`) and globally through numerous retail-accessible platforms,
though building and maintaining a genuinely robust automated system
requires meaningfully more technical skill and ongoing diligence than
many retail traders initially anticipate.

## Professional Insights
Professional algorithmic traders emphasize that automation doesn't
eliminate the need for sound strategy (Module 24), risk management
(Module 25), and position sizing (Module 26) — it only changes *how*
those rules are executed, from manual to mechanical. A poorly designed
strategy, automated, simply loses money faster and more consistently
than the same poor strategy traded manually.

## Common Mistakes
- Believing automation itself creates a trading edge, rather than
  simply executing whatever edge (or lack thereof) the underlying
  strategy already has.
- Deploying an automated system without adequate backtesting (`[29.03]`)
  or regulatory compliance awareness (`[29.07]`).
- Assuming automated systems require no ongoing human monitoring once deployed.

## Misconceptions
- **"Algorithmic trading and high-frequency trading are the same
  thing."** HFT is one specific, extreme category within the much
  broader algorithmic trading spectrum, not a synonym for it.
- **"Automating a strategy improves its underlying edge."** Automation
  changes execution consistency, not the strategy's fundamental,
  tested expectancy (`[24.01]`) — a poor strategy automated remains a poor strategy.
- **"Algo trading eliminates the need for human oversight."** Automated
  systems still require monitoring, maintenance, and periodic review,
  particularly as market conditions and regime (`[23.05]`) evolve.

## Key Takeaways
- Algorithmic trading executes predefined rules mechanically via
  computer programs, spanning simple strategy automation to sophisticated HFT.
- It structurally addresses `[28.01]`'s knowing-doing gap by removing
  in-the-moment human discretion from the automated portion of the process.
- Automation does not create an edge on its own — it executes whatever
  edge (or lack thereof) the underlying strategy already has.
- SEBI formally permitted algorithmic trading and direct market access
  for institutions starting around 2008, a milestone `[29.07]` develops further.

## Summary
Algorithmic trading executes trading decisions mechanically according to
predefined rules, spanning a wide spectrum from simple, retail-
accessible strategy automation to sophisticated institutional execution
algorithms, market making, and high-frequency trading, structurally
addressing `[28.01]`'s knowing-doing gap by removing in-the-moment
human discretion for whatever portion of the process is automated. The
next lesson examines the trade-offs between rule-based systematic
trading and discretionary trading directly, since the choice between
them is not simply "automation is always better."

3. **b)** — No, it executes whatever edge the underlying strategy already has.
4. **b)** — No, HFT is one extreme category within the broader algo trading spectrum.
