## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Order execution algorithms** solve a distinct problem from strategy
selection (Module 24): given a decision has already been made to buy or
sell a specific quantity, *how* should that order actually be placed
into the market to minimize adverse **market impact** (the tendency for
a large order to move the price unfavorably against the trader as it
executes, `[01.08]`)? **TWAP (Time-Weighted Average Price)** splits a
large order into smaller pieces executed at even intervals over a
specified time period. **VWAP (Volume-Weighted Average Price)** splits
a large order to match the market's typical volume pattern throughout
the day, executing more heavily during high-volume periods and less
during low-volume periods. **Iceberg orders** display only a small
visible portion of a much larger total order, automatically replenishing
the visible portion as it fills, concealing the order's true full size
from other market participants.

### Intuition
A single, large market order placed all at once can move the price
significantly against the trader before the full quantity is filled —
particularly for less liquid instruments. Each execution algorithm
manages this differently: TWAP spreads execution evenly across time
(simple, predictable), VWAP spreads execution according to when the
market is naturally most liquid (potentially less market impact, since
it trades more when more natural counterparty volume exists), and
Iceberg conceals order size specifically to avoid signaling large
intent to other market participants who might trade against it.

### Mental Model
```
EXECUTION ALGORITHM COMPARISON:

  TWAP: Split order EVENLY across TIME
    e.g., Buy 10,000 shares over 2 hours -> execute ~83 shares/minute,
    REGARDLESS of current volume conditions

  VWAP: Split order to MATCH the market's typical VOLUME PATTERN
    e.g., Buy MORE during high-volume periods (market open/close),
    LESS during typically quiet mid-day periods -- aims to achieve
    an average execution price close to the day's VWAP benchmark

  ICEBERG: Display only a SMALL portion of the TOTAL order
    e.g., True order = 50,000 shares; DISPLAYED order = 500 shares
    at a time, automatically REFILLING as each 500-share slice fills
    -- conceals TOTAL size from other market participants

  ALL THREE address: "HOW to execute a DECIDED trade with
  minimal market impact" -- NOT "WHETHER/WHEN to trade" (that's
  Module 24's strategy question, answered BEFORE execution begins)
```

### Visual Explanation
A TWAP execution's fill pattern, charted over time, shows a steady,
even trickle of small trades at regular intervals — a VWAP execution's
fill pattern shows trades clustering more heavily near market open and
close (typically higher-volume periods) with lighter activity mid-day —
and an Iceberg order's visible order book entry shows a small, constant
size that refreshes repeatedly rather than the true total size ever appearing.

## Real World Analogy
Execution algorithms are like different strategies for selling a large
quantity of a commodity without crashing its local market price: TWAP
is like selling a fixed amount every hour regardless of how busy the
market is; VWAP is like selling more heavily when the market is
naturally busiest (matching typical buyer activity); Iceberg is like
only ever displaying a small sample of goods for sale at a time, even
though a much larger total supply is available, specifically to avoid
alarming other sellers into undercutting the price.

## Indian Market Example
An institutional trader needing to buy a large quantity of a mid-cap
NSE stock uses a VWAP algorithm to spread the purchase across the
trading day, matching the stock's typical intraday volume pattern —
aiming to achieve an average execution price close to the day's actual
VWAP rather than pushing the price up sharply with a single large order
during a low-liquidity period. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A fund manager needing to sell a large position in a US stock uses an
Iceberg order to conceal the total order size, displaying only a small
visible portion at a time to avoid signaling to other market
participants (including potential HFT firms, `[29.08]` previewed) that
a large seller is present, which might otherwise cause the price to
move unfavorably in anticipation. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
VWAP became a standard institutional execution benchmark from the
1980s-90s onward as electronic trading and algorithmic execution tools
matured, with many institutional trading mandates explicitly measuring
a trader's execution quality against the day's VWAP as the performance
standard — a practice that remains widespread in institutional
transaction cost analysis today.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional trading desks routinely use sophisticated variants of
these basic execution algorithms (adding real-time adaptivity, dark
pool routing, and machine-learning-based volume prediction) as standard
practice for any order large enough to risk meaningful market impact —
execution quality is treated as a distinct, measurable skill separate
from the underlying investment decision itself.

## Retail Perspective
Most retail trade sizes are small enough relative to typical market
liquidity that market impact is rarely a significant concern, meaning
retail traders rarely need to build or use TWAP/VWAP/Iceberg algorithms
directly — but understanding these concepts helps retail traders
interpret institutional order flow patterns visible in market data
(e.g., unusually large volume clustering near VWAP-typical times) and
understand why very large trades don't simply appear as single, visible orders.

## Professional Insights
Professional execution traders emphasize that no single execution
algorithm is universally best — TWAP's predictability can be exploited
by other market participants who recognize the pattern, VWAP's
volume-matching can still produce significant impact in genuinely
illiquid instruments, and Iceberg orders can still leak information
through the repeated refill pattern itself, requiring ongoing judgment
and adaptation rather than pure formulaic reliance.

## Common Mistakes
- Confusing execution algorithms (how to place an already-decided
  trade) with strategy algorithms (whether/when to trade at all,
  Module 24) — they answer entirely different questions.
- Using a large market order for a position size that meaningfully
  exceeds the instrument's typical liquidity, without considering
  execution algorithm alternatives.
- Assuming execution algorithms are only relevant to institutions,
  missing their conceptual relevance for understanding market
  microstructure even at retail scale.

## Misconceptions
- **"TWAP/VWAP/Iceberg algorithms decide whether or when to trade."**
  They address purely HOW to execute an already-decided trade —
  the whether/when decision belongs to Module 24's strategy frameworks,
  made before execution begins.
- **"These execution algorithms are only relevant to large
  institutions."** While retail trade sizes rarely require them
  directly, understanding the concepts helps interpret market
  microstructure and institutional order flow patterns.
- **"VWAP always produces a better execution price than TWAP."**
  Neither is universally superior — the better choice depends on the
  specific instrument's liquidity pattern and the trader's specific goals.

## Key Takeaways
- Execution algorithms solve how to place an already-decided trade with
  minimal market impact — distinct from strategy algorithms (Module
  24's whether/when decisions).
- TWAP splits an order evenly across time; VWAP matches the market's
  typical volume pattern; Iceberg conceals total order size.
- VWAP became a standard institutional execution benchmark from the
  1980s-90s onward, still widely used in transaction cost analysis today.
- Retail trade sizes rarely require these algorithms directly, but
  understanding them illuminates institutional order flow and market microstructure.

## Summary
Order execution algorithms — TWAP, VWAP, and Iceberg — solve the
distinct, practical problem of placing an already-decided trade with
minimal market impact, a concern primarily relevant at institutional
scale but conceptually useful for understanding market microstructure
even for retail traders whose typical order sizes rarely require these
tools directly. The next lesson turns to building a simple trading bot's

   a) Display the full order size  b) Display only a small visible portion of a much larger total order  c) Cancel automatically  d) Only work for options

4. **b)** — Display only a small visible portion of a much larger total order, refilling as it fills.
5. **b) False** — That decision belongs to strategy (Module 24), made before execution begins.
