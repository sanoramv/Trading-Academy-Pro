## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Rule-based (systematic) trading** executes decisions according to
precisely defined, unambiguous criteria that could in principle be
coded and automated (`[29.01]`), removing subjective, in-the-moment
judgment. **Discretionary trading** relies on a trader's subjective
judgment, pattern recognition, and contextual interpretation — even
when following a structured playbook (`[13.10]`) — to make entry, exit,
and sizing decisions that aren't reducible to a fully precise rule set.
Many practical trading approaches are **hybrid**, systematizing some
components (e.g., risk management and position sizing, Modules 25-26)
while retaining discretion over others (e.g., overall market context
assessment).

### Intuition
Some trading concepts translate cleanly into precise rules — a moving
average crossover, an RSI threshold, an ATR-based stop distance — while
others resist full systematization — reading how "convincing" a
candlestick pattern looks in context (`[11.08]`), or judging whether
price action at a key level "feels" like genuine absorption versus
distribution (`[13.03]`). The choice between systematic and
discretionary approaches, or some hybrid, should follow from how
precisely a given trader's actual edge can be specified, not from an
assumption that either approach is universally superior.

## Real World Analogy
Rule-based versus discretionary trading resembles the difference
between a chess engine (fully systematic, evaluating positions via
precise, programmable criteria) and a human grandmaster's intuitive
"feel" for a position that resists full explanation even to the
grandmaster themselves — both can be highly skilled, but they operate
through fundamentally different processes, and many strong human
players today use engine analysis (systematic) to inform, not replace,
their own judgment (discretionary).

## Indian Market Example
A trader systematizes their Nifty theta-selling entry criteria
(`[24.09]`) completely — Delta-based strike selection, IV regime check
— but retains discretionary judgment over whether to skip trading
entirely around unusual, non-recurring events (e.g., an unscheduled
RBI announcement, `[03.10]`'s May 2022 example) that don't fit neatly
into the systematic framework's historical training data.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader fully automates a trend-following system's entry, exit, and
sizing (`[24.02]`, coded per `[29.01]`) but retains manual discretion
over whether to reduce overall system exposure during a subjectively-
assessed period of unusual macro uncertainty, blending systematic
execution with a discretionary risk overlay. **Hypothetical Example**
for illustration only.

## Historical Example
The debate between systematic and discretionary approaches has a long
history in trading, with figures like Ed Seykota and Richard Dennis
(the Turtle Traders, `[24.02]`) representing the systematic tradition
from the 1970s-80s, while discretionary macro traders like George Soros
represented a contrasting, judgment-heavy approach during the same
era — both traditions have produced documented long-term success,
suggesting neither approach is universally superior, only differently suited to different traders and edges.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional quant funds (Module 30 previewed) operate almost entirely
systematically, viewing discretionary human judgment as an
unpredictable, unbacktestable source of variance to be minimized — while
many institutional discretionary macro and fundamental funds explicitly
value human judgment as their core, differentiated edge, illustrating
that institutional practice spans the full spectrum, not a single approach.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders often benefit from being honest about which parts of
their actual edge are genuinely systematic versus discretionary —
attempting to fully automate a fundamentally discretionary process
(without a truly precise rule set) frequently produces a worse
automated version of a decent discretionary process, rather than genuine improvement.

## Professional Insights
Professional systematic traders emphasize that a rule-based system's
main advantage isn't necessarily *better* decisions than a skilled
discretionary trader would make — it's *consistent* decisions,
removing the variance `[28.01]`'s knowing-doing gap introduces into
discretionary execution, which can matter more than marginal decision quality for many traders.

## Common Mistakes
- Attempting to fully systematize a genuinely discretionary, context-
  dependent judgment without a precise enough rule set, producing a
  poor automated approximation.
- Assuming systematic approaches are inherently superior to
  discretionary ones, ignoring that both traditions have produced
  successful, documented long-term traders.
- Leaving risk management and position sizing discretionary even when
  entry/exit signals are systematized, undermining `[26.01]`'s
  consistency principle.

## Misconceptions
- **"Systematic trading is always more profitable than discretionary
  trading."** Both traditions have documented long-term success —
  neither is universally superior; fit depends on the trader's actual, specific edge.
- **"Discretionary trading means having no rules at all."** Structured
  discretionary approaches (like `[13.10]`'s playbook) still follow a
  defined process, just one that retains subjective, contextual
  judgment rather than fully precise, codeable criteria.
- **"Risk management should only be systematized if entry signals
  are also systematized."** Position sizing and risk rules (Modules
  25-26) should be applied consistently regardless of whether entry
  logic is systematic or discretionary.

## Key Takeaways
- Rule-based systems execute precisely defined, codeable criteria;
  discretionary trading relies on subjective, contextual judgment —
  many practical approaches are hybrids of both.
- The choice should follow from how precisely a specific edge can be
  defined, not an assumption that either approach is universally superior.
- Both systematic (Seykota, Dennis) and discretionary (Soros) traditions
  have produced documented long-term success historically.
- Risk management and position sizing (Modules 25-26) should be
  systematized consistently, regardless of whether entry/exit is systematic or discretionary.

## Summary
Rule-based systematic trading and discretionary trading represent two
ends of a spectrum rather than a binary choice — some trading concepts
translate cleanly into precise, codeable rules while others resist full
systematization, with many practical approaches blending systematic
execution and risk management with retained discretionary judgment over
context. The next lesson turns to backtesting fundamentals — how to
properly test a systematic (or partially systematic) strategy's
historical performance before deploying real capital.

## Quiz
1. What distinguishes rule-based systems from discretionary trading?
   a) Nothing, they're identical  b) Rule-based uses precisely defined, codeable criteria; discretionary relies on subjective judgment  c) Only discretionary trading has rules  d) Rule-based trading has no risk management

2. Is systematic trading always superior to discretionary trading?
   a) Yes, always  b) No — both traditions have documented long-term success  c) Discretionary is always better  d) Neither works

3. What should be systematized regardless of whether entry logic is discretionary?
   a) Nothing  b) Risk management and position sizing  c) Only the exit  d) Only the stop-loss

4. What is a hybrid trading approach?
   a) Only systematic  b) Combining systematic execution with retained discretionary judgment  c) Only discretionary  d) Illegal under SEBI rules

5. True or False: A structured discretionary playbook means having no defined process at all.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Rule-based uses precisely defined, codeable criteria; discretionary relies on subjective judgment.
2. **b)** — No, both traditions have documented long-term success.
3. **b)** — Risk management and position sizing.
4. **b)** — Combining systematic execution with retained discretionary judgment.
5. **b) False** — A structured discretionary playbook (`[13.10]`) still follows a defined process, just with retained subjective judgment.
