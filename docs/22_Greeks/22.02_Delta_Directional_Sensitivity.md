## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Delta** measures how much an option's premium is expected to change
for a ₹1 (or $1) move in the underlying, holding all else constant.
Call Deltas range from 0 to +1; put Deltas range from −1 to 0. A deep
ITM call approaches Delta = 1 (moves nearly point-for-point with the
underlying); a deep OTM call approaches Delta = 0 (barely moves at all);
an ATM option's Delta sits near 0.5 (call) or −0.5 (put). Delta is
commonly also read as an approximate probability the option finishes ITM
at expiry — a 0.30 Delta call is loosely interpreted as roughly a 30%
chance of expiring ITM, though this is an approximation, not an exact
probability.

### Intuition
Delta answers the most immediate question a position holder has: "if
the underlying moves right now, how much does my option's value move?"
An ITM option already resembles owning the underlying (high Delta,
moves closely with it); a far OTM option barely reacts to small moves
because it's still mostly betting on a low-probability outcome
(`[21.12]`'s theta decay trap logic connects here directly).

### Mental Model
```
DELTA RANGES:
  Deep ITM Call   -> Delta approaches +1.0
  ATM Call        -> Delta approximately +0.5
  Deep OTM Call   -> Delta approaches 0.0

  Deep ITM Put    -> Delta approaches -1.0
  ATM Put         -> Delta approximately -0.5
  Deep OTM Put    -> Delta approaches 0.0

ESTIMATED PRICE CHANGE:
  Expected Premium Change ~= Delta x (Change in Underlying)

  Example: Call Delta = 0.40, Underlying rises by 50 points
    Expected Premium Change ~= 0.40 x 50 = 20 points
```

### Visual Explanation
Plotting Delta against the underlying's price produces an S-shaped
curve for a call: near 0 far below the strike, rising steeply through
roughly 0.5 near the strike, and flattening near 1 far above the strike
— a put's Delta traces the mirror-image S-curve between 0 and −1.

## Real World Analogy
Delta is like the gearing on a bicycle: a high gear (high Delta) means
each pedal stroke (each point the underlying moves) translates almost
directly into distance covered (premium change); a low gear (low Delta)
means many pedal strokes produce comparatively little distance — useful
for climbing slowly (small, controlled exposure) but requiring far more
underlying movement to go anywhere meaningful.

## Indian Market Example
A trader compares two Nifty calls: a 24,000 strike with Delta 0.55 and a
24,600 strike with Delta 0.15, Nifty at 24,050 (illustrative figures).
If Nifty rises 100 points, the 24,000 call is expected to gain roughly
0.55 × 100 = 55 points, while the 24,600 call is expected to gain only
roughly 0.15 × 100 = 15 points — the same underlying move producing very
different premium responses depending on Delta. **Hypothetical Example**
for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader holds a 200-strike AAPL call with Delta 0.60 and wants to
hedge directional exposure; since one contract typically represents 100
shares, Delta 0.60 means the position behaves like owning roughly 60
shares of AAPL — selling 60 shares would approximately Delta-hedge (make
Delta-neutral) the position against small underlying moves.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
Delta-hedging as a formal technique became widely practiced by
institutional options desks following the popularization of the
Black-Scholes model (`[22.01]`) in the 1970s, giving market makers a
systematic way to remain approximately neutral to small underlying price
moves while still collecting the bid-ask spread and time value from
their market-making activity.

## Institutional Perspective
Market makers continuously Delta-hedge their large option books by
trading the underlying in the opposite direction, aiming to keep
aggregate portfolio Delta near zero — profiting instead from the
bid-ask spread and other Greeks (particularly Theta and Vega) rather
than from directional bets on the underlying itself.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders often use Delta as a rough proxy for "how aggressive" an
option position is — a high-Delta ITM option behaves similarly to
owning the stock (lower relative leverage on the premium paid), while a
low-Delta OTM option is a smaller, more leveraged, lower-probability bet
(`[21.12]`'s theta decay trap connects directly here).

## Professional Insights
Professional traders watch not just current Delta but how Delta itself
is expected to change as the underlying moves — the subject of the next
lesson, Gamma — since a position's Delta is not static and can shift
meaningfully, especially near the strike close to expiry.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating Delta as an exact probability rather than an approximation.
- Ignoring that Delta changes as the underlying moves — a hedge sized
  correctly today may be miscalibrated after a significant move.
- Comparing options across different underlyings by premium alone
  without accounting for how differently their Deltas will respond to a
  move.

## Misconceptions
- **"Delta is a precise, guaranteed probability of expiring ITM."** It
  is a commonly used approximation derived from the pricing model, not
  an exact, guaranteed probability.
- **"A 0.50 Delta option is 'safer' than a 0.20 Delta option."** Delta
  describes directional sensitivity, not overall risk — an ATM option
  can still lose its full premium.
- **"Delta stays constant as the underlying moves."** It changes
  continuously — a fact Gamma (`[22.03]`) specifically quantifies.

## Key Takeaways
- Delta measures expected premium change per 1-point move in the
  underlying — calls range 0 to +1, puts range −1 to 0.
- ATM options have Delta near ±0.5; deep ITM approaches ±1; deep OTM
  approaches 0.
- Delta is commonly used as an approximate probability of expiring ITM.
- Delta-hedging offsets directional exposure by trading the underlying
  in the opposite Delta-equivalent amount.

## Summary
Delta measures how much an option's premium is expected to move for
each point the underlying moves, ranging from 0 to ±1 depending on
moneyness (`[21.03]`), and doubles as a rough approximation of the
probability of expiring ITM. It is the most fundamental of the Greeks
and the starting point for Delta-hedging, a core institutional risk
management practice. The next lesson turns to Gamma — the rate at which
Delta itself changes — completing the picture of how directional
exposure evolves as the underlying moves.

## Quiz
1. What is the Delta range for a call option?
   a) −1 to 0  b) 0 to +1  c) −1 to +1  d) 0 to 100

2. An ATM option's Delta is approximately:
   a) 0  b) ±0.5  c) ±1  d) Undefined

3. Delta is commonly used as an approximation of:
   a) Time decay  b) The probability of expiring ITM  c) Interest rate risk  d) Dividend yield

4. What does "Delta-hedging" involve?
   a) Buying more options  b) Trading the underlying to offset directional exposure  c) Ignoring the Greeks entirely  d) Closing the position

5. True or False: Delta remains constant regardless of how the underlying moves.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — 0 to +1 for calls.
2. **b)** — Approximately ±0.5 for an ATM option.
3. **b)** — The approximate probability of expiring ITM.
4. **b)** — Trading the underlying in the opposite Delta-equivalent amount.
5. **b) False** — Delta changes continuously as the underlying moves (Gamma quantifies this).
