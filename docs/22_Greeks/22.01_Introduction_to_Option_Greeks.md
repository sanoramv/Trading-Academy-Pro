## Core Concepts

### Definition
The **Greeks** are a set of sensitivity measures — each one a partial
derivative of an option's price with respect to one specific input —
that describe how an option's premium is expected to change given a
small change in exactly one variable, holding all others constant. The
five primary Greeks are: **Delta** (sensitivity to the underlying's
price), **Gamma** (sensitivity of Delta itself to the underlying's
price), **Theta** (sensitivity to time passing), **Vega** (sensitivity
to implied volatility), and **Rho** (sensitivity to interest rates).
Each is generated by an option pricing model (most commonly
Black-Scholes, `[21.04]` previewed) from the option's strike, underlying
price, time to expiry, volatility, and interest rate inputs.

### Intuition
Module 21's payoff diagrams answer "what is this position worth at
expiry?" — a single, fixed-in-time snapshot. But a trader holding a
position today needs a different question answered: "how much will this
position's value change *right now* if the underlying moves ₹50, or if
a day passes, or if volatility shifts?" The Greeks answer exactly that —
they convert the static expiry picture into a live, moment-to-moment
sensitivity dashboard.

### Mental Model
```
GREEK       ISOLATES SENSITIVITY TO...           UNITS (approx.)
-----       ----------------------------------   ---------------------
Delta       Underlying price change              Change in premium per
                                                  1-point underlying move
Gamma       Delta's own rate of change            Change in Delta per
                                                  1-point underlying move
Theta       Time passing (one day)                Change in premium per
                                                  day, all else equal
Vega        Implied volatility change             Change in premium per
                                                  1% IV change
Rho         Interest rate change                  Change in premium per
                                                  1% rate change (usually
                                                  smallest, least-watched)
```

### Visual Explanation
Picture an option's premium as sitting at the center of a dashboard with
five separate dials, each dial controlled by one input variable and
calibrated by one Greek — moving any single dial while holding the
others fixed shows exactly how much the premium shifts, in isolation
from everything else that might also be changing simultaneously in a
real market.

## Real World Analogy
The Greeks are like a car's dashboard gauges — speedometer (Delta),
rate of acceleration (Gamma), fuel burn rate (Theta), and a sensitivity-
to-road-conditions gauge (Vega) — each one isolates one specific aspect
of the car's current state, letting a driver understand exactly what's
driving performance right now, rather than only knowing the destination
(the expiry payoff) without any sense of the moment-to-moment journey.

## Indian Market Example
An NSE Nifty option chain (`[21.11]`) commonly displays Delta, Gamma,
Theta, and Vega alongside OI and IV for every strike — a trader
comparing two Nifty calls at different strikes can use these columns
directly to see which option is more sensitive to a quick Nifty move
(higher Delta/Gamma) versus which is decaying faster day to day (more
negative Theta), without needing to compute the Black-Scholes formula
by hand.

## US Market Example
US brokers (e.g., displaying CBOE-listed option data) similarly present
the Greeks per contract; a trader evaluating an SPY option ahead of a
Federal Reserve rate decision (`[06.02]`) might specifically check Rho
alongside Vega, since a rate-sensitive event affects both inputs
simultaneously, unlike most ordinary trading days where Rho's effect is
negligible.

## Historical Example
The Greeks derive from the Black-Scholes-Merton option pricing model,
published in 1973, which for the first time gave traders a closed-form
way to compute these sensitivities rather than relying purely on
intuition or historical pattern-matching — a genuine turning point in
how options were priced and risk-managed industry-wide, later extended
by numerous refinements the Black-Scholes model's own limitations
prompted.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional trading desks and market makers manage entire portfolios
of options by their *aggregate* Greeks (summed across every position)
rather than analyzing each contract individually — a book that is
"Delta-neutral" or "Gamma-neutral" has had its aggregate exposure to
that variable specifically hedged away, a practice this module builds
toward in `[22.07]`.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders frequently ignore the Greeks entirely, focusing only on
premium and strike — but two options with the same premium can carry
very different risk profiles once Delta, Gamma, Theta, and Vega are
compared, information the theta decay trap discussion (`[21.12]`)
already showed matters concretely.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders treat the Greeks as *estimates from a model*, not
guaranteed outcomes — the underlying pricing model's assumptions
(constant volatility, no jumps, continuous trading) don't perfectly
hold in real markets, so Greeks values shift as market conditions and
the model's own inputs change, sometimes abruptly around news events.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating a Greek's current value as fixed rather than something that
  itself changes constantly as the underlying, time, and volatility move.
- Focusing on premium and strike alone while ignoring what the Greeks
  reveal about a position's actual risk exposure.
- Assuming Rho matters as much as Delta/Theta/Vega for typical short-
  dated equity/index options, when its effect is usually comparatively small.

## Misconceptions
- **"Greeks predict exactly what will happen."** They are instantaneous
  sensitivity estimates from a model, valid for small changes, holding
  other variables constant — not precise forecasts.
- **"All five Greeks matter equally for every trade."** Their relative
  importance shifts with context — Theta dominates near expiry, Vega
  dominates around volatility-sensitive events, Rho rarely dominates for
  short-dated equity/index options.
- **"Only professional traders need to understand the Greeks."** Any
  option buyer or seller benefits from knowing which variable is driving
  their position's day-to-day P&L.

## Key Takeaways
- The five primary Greeks: Delta (price), Gamma (Delta's own change),
  Theta (time), Vega (volatility), Rho (interest rates).
- Each Greek isolates sensitivity to exactly one input, holding others
  constant — derived from an option pricing model, most commonly
  Black-Scholes.
- Greeks convert Module 21's static, expiry-only payoff view into a
  live, moment-to-moment risk dashboard.
- Institutional desks manage aggregate Greeks across entire portfolios,
  not just single contracts.

## Summary
The Greeks — Delta, Gamma, Theta, Vega, and Rho — each isolate an
option's sensitivity to exactly one input variable, transforming Module
21's static expiry-day payoff diagrams into a live risk dashboard usable
at any point before expiry. Having introduced what each Greek measures
in broad terms, the next four lessons take each of the primary Greeks in
turn, starting with Delta — the most fundamental and widely used of the
five.

## Quiz
1. Which Greek measures sensitivity to implied volatility?
   a) Delta  b) Theta  c) Vega  d) Rho

2. What does Gamma measure?
   a) Time decay  b) The rate of change of Delta itself  c) Interest rate sensitivity  d) Strike price

3. Which pricing model originated the modern Greeks framework?
   a) CAPM  b) Black-Scholes (1973)  c) Dividend Discount Model  d) DuPont Analysis

4. Why do institutional desks track aggregate Greeks across a portfolio?
   a) It's required by SEBI  b) To understand and hedge total exposure to each variable, not just per contract  c) It has no practical use  d) Only for tax reporting

5. True or False: Greeks are guaranteed predictions of future option price changes.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **c)** — Vega measures sensitivity to implied volatility.
2. **b)** — Gamma measures the rate of change of Delta itself.
3. **b)** — Black-Scholes (1973) originated the modern Greeks framework.
4. **b)** — To understand and hedge total exposure to each variable across an entire portfolio.
5. **b) False** — They are instantaneous estimates from a model, not guaranteed outcomes.
