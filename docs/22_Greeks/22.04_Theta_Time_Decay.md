## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Theta** measures how much an option's premium is expected to decline
over one day's passage, holding the underlying price and volatility
constant. It is typically expressed as a negative number for long
option positions (premium erodes with time) and a positive number for
short option positions (the seller benefits from that same erosion).
Theta is the precise, per-day quantification of the time value erosion
`[21.04]` first introduced conceptually and `[21.12]` identified as the
mechanism behind the theta decay trap.

### Intuition
Every option loses a little of its time value each day simply because
one fewer day remains for a favorable move to occur — Theta puts an
exact number on how much. Crucially, this erosion is not linear: it
accelerates as expiry approaches, especially for ATM options, meaning
the same option can lose far more value in its final week than it did
in an equivalent earlier week.

### Mental Model
```
THETA SIGN CONVENTION:
  Long Call / Long Put   -> Theta is NEGATIVE (position loses value daily)
  Short Call / Short Put -> Theta is POSITIVE (position gains value daily)

ESTIMATED DAILY PREMIUM CHANGE (all else equal):
  New Premium ~= Old Premium + Theta  (one day later)

  Example: Long call, Theta = -3.50
    Tomorrow's expected premium ~= Today's premium - 3.50
    (assuming underlying price and volatility unchanged)

ACCELERATION NEAR EXPIRY (ATM options):
  Theta magnitude generally GROWS larger (more negative for buyers)
  as expiry approaches -- the erosion curve from [21.04] made precise.
```

### Visual Explanation
Plotting an ATM option's remaining time value against days-to-expiry
retraces `[21.04]`'s erosion curve — Theta is the slope of that curve at
any given day, and the slope itself steepens (grows more negative) as
the curve approaches zero at expiry, visually confirming why the final
days matter disproportionately.

## Real World Analogy
Theta is like a melting ice block sold by weight: early on, it melts
slowly (small daily weight loss, shallow Theta), but as the block gets
thinner near the end, it melts progressively faster per day (steep
Theta) — the seller of "ice futures" (the option seller collecting
Theta) benefits from that same accelerating melt, while the buyer
holding the block loses value faster the longer they wait.

## Indian Market Example
A trader holds a Nifty weekly ATM call with Theta of −25 per day
(illustrative figure) with three days remaining to expiry. All else
equal, the position is expected to lose roughly ₹25 in premium value
each day purely from time passing — and because it's a weekly ATM
option, this Theta magnitude is typically far larger, proportionally,
than an equivalent monthly option's daily decay early in its life.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader has sold an ATM SPY put with Theta of +0.15 per day
(illustrative figure), meaning the position is expected to gain roughly
$15 per contract (100 shares) per day purely from time passing, all
else equal — the structural benefit institutional Theta-selling desks
(`[21.06]`, `[21.10]`) systematically harvest across large books.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
Weekly Nifty and Bank Nifty options, introduced and expanded on the NSE
in the years following their initial launch, dramatically shortened the
typical holding period for many Indian retail options traders — a shift
that, per SEBI's published F&O retail loss studies (`[21.12]`), has been
associated with concentrated buyer losses, consistent with the
accelerated Theta decay this lesson quantifies applying over ever
shorter time windows.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional Theta-selling strategies (`[24.09]` previewed) are
explicitly built around harvesting positive Theta systematically across
diversified strikes and expiries, while simultaneously managing the
Gamma risk (`[22.03]`) that comes paired with it — Theta and Gamma are
often described as sitting on opposite sides of the same risk trade-off
for a short option position.

## Retail Perspective
Retail option buyers should treat a position's Theta as a known,
quantifiable daily cost to weigh against the probability and expected
magnitude of a favorable move — the same discipline `[21.12]`'s
pre-trade checklist recommends, now expressed as a specific number
rather than a general caution.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders distinguish "Theta P&L" (value lost purely to time,
all else equal) from the position's *total* daily P&L, which also
reflects Delta/Gamma (underlying moves) and Vega (volatility shifts)
simultaneously — isolating Theta's specific contribution requires
holding the other Greeks' effects constant, exactly as the definition
specifies.

## Common Mistakes
- Assuming Theta is constant day to day rather than accelerating for ATM
  options near expiry.
- Ignoring Theta entirely when evaluating a long option position's
  expected holding cost.
- Attributing all of a day's premium change to Theta alone, when Delta/
  Gamma and Vega moves are usually also contributing simultaneously.

## Misconceptions
- **"Theta decay happens evenly across an option's entire life."** It
  is non-linear and generally accelerates as expiry approaches,
  especially for ATM strikes.
- **"A negative Theta always means the position is losing money
  overall."** It means the position is losing value *from time alone* —
  a favorable underlying move (positive Delta effect) can more than
  offset it on any given day.
- **"Only option sellers need to think about Theta."** Buyers
  experience it as a real, quantifiable cost that must be weighed
  against thesis timing (`[21.12]`).

## Key Takeaways
- Theta measures expected daily premium change from time alone, holding
  underlying and volatility constant.
- Negative for long positions (cost to the buyer); positive for short
  positions (benefit to the seller).
- Theta accelerates for ATM options as expiry approaches — the precise
  version of `[21.04]`'s erosion curve.
- Theta and Gamma sit on opposite sides of the same trade-off for short
  option positions: harvesting Theta means bearing Gamma risk.

## Summary
Theta gives a precise, per-day number to the time value erosion `[21.04]`
first introduced and `[21.12]` identified as the mechanism behind common
option buyer losses — negative for buyers, positive for sellers, and
accelerating for ATM options as expiry approaches. Having now covered
the underlying-price Greeks (Delta, Gamma) and the time Greek (Theta),
the next lesson turns to Vega — sensitivity to implied volatility, the
final primary Greek most traders actively monitor.

## Quiz
1. What does Theta measure?
   a) Volatility sensitivity  b) Expected daily premium change from time alone  c) Interest rate sensitivity  d) Directional sensitivity

2. What sign does Theta typically have for a long option position?
   a) Positive  b) Negative  c) Always zero  d) Undefined

3. When does Theta decay generally accelerate most for ATM options?
   a) Far from expiry  b) As expiry approaches  c) It never accelerates  d) Only for ITM options

4. Why might an option seller favor Theta?
   a) It has no effect on sellers  b) Positive Theta means the short position gains value daily from time alone  c) Sellers are unaffected by time  d) Theta only affects buyers

5. True or False: Theta decay is linear and constant throughout an option's life.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Expected daily premium change from time passing alone.
2. **b)** — Negative, since the long position loses value daily.
3. **b)** — As expiry approaches, especially for ATM options.
4. **b)** — Positive Theta means the short position gains value daily from time alone.
5. **b) False** — Decay is non-linear and generally accelerates near expiry.
