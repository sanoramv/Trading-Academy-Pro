## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Position Greeks management** means evaluating a trade's Delta, Gamma,
Theta, and Vega together — not as isolated numbers — to understand its
combined exposure to direction, acceleration, time, and volatility
simultaneously. For a multi-leg position, the Greeks are simply summed
across all legs (accounting for long/short sign) to produce the
position's *net* Greeks. **Neutrality** describes a position engineered
so its net exposure to one specific Greek is approximately zero —
Delta-neutral (no net directional exposure), Gamma-neutral (Delta
itself won't shift much), or Vega-neutral (no net IV exposure) — used to
isolate exposure to the remaining Greeks deliberately.

### Intuition
Every multi-leg strategy from Module 21 is, underneath its payoff
diagram, a specific combination of Greeks. An iron condor (`[21.10]`)
isn't just "a range-bound bet" — it's a position with near-zero net
Delta (symmetric strikes), negative net Gamma (short options dominate),
positive net Theta (collecting time decay), and negative net Vega
(hurt by rising IV). Reading the Greeks together reveals *why* the
payoff diagram looks the way it does, and what specifically will hurt
or help the position before expiry.

### Mental Model
```
NET POSITION GREEKS = Sum of each leg's Greek (respecting long/short sign)

Example: IRON CONDOR (short put spread + short call spread, [21.10])
  Net Delta  ~= near ZERO   (symmetric short strikes largely offset)
  Net Gamma  ~= NEGATIVE    (net short options -> Delta can shift against you)
  Net Theta  ~= POSITIVE    (net short options -> collects time decay)
  Net Vega   ~= NEGATIVE    (net short options -> hurt by rising IV)

NEUTRALITY (deliberately targeting a net-zero Greek):
  Delta-neutral  -> hedge directional exposure (trade underlying/options
                     to offset net Delta near zero)
  Gamma-neutral  -> add options to reduce how fast Delta itself can shift
  Vega-neutral   -> balance long/short Vega legs to reduce IV sensitivity
```

### Visual Explanation
Picture a position's four main Greeks as a small dashboard readout
updated continuously: Delta near zero but Gamma sharply negative flags
"currently balanced, but a big move could break that balance fast" —
exactly the situation a trader holding an ATM iron butterfly
(`[21.10]`) close to expiry faces, and precisely why professional desks
re-check Greeks intraday, not just at entry.

### ASCII Diagram
```

  Delta:  ~0     <- currently balanced, no strong directional lean
  Gamma:  ---    <- (negative) balance can break QUICKLY if underlying moves
  Theta:  +++    <- (positive) earning time decay every day held
  Vega:   ---    <- (negative) hurt if IV rises unexpectedly

  READ TOGETHER: "This position profits from TIME passing quietly,
  but is vulnerable to a SUDDEN move (Gamma) or a VOLATILITY spike (Vega)
  -- exactly matching the iron butterfly payoff diagram's shape, [21.10]"
```

### Flowchart
```
[Open or review a position -- single-leg or multi-leg]
        |
        v
[Sum each leg's Delta, Gamma, Theta, Vega with correct long/short sign]
        |
        v
[Net Delta far from zero?] --YES--> [Position has a clear directional lean;
                                       consider whether that's intentional]
        | NO (near zero)
        v

   position remains vulnerable to a SUDDEN move or IV shift -- monitor closely]
        | NO

   time decay in quiet conditions -- matches short-premium strategies, [21.10]]
        |
        v

```

## Real World Analogy
Managing a position by its Greeks is like a ship's captain reading
several instruments together rather than just the compass: heading
(Delta) alone doesn't reveal that the ship is about to hit rough seas
(high Gamma/Vega) even while pointed in a stable direction — a
complete picture requires reading every gauge together, continuously,
not just once at departure.

## Indian Market Example
A trader holds a Nifty iron condor (`[21.10]`) with net Delta near zero,
net Theta strongly positive, and net Vega meaningfully negative
(illustrative scenario). Ahead of an RBI policy announcement (`[06.02]`,
`[03.10]`), the trader recognizes the position's negative Vega makes it
vulnerable to the IV spike such announcements often produce — prompting
a decision to close or adjust the position before the announcement
rather than holding through it unmanaged.

## US Market Example
A trader holds a long SPY call (positive Delta, positive Gamma, negative
Theta, positive Vega) and wants to reduce directional risk while keeping
some upside exposure. Selling a further-OTM call against it (converting
to a bull call spread, `[21.08]`) reduces net Delta and flips net Theta
from a cost toward less of a drag, while also capping the position's
Vega exposure — a practical example of using the Greeks to guide a
structural adjustment.

## Historical Example
Institutional options desks' practice of continuously re-hedging Delta
and monitoring Gamma exposure intraday became standard risk-management
procedure industry-wide following the widespread adoption of the
Black-Scholes framework (`[22.01]`) from the 1970s onward — a discipline
that later proved directly relevant during sharp, fast-moving episodes
like the 2010 Flash Crash and the March 2020 COVID crash, when
positions' Gamma and Vega exposures shifted extremely quickly.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional risk systems display aggregate Greeks across an entire
portfolio in real time, flagging when net Delta, Gamma, or Vega drifts
beyond preset risk limits — position-level Greeks management (this
lesson's focus) is the same discipline applied at portfolio scale.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders rarely need real-time institutional-grade Greeks
monitoring, but should still check a position's net Greeks before
entering a multi-leg trade and periodically while holding it — most
brokers displaying Greeks per leg on the option chain (`[21.11]`) make
this straightforward without requiring manual calculation.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders often describe a position's Greeks profile as its
"true" strategy, more so than its name — two positions both labeled
"iron condor" can carry meaningfully different net Greeks depending on
strike selection and time to expiry, so experienced traders check the
actual Greeks rather than relying on the strategy label alone.

## Common Mistakes
- Evaluating only one Greek (commonly just Delta) and missing meaningful
  Gamma or Vega exposure the position also carries.
- Assuming a position's Greeks at entry remain valid throughout the
  holding period without periodic re-checking.
- Treating "neutral" as "risk-free" — a Delta-neutral position can still
  carry substantial Gamma or Vega risk.

## Misconceptions
- **"A Delta-neutral position has no risk."** It has no net *directional*
  risk specifically — Gamma and Vega exposure can remain substantial.
- **"All iron condors behave identically."** Strike selection and time
  to expiry materially change the net Greeks, even for the same named
  strategy.
- **"Checking Greeks once at entry is sufficient."** They shift
  continuously as underlying price, time, and IV all change — periodic
  re-checking is part of active position management.

## Key Takeaways
- Net position Greeks = sum of each leg's Greek, respecting long/short sign.
- Reading Delta, Gamma, Theta, and Vega together reveals a position's
  full risk picture — no single Greek tells the whole story.
- Neutrality (Delta-, Gamma-, or Vega-neutral) targets a specific
  exposure toward zero to isolate remaining risks deliberately.
- A strategy's name (e.g., "iron condor") doesn't fully specify its
  risk — the actual net Greeks, driven by strikes and time to expiry, do.

## Summary
Managing a position by its Greeks means reading Delta, Gamma, Theta, and
Vega together as a combined risk picture, summing each leg's
contribution for multi-leg strategies, and periodically re-checking
since all four shift continuously as market conditions change — the
practical synthesis of every Greek covered individually in this module
so far. The next and final lesson in this module works through a full
case study, adjusting a live position's Greeks profile step by step as
market conditions evolve.

## Quiz
1. How do you calculate a multi-leg position's net Delta?
   a) Use only the largest leg's Delta  b) Sum each leg's Delta, respecting long/short sign  c) Average the strikes  d) Ignore short legs

2. Does a Delta-neutral position have zero total risk?
   a) Yes, always  b) No — Gamma and Vega exposure can remain  c) Only for calls  d) Only near expiry

3. Why might two positions both called "iron condor" carry different risk?
   a) They can't differ  b) Strike selection and time to expiry change the net Greeks  c) Only the premium matters  d) Naming guarantees identical risk

4. Why should a trader re-check a position's Greeks periodically?
   a) They never change  b) They shift continuously as underlying, time, and IV change  c) Only needed once a year  d) Only relevant at expiry

5. True or False: Reading only Delta is sufficient to fully understand a position's risk.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Sum each leg's Delta, respecting long/short sign.
2. **b)** — No, Gamma and Vega exposure can remain substantial even when Delta-neutral.
3. **b)** — Strike selection and time to expiry change the net Greeks even under the same strategy name.
4. **b)** — Greeks shift continuously as underlying, time, and IV all change.
5. **b) False** — Delta alone misses Gamma, Theta, and Vega exposure.
