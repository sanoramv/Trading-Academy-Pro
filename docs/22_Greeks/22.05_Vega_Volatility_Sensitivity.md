## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Vega** measures how much an option's premium is expected to change
for a 1-percentage-point change in implied volatility (IV), holding the
underlying price and time constant. Vega is positive for both long
calls and long puts — rising IV increases premium for option buyers
regardless of direction, and falling IV decreases it. Vega is highest
for ATM options and for options with more time remaining to expiry;
it shrinks toward zero for deep ITM/OTM options and for options very
close to expiry, where there's little time left for volatility to
matter.

### Intuition
Implied volatility reflects the market's expectation of how much the
underlying will move before expiry — higher expected movement makes
every strike more likely to be reached, so option premiums rise across
the board when IV rises, independent of which direction the underlying
actually moves. Vega quantifies exactly how much of an option's premium
depends on this expectation rather than on the underlying's price itself.

### Mental Model
```
VEGA BEHAVIOR:
  Long Call / Long Put   -> Vega is POSITIVE (rising IV helps, falling IV hurts)
  Short Call / Short Put -> Vega is NEGATIVE (falling IV helps, rising IV hurts)

  Highest Vega: ATM options, longer time to expiry
  Lowest Vega:  Deep ITM/OTM options, very close to expiry

ESTIMATED PREMIUM CHANGE (all else equal):
  Expected Premium Change ~= Vega x (Change in IV, in percentage points)

  Example: Long call, Vega = 12, IV drops from 18% to 14% (a 4-point drop)
    Expected Premium Change ~= 12 x (-4) = -48  (a loss purely from IV falling)
```

### Visual Explanation
Vega's shape across strikes mirrors Gamma's (`[22.03]`): a hump peaking
at ATM and shrinking toward both wings — but unlike Gamma, which shrinks
as expiry nears, Vega generally shrinks with *less* time remaining
regardless of strike, since less time means less opportunity for
volatility to matter before expiry.

## Real World Analogy
Vega is like the price of flood insurance responding to a weather
forecast: even before any rain actually falls, insurance premiums rise
the moment forecasters raise the probability of a storm, and fall again
once the forecast calms down — the premium moves with the *expectation*
of movement, not the movement itself, exactly as an option's premium
moves with IV independent of the underlying's actual price.

## Indian Market Example
Ahead of a Union Budget announcement (`[03.10]`), Nifty option IV
typically rises across strikes as the market prices in uncertainty; a
trader holding a long ATM call with Vega 15 (illustrative figure) sees
premium rise purely from this IV increase even before the Budget is
delivered — and often sees a sharp premium drop immediately after the
announcement (IV crush) even if Nifty's actual price barely moves,
precisely because the uncertainty that inflated IV has now resolved.

## US Market Example
Ahead of a company's quarterly earnings release, IV on its options
typically rises in the days before the announcement; a trader who buys
a straddle (`[21.09]`) specifically to bet on the earnings-driven move
must ensure the actual price move is large enough to overcome not just
Theta decay but also the Vega-driven IV crush expected immediately after
results are announced — the same dynamic `[21.12]`'s US market example
described from the buyer-mistake angle.

## Historical Example
India VIX (Module 23, previewed) spiked to extreme levels during the
March 2020 COVID crash, reflecting sharply elevated implied volatility
across Nifty options — option premiums during that period were inflated
substantially by Vega effects alone, independent of the underlying
moves themselves, illustrating how large a role implied volatility can
play in premium during genuine market stress.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional volatility desks trade Vega as directly and deliberately
as directional desks trade Delta — taking explicit "long volatility" or
"short volatility" positions based on a view of whether current IV is
too high or too low relative to their own forecast of future realized
movement, a practice Module 23 develops in depth.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders buying options ahead of known events should specifically
check current IV levels (via the option chain, `[21.11]`) before
entering, since a position with high positive Vega entered when IV is
already elevated is exposed to a real, quantifiable IV crush risk even
if the directional thesis proves correct.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders distinguish implied volatility (the market's
current pricing input, what Vega measures sensitivity to) from realized
volatility (the underlying's actual historical movement) — a persistent
gap between the two, generally with implied running higher on average,
underlies much of institutional Theta-selling activity (`[22.04]`),
developed fully in Module 23.

## Common Mistakes
- Buying options right before a known event without checking whether IV
  is already elevated, then being surprised by IV crush after the event.
- Attributing a premium change entirely to the underlying's move without
  recognizing Vega's separate contribution.
- Ignoring that Vega shrinks as expiry approaches — a position's IV
  sensitivity is not constant across its life.

## Misconceptions
- **"Vega only matters for long-dated options."** It matters most for
  longer-dated ATM options, but any option retains some Vega exposure
  until very close to expiry.
- **"Rising IV always means the underlying is about to move."** Rising
  IV reflects the market's *expectation* of movement, not a guarantee —
  the anticipated event can still resolve with minimal actual price change.
- **"IV crush only affects option buyers."** It benefits option sellers
  (negative Vega) symmetrically — the same mechanism, opposite side.

## Key Takeaways
- Vega measures expected premium change per 1-percentage-point change in
  implied volatility, holding underlying price and time constant.
- Positive for long options (rising IV helps); negative for short
  options (falling IV helps).
- Highest for ATM, longer-dated options; shrinks toward zero for deep
  ITM/OTM and options close to expiry.
- IV crush — a sharp post-event IV drop — is a direct, quantifiable Vega
  effect, distinct from Theta or Delta.

## Summary
Vega measures an option's sensitivity to implied volatility itself,
independent of the underlying's actual price movement — highest for
ATM, longer-dated options, and the precise mechanism behind the IV
crush risk `[21.12]` first flagged for option buyers around known
events. With Delta, Gamma, Theta, and Vega now covered, the next lesson
completes the primary Greeks with Rho (interest rate sensitivity) and
introduces the second-order Greeks briefly, before Module 22 turns to
managing all of these together in a live position.

## Quiz
1. What does Vega measure?
   a) Time decay  b) Sensitivity to implied volatility  c) Directional sensitivity  d) Interest rate sensitivity

2. Is Vega positive or negative for a long call?
   a) Positive  b) Negative  c) Always zero  d) Undefined

3. Where is Vega generally highest?
   a) Deep ITM, near expiry  b) ATM, longer time to expiry  c) Deep OTM, near expiry  d) It's constant

4. What causes "IV crush"?
   a) Rising interest rates  b) A sharp drop in implied volatility after an event resolves  c) Time decay alone  d) A stock split

5. True or False: Vega exposure stays constant regardless of time to expiry.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Sensitivity to implied volatility.
2. **a)** — Positive; rising IV increases a long call's premium.
3. **b)** — ATM options with more time remaining to expiry.
4. **b)** — A sharp drop in implied volatility after an uncertain event resolves.
5. **b) False** — Vega shrinks as expiry approaches, regardless of strike.
