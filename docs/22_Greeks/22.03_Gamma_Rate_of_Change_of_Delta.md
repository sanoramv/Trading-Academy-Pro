# [22.03] Gamma — Rate of Change of Delta

> Module: Greeks · Lesson 3 of 8 · Estimated time: 30 minutes

## Learning Objectives
- Define Gamma precisely as the second derivative of premium with respect to the underlying.
- Explain why Gamma peaks for ATM options and shrinks for deep ITM/OTM options.
- Understand why Gamma matters most for short option sellers and near expiry.
- Connect Gamma to the practical difficulty of maintaining a Delta-neutral hedge.

- `[22.02]` Delta — Directional Sensitivity
- `[22.01]` Introduction to Option Greeks

## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Gamma** measures how much an option's Delta (`[22.02]`) is expected to
change for a 1-point move in the underlying. Where Delta is the "speed"
of an option's price response to the underlying, Gamma is the
"acceleration" — it tells a trader how quickly Delta itself will shift
as the underlying moves, which in turn determines how quickly a
Delta-based hedge becomes outdated. Gamma is highest for ATM options and
approaches zero for both deep ITM and deep OTM options; it also
generally increases as expiry approaches for ATM options specifically.

### Intuition
An option's Delta isn't fixed — it moves as the underlying moves, and
Gamma quantifies exactly how fast. An ATM option sits at the point where
a small move can meaningfully shift the probability of finishing ITM
(Delta swings quickly), while a deep ITM or deep OTM option's Delta is
already near its extreme (1, −1, or 0) and has little room left to move
— explaining why Gamma is concentrated near the strike.

### Mental Model
```
GAMMA BEHAVIOR:
  Deep ITM/OTM options  -> Gamma near ZERO (Delta already near its extreme)
  ATM options            -> Gamma at its HIGHEST (Delta most sensitive to
                             a change in the underlying)

  As expiry approaches, ATM Gamma generally INCREASES further
  (Delta can swing from near 0 to near 1 very quickly in the final days)

ESTIMATED DELTA CHANGE:
  Expected Delta Change ~= Gamma x (Change in Underlying)

  Example: Gamma = 0.03, Underlying rises 20 points
    Expected NEW Delta ~= Old Delta + (0.03 x 20) = Old Delta + 0.60
```

### Visual Explanation
Plotting Gamma against the underlying's price produces a single hump
centered near the strike — low on both wings (deep ITM and deep OTM),
peaking sharply at ATM — the mirror image of Delta's flat-to-steep-to-
flat S-curve from `[22.02]`, since Gamma is Delta's own rate of change.

### Flowchart
```
[Position is near expiry AND near the strike (ATM)?]
        |--YES--> [HIGH GAMMA ZONE: Delta can swing rapidly;
                    a Delta-hedge built even hours ago may already
                    be stale -- re-hedge frequently]
        | NO (far ITM/OTM, or far from expiry)
        v
[LOW GAMMA ZONE: Delta changes slowly; existing Delta-hedge
 remains reasonably accurate for longer]
        |
        v
[Short option sellers: HIGH GAMMA = HIGH RISK
 (a small move can rapidly increase directional exposure --

```

## Real World Analogy
If Delta is a car's speedometer, Gamma is how quickly that speed can
change — a car with a powerful engine (high Gamma) can go from slow to
fast in a moment, requiring the driver to react quickly and often; a car
with a weak engine (low Gamma) changes speed sluggishly, giving the
driver more time to adjust. ATM options are the powerful-engine case:
their "speed" (Delta) can change abruptly.

## Indian Market Example
A trader has sold a Nifty 24,000 ATM call with Nifty currently at 24,000
and high Gamma (illustrative scenario), one day before expiry. A sudden
150-point Nifty rally can push the call's Delta from roughly 0.50 toward
0.85–0.90 within the same session, sharply increasing the seller's
directional exposure almost as fast as the underlying itself moved —
precisely the risk institutional desks manage by re-hedging frequently
near expiry.

## US Market Example
A market maker holds a Delta-neutral book heavily weighted toward
ATM SPY options ahead of a Federal Reserve announcement (`[06.02]`).
Because Gamma is elevated for ATM strikes, even a moderate post-
announcement move can push the book significantly away from
Delta-neutral within minutes, requiring active re-hedging that a
lower-Gamma, further-dated or further-OTM book would not need nearly as
urgently.

## Historical Example
Rapid, cascading market maker re-hedging in response to high Gamma
exposure — sometimes called "Gamma-driven" flow — has been cited by
market analysts as an amplifying factor during some sharp intraday
moves in both US and Indian index options markets, particularly on
weekly expiry days when large ATM open interest concentrates Gamma
exposure market-wide (`[21.11]`'s OI discussion connects directly here),
though isolating Gamma's precise contribution to any single historical
move remains difficult to establish with certainty.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional options desks explicitly monitor "Gamma exposure" (often
called GEX) across their books and, at a market-wide level, analysts
estimate aggregate dealer Gamma positioning to infer whether market
makers are likely to be buying or selling into moves — a topic connecting
directly to volatility regime behavior developed further in Module 23.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders rarely actively manage Gamma directly, but should
understand that selling ATM options close to expiry (a common weekly
options income strategy, `[24.09]` previewed) carries meaningfully
higher Gamma risk than selling further-dated or further-OTM options —
directional exposure can build quickly against a seller in this
specific configuration.

## Professional Insights
Professional Gamma management often involves deliberately choosing to
be "long Gamma" (net option buyer, benefiting from rapid Delta shifts in
either direction) versus "short Gamma" (net option seller, exposed to
rapid Delta shifts working against the position) as a portfolio-level
stance, not just a single-position decision.

## Common Mistakes
- Selling ATM options very close to expiry without recognizing the
  elevated Gamma risk this specific combination carries.
- Assuming a Delta-hedge set earlier in the day remains accurate without
  checking how much Gamma has caused Delta to drift since then.
- Confusing high Gamma with high risk in all contexts — for a long
  option buyer, high Gamma near a favorable move is actually beneficial,
  accelerating gains.

## Misconceptions
- **"Gamma is only relevant to professional market makers."** Any
  trader selling options near expiry, especially ATM, is directly
  exposed to Gamma risk, whether or not they track the number explicitly.
- **"High Gamma is always bad."** It is a risk for option sellers facing
  an adverse move, but a benefit for option buyers facing a favorable
  one — Gamma amplifies Delta's response in both directions.
- **"Gamma stays the same throughout an option's life."** It rises for
  ATM options as expiry approaches and falls again once deep ITM/OTM.

## Key Takeaways
- Gamma measures the rate of change of Delta per 1-point underlying
  move — the "acceleration" to Delta's "speed."
- Gamma peaks for ATM options and shrinks toward zero for deep ITM/OTM
  options; it generally rises further for ATM options as expiry nears.
- High Gamma means a Delta-hedge can become stale quickly, requiring
  frequent re-hedging.
- Short option sellers face elevated risk in high-Gamma conditions;
  long option buyers benefit from it during favorable moves.

## Summary
Gamma completes the picture Delta (`[22.02]`) began — measuring not just
an option's current directional sensitivity but how quickly that
sensitivity itself will change as the underlying moves, peaking for ATM
options and posing the greatest hedging challenge close to expiry. The
next lesson turns to Theta — the Greek most closely tied to `[21.04]`'s
and `[21.12]`'s time value decay discussion, now made precise as a
per-day number.

## Quiz
1. What does Gamma measure?
   a) Time decay  b) The rate of change of Delta itself  c) Interest rate sensitivity  d) Dividend impact

2. Where is Gamma typically highest?
   a) Deep ITM  b) Deep OTM  c) ATM  d) It's constant everywhere

3. What happens to a Delta-hedge when Gamma is high?
   a) It remains accurate indefinitely  b) It can become stale quickly, requiring frequent re-hedging  c) It becomes irrelevant  d) Gamma has no effect on hedging

4. High Gamma is generally riskiest for:
   a) Long option buyers  b) Short option sellers  c) Neither  d) Only bond traders

5. True or False: Gamma is constant throughout an option's life regardless of time to expiry.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — The rate of change of Delta itself.
2. **c)** — ATM options, where Delta is most sensitive to underlying moves.
3. **b)** — It can become stale quickly, requiring frequent re-hedging.
4. **b)** — Short option sellers, who face rapidly building exposure against them.
5. **b) False** — ATM Gamma generally increases as expiry approaches.
