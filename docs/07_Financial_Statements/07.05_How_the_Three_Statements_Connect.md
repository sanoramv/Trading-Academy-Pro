# [07.05] How the Three Statements Connect

- Build a mental "closed loop" model of the three-statement system.
- Practice tracing a single transaction's effect across all three statements.

## Core Concepts

### Definition
The three financial statements are not independent documents — they form
a single, interconnected system where changes in one necessarily affect
the others. The key linkages are: (1) **Net Profit** (bottom of the Income
Statement) flows into **Retained Earnings** (part of Shareholders' Equity
on the Balance Sheet); (2) the **Cash Flow Statement** starts from Net
Profit and reconciles it to the actual change in the **Cash** balance
(a Current Asset on the Balance Sheet); and (3) the **ending cash balance**
on the Cash Flow Statement must exactly match the **cash line item** on the
Balance Sheet.

### Intuition
This interconnection exists because accounting is a closed system —
every transaction a business undertakes must be recorded consistently
across all relevant statements, and the whole point of maintaining all
three together (rather than just one) is that they cross-check and
validate each other. If you understand these links, you can predict how a
single event (a sale, a loan, a purchase) ripples through all three
statements simultaneously — a skill separating genuine financial statement
literacy from simply being able to read each statement in isolation.

### Flowchart
```
[A company makes a Rs 1,000 cash SALE with Rs 600 cost of goods sold]

 (before other expenses/taxes, simplified for illustration)]
        |
        v

 (asset decreases, goods sold), Retained Earnings +400
 (equity increases, from the profit)]
        |

 change/COGS, depending on when the inventory was originally

 Statement's net change in cash? If yes, the three statements are
 CONSISTENT.]
```

## Real World Analogy
Understanding how the three statements connect is like understanding how
a car's speedometer, fuel gauge, and odometer are all connected to the
same underlying engine and driving activity — you wouldn't expect the
odometer to show you drove 100 km while the fuel gauge shows no fuel was
used at all; if the numbers don't reconcile as expected given how the car
actually works, something is wrong (either with your understanding, or
with the car's data itself). Similarly, if a company's income statement,
balance sheet, and cash flow statement don't reconcile the way this
lesson's framework predicts, that inconsistency itself is a signal worth
investigating.

## Indian Market Example
When an Indian company issues new shares to raise capital (a Financing
Cash Flow inflow, `[07.04]`), this transaction simultaneously increases
**Cash** (Balance Sheet asset) and **Share Capital** (Balance Sheet
equity) — a clean, traceable example of a single transaction rippling
across both the Cash Flow Statement and Balance Sheet simultaneously,
exactly the kind of cross-statement tracing this lesson teaches, commonly
seen when Indian companies raise capital via QIPs or rights issues
(`[01.06]`).

## US Market Example
When a US company repurchases its own shares (a common capital allocation
strategy, particularly among mature, cash-generative companies like many
large US technology firms), this transaction simultaneously reduces
**Cash** (Balance Sheet asset), reduces **Shareholders' Equity** (fewer
shares outstanding), and appears as a **negative Financing Cash Flow**
item — another clean, traceable illustration of a single capital
allocation decision rippling consistently across the connected statements.

## Historical Example
Forensic accountants investigating historical accounting fraud cases
(including Enron in the US and Satyam in India, both referenced in
`[07.01]` and covered fully in `[31.10]`) have specifically used
three-statement reconciliation techniques — checking whether reported
profit genuinely flows through to consistent cash and balance sheet
changes — as a core investigative method for identifying where and how
financial statements had been manipulated or misrepresented, demonstrating
this lesson's framework's real, practical forensic value beyond routine
analysis.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional financial modeling analysts build fully **linked
three-statement models** in spreadsheets, where changing a single input
assumption (e.g., a revenue growth rate) automatically flows through and
updates all three statements consistently — a technical skill directly
built on the conceptual connections this lesson teaches, forming the
backbone of most professional equity research and corporate finance work.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors who understand these connections gain a powerful
sense-checking tool — if a company reports strong net profit growth but
its cash balance and retained earnings aren't moving in a way consistent
with that profit, understanding the expected linkages helps flag the
discrepancy as worth investigating further, rather than accepting the
headline profit figure at face value.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts specifically watch for **unexplained divergences**
between the statements — for example, rapidly growing accounts receivable
(a Balance Sheet item) alongside strong reported revenue growth (Income
Statement) can signal that revenue is being recognized before cash is
actually being collected, a pattern worth investigating further using the
revenue recognition concepts covered fully in `[07.06]`.

## Common Mistakes
- Reading each statement in complete isolation, missing the expected
  connections and cross-checks between them.
- Not verifying that a company's cash flow statement's ending cash
  balance actually matches its balance sheet's cash line item.
- Assuming any single transaction only affects one statement, when in
  reality most transactions ripple across all three simultaneously.

## Misconceptions
- **"The three statements are independent documents with no required
  relationship to each other."** They form a single, interconnected
  accounting system — genuine financial statement literacy requires
  understanding these connections, not just reading each document in
  isolation.
- **"Only professional analysts need to understand these connections."**
  As this lesson's retail perspective illustrates, even a basic
  understanding of expected linkages gives any investor a valuable,
  practical sense-checking tool.
- **"If a company's statements don't perfectly reconcile in a way you can
  immediately trace, it must be fraud."** While unexplained divergences
  are worth investigating, plenty of legitimate accounting complexity
  (multiple subsidiaries, currency translation, complex non-cash items)
  can make exact reconciliation challenging even for genuinely honest
  companies — investigate divergences with appropriate humility, not
  automatic suspicion.

## Key Takeaways
- Net Profit flows into Retained Earnings (Balance Sheet Equity); the Cash
  Flow Statement reconciles Net Profit to actual cash change; ending cash
  on the Cash Flow Statement must match the Balance Sheet's cash line.
- Every transaction typically ripples across all three statements
  simultaneously, not just one.
- Forensic accountants use three-statement reconciliation as a core fraud
  investigation technique.
- Understanding these connections gives any investor, not just
  professionals, a valuable sense-checking tool.

## Summary
The three financial statements form a single, interconnected accounting
system — net profit flows into retained earnings, the cash flow statement
reconciles profit to actual cash, and that cash figure must match the
balance sheet — meaning genuine financial statement literacy requires
tracing these connections, not just reading each statement in isolation.
This closed-loop understanding, used by forensic accountants investigating
cases like Enron and Satyam, completes this module's core three-statement
foundation and sets up the next lesson's deeper focus on revenue
recognition — one of the most common places these connections reveal
earnings quality concerns.

## Quiz
1. Where does Net Profit flow on the Balance Sheet?
   a) Into Current Liabilities
   b) Into Retained Earnings (Shareholders' Equity)
   c) Into Accounts Payable
   d) It doesn't flow anywhere on the Balance Sheet

2. What must the Cash Flow Statement's ending cash balance match?
   a) The Income Statement's revenue figure
   b) The Balance Sheet's cash line item
   c) Nothing — they're unrelated
   d) The company's stock price

3. Why do forensic accountants use three-statement reconciliation?
   a) It's a legal requirement with no analytical purpose
   b) To identify where financial statements may have been manipulated
   c) Only to calculate taxes
   d) It has no real investigative use

4. What can rapidly growing accounts receivable alongside strong revenue
   growth potentially signal?
   a) Nothing worth investigating
   b) That revenue may be recognized before cash is actually collected
   c) That the company has no customers
   d) That the company is debt-free

5. True or False: The three financial statements are completely
   independent with no required relationship to each other.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Net Profit flows into Retained Earnings within Shareholders' Equity.
2. **b)** — The Cash Flow Statement's ending cash balance must match the Balance Sheet's cash line item.
3. **b)** — Forensic accountants use this technique to identify statement manipulation or misrepresentation.
4. **b)** — This pattern can signal revenue being recognized before cash collection, worth investigating.
5. **b) False** — The three statements form a single, interconnected accounting system.
