## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Depreciation** is the accounting practice of spreading the cost of a
**tangible** asset (machinery, buildings, vehicles) over its useful life,
rather than expensing the entire cost in the year of purchase.
**Amortization** is the equivalent concept applied to **intangible**
assets (patents, trademarks, goodwill from acquisitions). Both are
**non-cash expenses** — they reduce reported profit on the Income
Statement without any actual cash leaving the company in that period (the
cash was already spent when the asset was originally purchased). **EBITDA**
(Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization) adds
these non-cash charges back to Operating Profit, a metric widely used by
analysts to approximate a company's core cash-generating capacity before
financing, tax, and non-cash accounting effects.

### Intuition
Depreciation and amortization exist because of the **matching
principle** — if a company buys a machine for Rs 10 crore that will be
used productively for 10 years, expensing the entire Rs 10 crore in year
one would understate that year's true profitability (since the machine
will keep generating revenue for years) and overstate profitability in
later years (when the "free" already-paid-for machine keeps producing
revenue with no further cost recorded). Spreading the cost evenly (or by
another systematic method) across the asset's useful life better matches
costs to the revenue they help generate.

### Mental Model
```
[Company buys machinery for Rs 10 crore, useful life 10 years]
        |
        v
CASH FLOW STATEMENT: Rs 10 crore cash outflow recorded ONCE,
                       in the year of purchase (as Capital
                       Expenditure, per [07.04])
        |
        v
INCOME STATEMENT: Rs 1 crore DEPRECIATION expense recorded
                    EACH YEAR for 10 years (spreading the cost,
                    NO actual cash leaves the company in years
                    2-10 for this expense)
        |
        v
EBITDA = Operating Profit (EBIT) + Depreciation + Amortization
         (adds back these non-cash charges to approximate core
          cash-generating capacity)
```

### Visual Explanation
The gap between when cash is actually spent (Cash Flow Statement, all at
once) and when the corresponding expense is recognized (Income Statement,
spread over years) is precisely why depreciation and amortization are
non-cash items requiring add-back in EBITDA and cash flow reconciliation.

### ASCII Diagram
```
   DEPRECIATION TIMING MISMATCH (illustrative)

   Year:                1     2     3     4    ...    10
   Cash outflow
   (CapEx, [07.04]):   -10     0     0     0    ...     0
                        ^
                        | (entire Rs 10cr spent in Year 1)

   Depreciation expense
   (Income Statement):  -1    -1    -1    -1    ...    -1
                        (spread evenly across all 10 years,
                         NO further cash leaves the company
                         for this specific expense after Year 1)

   RECONCILING PROFIT TO CASH (Cash Flow Statement, Operating
   section):
   Net Profit (includes -1 depreciation charge)
   + ADD BACK Depreciation (+1, since it's non-cash)
   = Cash-adjusted operating figure
```

### Flowchart
```
[Company reports Net Profit including a Depreciation/
 Amortization expense]
        |
        v
[Cash Flow Statement ADDS BACK Depreciation/Amortization to
 Net Profit, since no cash actually left the company for this
 specific expense in the current period]
        |
        v
[EBITDA = Operating Profit + Depreciation + Amortization --
 used by analysts to compare companies with different asset
 bases/depreciation policies on a more standardized basis]
```

## Real World Analogy
Depreciation is like paying for a multi-year gym membership in one lump
sum but recognizing the "cost" of that membership gradually, month by
month, as you actually use it — even though the cash left your account
entirely in month one, it would be misleading to say the gym cost you
nothing in months two through twelve; spreading the recognized cost across
the months you actually benefit from the membership gives a more accurate
picture of your true monthly "cost of living," exactly as depreciation
aims to more accurately reflect a company's true periodic cost of using
its long-term assets.

## Indian Market Example
Indian capital-intensive companies (telecom, power generation,
infrastructure — the same sectors highlighted in `[07.07]`'s capital
structure discussion) typically carry very large depreciation charges
given their heavy investment in physical infrastructure — analysts
covering these sectors routinely focus on **EBITDA margins** rather than
net margins alone, since large depreciation charges (a real but non-cash
expense) can make net profit figures look weaker than the company's
actual underlying cash-generating capacity.

## US Market Example
US technology and media companies that grow substantially through
**acquisitions** often carry large **goodwill and intangible asset
amortization** charges on their income statements (reflecting the premium
paid over the acquired company's identifiable net assets) — analysts
covering these companies frequently use **"adjusted EBITDA"** or
**non-GAAP earnings** measures that add back these amortization charges,
though professional analysts also caution that excessive reliance on
"adjusted" figures that exclude too many real, recurring costs can itself
become a earnings-quality concern worth scrutinizing (connecting directly
to `[07.06]`'s earnings quality theme).

## Historical Example
The rise of **EBITDA as a widely-used metric** during the 1980s, notably
associated with the leveraged buyout boom of that era (`[07.07]`),
reflected investors' and analysts' desire for a standardized measure of
cash-generating capacity that could support debt-servicing analysis
independent of each company's specific depreciation policy choices and
capital structure — though EBITDA has also been criticized (notably by
investor Warren Buffett, among others) for potentially obscuring genuinely
real and recurring capital expenditure needs that eventually require cash,
even though depreciation itself is non-cash in any given period.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional credit analysts specifically use **Debt / EBITDA** ratios as
a standard measure of how many years of core cash-generating capacity
would be needed to repay a company's total debt — a widely-used,
standardized leverage metric in credit analysis and rating methodologies,
directly building on this lesson's EBITDA concept.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors sometimes treat EBITDA as equivalent to "cash profit"
without understanding its limitations — EBITDA still ignores real capital
expenditure needs (money that genuinely must be spent to maintain/replace
aging assets, even though depreciation itself isn't a current-period cash
outflow) and working capital changes, making Free Cash Flow (`[07.04]`)
generally a more complete cash-based metric than EBITDA alone.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts specifically distinguish **maintenance capital
expenditure** (spending required just to keep existing operations
running, roughly analogous to ongoing depreciation) from **growth capital
expenditure** (spending to expand capacity/enter new markets) — since
EBITDA effectively ignores both categories entirely, a more refined
analysis often separately considers whether EBITDA-based cash generation
is sufficient to cover at least the maintenance capital expenditure
component, not just debt service.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating EBITDA as equivalent to actual cash profit, ignoring real
  capital expenditure needs.
- Not recognizing that large depreciation/amortization charges are real
  but non-cash, potentially making net profit look weaker than genuine
  cash-generating capacity for capital-intensive businesses.
- Over-relying on "adjusted EBITDA" or non-GAAP figures without checking
  what specifically has been excluded and why.

## Misconceptions
- **"Depreciation means the asset has no remaining value."** Depreciation
  is an accounting allocation of historical cost over useful life — it
  doesn't necessarily reflect the asset's genuine current market value or
  remaining productive capacity, which can differ from its depreciated
  "book value."
- **"EBITDA is a complete measure of a company's cash generation."** As
  the Professional Insights and Warren Buffett criticism note, EBITDA
  ignores real capital expenditure and working capital needs — Free Cash
  Flow (`[07.04]`) is generally a more complete cash-based measure.
- **"All non-cash items are depreciation/amortization."** Other non-cash
  items exist too (stock-based compensation, changes in provisions/
  reserves, unrealized gains/losses on investments) — depreciation and
  amortization are simply the most common and widely discussed examples.

## Key Takeaways
- Depreciation (tangible assets) and amortization (intangible assets)
  spread an asset's cost over its useful life, matching costs to the
  revenue they help generate — both are non-cash expenses.
- EBITDA adds back these non-cash charges to approximate core cash-
  generating capacity, useful for cross-company comparison but incomplete
  (ignores capital expenditure and working capital needs).
- Free Cash Flow remains a more complete cash-based metric than EBITDA
  alone.
- Debt/EBITDA is a standard institutional leverage metric building
  directly on this lesson's concepts.

## Summary
Depreciation and amortization — non-cash expenses spreading an asset's
cost over its useful life via the matching principle — explain why
reported profit can differ from actual current-period cash flow, and why
analysts widely use EBITDA to approximate core cash-generating capacity,
while remaining aware of EBITDA's real limitations regarding capital
expenditure and working capital needs that Free Cash Flow (`[07.04]`)
more fully captures. This completes the module's deep-dive into individual
statement mechanics, setting up the final two lessons' practical focus on
actually reading real Indian and US company filings.

## Revision Notes
- Depreciation: tangible assets. Amortization: intangible assets. Both
  spread cost over useful life via the matching principle — non-cash
  expenses.
- EBITDA = Operating Profit (EBIT) + Depreciation + Amortization —
  approximates core cash-generating capacity, but ignores CapEx/working
  capital needs.
- Free Cash Flow (`[07.04]`) is a more complete cash-based measure than
  EBITDA.
- Debt/EBITDA: standard institutional leverage metric.
- Warren Buffett and others have criticized over-reliance on EBITDA for
  ignoring real, recurring capital needs.

## Quiz
1. What is depreciation applied to?
   a) Intangible assets only  b) Tangible assets (machinery, buildings, vehicles)  c) Cash only  d) Revenue

2. What is EBITDA?
   a) Net Profit minus Taxes only
   b) Operating Profit with Depreciation and Amortization added back
   c) Revenue minus COGS
   d) Total Assets minus Total Liabilities

3. Why is EBITDA considered an incomplete cash-based measure?
   a) It's actually a complete and perfect measure
   b) It ignores real capital expenditure and working capital needs
   c) It only applies to Indian companies
   d) It includes too many cash items

4. What is Debt/EBITDA commonly used for?
   a) Calculating dividend payments
   b) A standard leverage metric estimating years needed to repay debt from cash-generating capacity
   c) Setting stock prices directly
   d) Calculating income tax

5. True or False: Depreciation expense means cash is actually leaving the
   company in the current period for that specific charge.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Depreciation applies to tangible assets like machinery, buildings, and vehicles.
2. **b)** — EBITDA adds back Depreciation and Amortization to Operating Profit.
3. **b)** — EBITDA ignores real capital expenditure and working capital needs, unlike Free Cash Flow.
4. **b)** — Debt/EBITDA is a standard leverage metric used in credit analysis.
5. **b) False** — Depreciation is a non-cash expense; the actual cash was spent when the asset was originally purchased.
