# [07.07] Debt, Equity & Capital Structure on the Balance Sheet

> Module: Financial Statements · Lesson 7 of 10 · Estimated time: 30 minutes

## Learning Objectives

- Learn the trade-offs debt financing introduces (leverage's double edge).
- Calculate and interpret interest coverage ratio.
- Recognize how capital structure differs meaningfully by industry.

## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Capital structure** refers to the specific mix of debt and equity a
company uses to fund its assets and operations. **Debt financing**
(borrowing via loans or bonds) requires fixed interest payments and
principal repayment regardless of business performance, but doesn't dilute
ownership. **Equity financing** (issuing shares) requires no fixed
repayment obligation but dilutes existing owners' proportional stake and
typically costs more over the long run since equity investors demand
higher returns to compensate for bearing more risk (they're paid after
creditors in any distress scenario, per `[01.03]`'s equity-vs-debt
distinction).

### Intuition
Debt is often called "leverage" because, like a physical lever, it can
amplify returns to equity holders when a business performs well (since
debt holders only receive their fixed interest regardless of how well the
business does, leaving more upside for equity holders) — but this same
leverage amplifies losses when a business performs poorly, since fixed
debt obligations must still be paid even when revenue and profit decline,
directly connecting to the 2008 GFC leverage example from `[07.03]`.

### Mental Model
```
CAPITAL STRUCTURE SPECTRUM:

  100% EQUITY  <-------------------------------->  HIGH DEBT/LEVERAGE
  (no leverage,      (moderate, typical             (high leverage,
   no fixed            capital structure)             amplified
   obligations,                                        returns AND
   most dilution)                                       amplified risk)

  LEVERAGE'S DOUBLE EDGE:
  Good business performance + debt --> AMPLIFIED equity returns
  Poor business performance + debt --> AMPLIFIED equity losses,
                                          potential default risk
```

### Visual Explanation
Interest Coverage Ratio (Operating Profit / Interest Expense) is a key
practical check on whether a company's debt level is safely serviceable
by its actual earnings.

### ASCII Diagram
```
   INTEREST COVERAGE RATIO EXAMPLE

   Operating Profit (EBIT):      Rs 1,500 crore
   Interest Expense:               Rs 300 crore
   ---------------------------------------------
   Interest Coverage Ratio = 1,500 / 300 = 5.0x

   INTERPRETATION GUIDE (illustrative, varies by industry):
   Ratio > 5x:    Generally comfortable debt servicing capacity
   Ratio 2-5x:    Moderate -- worth monitoring trend
   Ratio < 2x:    Elevated risk -- limited cushion if earnings decline
   Ratio < 1x:    Operating profit doesn't even cover interest --
                   significant financial distress risk
```


## Real World Analogy
Capital structure is like choosing how to fund a home purchase — paying
entirely in cash (100% equity-like, no fixed payment obligation, but
requires having/committing all that capital upfront) versus taking a large
mortgage (debt-like, requiring fixed monthly payments regardless of your
income fluctuations, but letting you commit less upfront capital and
potentially benefit more if the home's value rises significantly relative
to your smaller cash investment). A homeowner with a very large mortgage
relative to their income faces genuine risk if their income drops
temporarily — exactly the leverage risk this lesson describes at the
corporate level.

## Indian Market Example
Indian companies in **capital-intensive sectors** like power generation,
infrastructure, and telecommunications have historically operated with
notably higher debt levels (given the large upfront capital required to
build physical infrastructure with predictable, long-term cash flows) than
companies in **asset-light sectors** like IT services (which typically
carry minimal debt, funding growth largely through retained equity
earnings) — illustrating how capital structure norms differ meaningfully
by industry, not a one-size-fits-all standard.

## US Market Example
US **private equity leveraged buyouts (LBOs)** — where a company is
acquired using a significant proportion of borrowed money, with the
acquired company's own future cash flows used to service that debt —
represent an extreme, deliberate application of the leverage principle
this lesson describes, aiming to amplify equity returns for the private
equity investors, while also meaningfully increasing financial risk if the
acquired business's performance disappoints relative to expectations.

## Historical Example
Highly leveraged financial institutions were a central factor in the
**2008 Global Financial Crisis** (`[07.03]`, `[31.01]`) — many banks and
investment firms operated with debt-to-equity ratios far higher than
typical non-financial companies (a structural feature of the banking
business model itself, but taken to unusually aggressive extremes at
several institutions leading into 2008), leaving minimal equity cushion to
absorb losses when asset values declined, directly illustrating this
lesson's "amplified losses" side of the leverage double-edge in the most
consequential possible real-world context.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional credit rating agencies (like CRISIL and ICRA in India, or
Moody's and S&P globally) assign formal credit ratings substantially based
on capital structure analysis — assessing debt levels, interest coverage,
and cash flow stability relative to debt obligations — with these ratings
directly affecting a company's borrowing costs, since lower-rated
(higher-perceived-risk) companies must pay higher interest rates to
compensate lenders for the additional risk.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors sometimes assume "more debt is always worse" without
considering industry context — a stable, predictable-cash-flow utility
company can safely carry meaningfully more debt than a highly cyclical or
unpredictable-cash-flow business, making industry-relative comparison
(not an absolute debt level judgment) the more appropriate analytical
approach.

## Professional Insights
Professional credit analysts specifically examine **debt covenants** —
contractual conditions attached to a company's borrowing agreements
(e.g., maintaining a minimum interest coverage ratio, or a maximum
debt-to-equity level) — since breaching these covenants can trigger
serious consequences (accelerated repayment demands, restricted operating
flexibility) well before an outright default occurs, making covenant
awareness an important, if more advanced, credit analysis skill.

## Common Mistakes
- Judging a company's debt level as "good" or "bad" in absolute terms
  without comparing it to industry peers with similar cash flow stability
  characteristics.
- Ignoring the interest coverage ratio and focusing only on the debt-to-
  equity ratio, missing whether current earnings can actually service the
  debt comfortably.
- Assuming all leverage is inherently dangerous, missing that moderate,
  well-managed leverage is a normal, often value-additive part of most
  companies' capital structure.

## Misconceptions
- **"Zero debt is always the safest, best capital structure."** While
  debt-free companies avoid leverage risk entirely, moderate, well-managed
  debt can be an efficient, lower-cost capital source (interest is
  typically tax-deductible, and debt doesn't dilute ownership) — the goal
  is appropriate, sustainable leverage for the specific business, not
  necessarily zero debt.
- **"All industries should have similar debt-to-equity ratios."** As the
  Indian power/infrastructure versus IT services comparison illustrates,
  appropriate capital structure varies substantially by industry cash flow
  characteristics.
- **"High leverage always leads to financial distress."** Many companies
  successfully operate with significant, well-managed leverage for
  extended periods — distress typically results from leverage combined
  with deteriorating business performance or cash flow, not leverage
  alone.

## Key Takeaways
- Capital structure is the debt-equity financing mix; debt offers no
  dilution but fixed obligations, equity offers no fixed obligations but
  dilutes ownership.
- Leverage amplifies both returns (in good times) and losses (in bad
  times) — a genuine double edge, illustrated starkly by the 2008 GFC.
- Interest Coverage Ratio (EBIT / Interest Expense) is a key practical
  debt-servicing capacity check.
- Appropriate capital structure varies significantly by industry cash
  flow stability, not a universal standard.

## Summary
Capital structure — the mix of debt and equity financing a company
uses — introduces a genuine trade-off between the no-dilution-but-fixed-
obligation nature of debt and the no-fixed-obligation-but-dilutive nature
of equity, with leverage's double-edged amplification of both returns and
risk starkly illustrated by the 2008 Global Financial Crisis. Understanding
industry-relative capital structure norms and practical checks like
interest coverage ratio completes this module's balance sheet analytical
toolkit, setting up the next lesson's focus on depreciation, amortization,
and other non-cash items that further shape reported financial statements.

   a) It has no effect on returns or risk
   b) It amplifies both good-time returns and bad-time losses
   c) It only ever amplifies returns, never losses
   d) It only ever amplifies losses, never returns

3. How is Interest Coverage Ratio calculated?
   a) Revenue divided by Debt
   b) Operating Profit (EBIT) divided by Interest Expense
   c) Net Profit divided by Revenue
   d) Assets divided by Liabilities

4. What historical event starkly illustrates leverage's downside risk?
   a) The 2021 Zomato IPO
   b) The 2008 Global Financial Crisis
   c) The 1875 founding of BSE
   d) The 2023 T+1 settlement transition

5. True or False: Zero debt is always the objectively safest and best
   capital structure for every company.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Capital structure is the debt-equity financing mix a company uses.
2. **b)** — Leverage amplifies both returns in good times and losses in bad times.
3. **b)** — Interest Coverage Ratio = Operating Profit (EBIT) / Interest Expense.
4. **b)** — The 2008 GFC starkly illustrated leverage's downside risk at highly leveraged financial institutions.
5. **b) False** — Moderate, well-managed debt can be an efficient capital source; appropriate structure depends on the specific business.
