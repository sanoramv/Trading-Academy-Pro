## Core Concepts

### Definition
The **three financial statements** every public company must prepare and
disclose are: the **Income Statement** (also called the Profit & Loss
statement, or P&L) — showing revenue, expenses, and profit over a period;
the **Balance Sheet** — showing what a company owns (assets), owes
(liabilities), and the residual owners' claim (equity) at a single point in
time; and the **Cash Flow Statement** — showing actual cash moving in and
out of the business over a period, categorized into operating, investing,
and financing activities.

### Intuition
Each statement answers a different question that, together, gives a
complete financial picture no single statement could provide alone: the
Income Statement asks **"Did the company make money this period?"**, the
Balance Sheet asks **"What does the company own and owe right now?"**, and
the Cash Flow Statement asks **"Where did the actual cash come from and go
to?"** A company can show a profit on its Income Statement while running
out of cash (if profits are tied up in unpaid customer invoices, for
example) — which is exactly why analysts insist on reading all three
together, never just one in isolation.

### Mental Model
```
INCOME STATEMENT:    "Did we make money?"        (a PERIOD — e.g.,
                       Revenue - Expenses = Profit  a quarter or year)

BALANCE SHEET:        "What do we own & owe?"     (a SNAPSHOT — a
                       Assets = Liabilities +       single point in
                                Equity               time)

CASH FLOW STATEMENT:  "Where did cash actually    (a PERIOD — same
                        come from & go?"            timeframe as the
                       Operating + Investing +       Income Statement)
                       Financing = Net Cash Change
```

### Visual Explanation
The three statements are deeply interconnected — profit from the Income
Statement flows into the Balance Sheet's equity section (as retained
earnings), and the Cash Flow Statement explains the actual cash-based
reality behind the Income Statement's accrual-based profit figure.

## Real World Analogy
Think of assessing a company like assessing a person's overall financial
health using three different documents: their monthly pay stub (Income
Statement — did they earn money this month?), their net worth statement
listing everything they own and owe (Balance Sheet — what's their overall
financial position right now?), and their bank account transaction history
(Cash Flow Statement — where did their actual money really come from and
go?). A person could show a healthy pay stub while their bank account is
nearly empty (spending it all, or having it tied up in illiquid assets) —
exactly the kind of gap only becomes visible when you look at all three
documents together.

## Indian Market Example
Indian public companies file their financial statements as part of their
**annual reports** and **quarterly results**, available on the **NSE and
BSE websites**, as well as directly from company investor relations pages,
prepared under **Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS)**, which are largely
converged with international standards (IFRS) — a topic developed further
in `[07.09]`'s dedicated treatment of reading an Indian annual report.

## US Market Example
US public companies file detailed financial statements with the **SEC**
(`[03.02]`'s counterpart regulator) as part of their **10-K** (annual
report) and **10-Q** (quarterly report) filings, prepared under **US GAAP
(Generally Accepted Accounting Principles)** — publicly accessible via the
SEC's **EDGAR database**, a topic developed further in `[07.10]`'s
dedicated treatment of reading a US 10-K/10-Q.

## Historical Example
The **Enron scandal (2001)** in the US, and India's own **Satyam
scandal (2009)** — covered in full case-study depth in `[31.10]` — both
involved companies presenting financial statements that misrepresented
their true financial condition, contributing to major reforms in financial
reporting standards and auditing requirements (including the US
Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002) in the aftermath. These historical episodes
underscore why learning to read and critically assess financial statements
yourself — rather than relying purely on reported headline numbers or
others' interpretations — is a genuinely valuable, protective skill for
any serious investor.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional equity research analysts build detailed **financial
models** — essentially their own reconstructed, forward-projected versions
of all three statements — as the foundation of their investment
recommendations, treating the three-statement framework not as a one-time
reading exercise but as an ongoing, continuously-updated analytical
structure.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors very commonly look only at a single headline number
(often just revenue or profit growth) without examining the balance sheet
or cash flow statement at all — missing warning signs (like deteriorating
cash generation despite reported profit growth) that a fuller three-
statement read would reveal, precisely the gap this module exists to
close.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts often say **"profit is an opinion, cash is a
fact"** — reflecting that the Income Statement's profit figure involves
numerous accounting judgments and estimates (revenue recognition timing,
depreciation assumptions, and more, covered in `[07.06]`, `[07.08]`), while
the Cash Flow Statement reflects actual, harder-to-manipulate cash
movements, making it an especially valuable cross-check against the
Income Statement's reported profitability.

## Common Mistakes
- Looking at only the Income Statement (or worse, only revenue or EPS)
  without checking the Balance Sheet or Cash Flow Statement.
- Assuming reported profit automatically means the company generated
  actual cash during the period.
- Not knowing where to actually find these statements for a specific
  company (covered practically in `[07.09]`, `[07.10]`).

## Misconceptions
- **"A company reporting a profit is automatically in good financial
  health."** As this lesson's "profit is an opinion, cash is a fact"
  framing suggests, profit alone doesn't guarantee genuine financial
  health — a full three-statement read is needed.
- **"The Balance Sheet shows a company's performance over the year, like
  the Income Statement does."** The Balance Sheet is a snapshot at a single
  point in time (like a photograph), not a performance-over-a-period
  measure (like the Income Statement and Cash Flow Statement, which are
  more like a video covering a period).
- **"Financial statements are too complex for a beginner to ever
  understand."** This module builds this literacy from zero, one
  statement at a time — no prior accounting background is assumed or
  required.

## Key Takeaways
- The three financial statements are the Income Statement (profitability
  over a period), Balance Sheet (financial position at a point in time),
  and Cash Flow Statement (actual cash movement over a period).
- All three must be read together — any one alone can present a misleading
  or incomplete picture.
- "Profit is an opinion, cash is a fact" captures why the Cash Flow
  Statement is an especially valuable cross-check.
- Indian companies file under Ind AS (via NSE/BSE); US companies file
  under US GAAP (via SEC EDGAR).

## Summary
The three financial statements — Income Statement, Balance Sheet, and Cash
Flow Statement — together answer the fundamental questions of whether a
company is profitable, what it owns and owes, and where its actual cash
comes from and goes, with historical scandals like Enron and Satyam
underscoring why genuine, independent literacy in reading these statements
protects investors from relying purely on others' interpretations. This
overview sets up the next three lessons' detailed, statement-by-statement
treatment, starting with the Income Statement.

## Revision Notes
- Income Statement: profitability over a PERIOD (Revenue - Expenses =
  Profit).
- Balance Sheet: financial position at a POINT IN TIME (Assets =
  Liabilities + Equity).
- Cash Flow Statement: actual cash movement over a PERIOD (Operating +
  Investing + Financing = Net Change in Cash).
- "Profit is an opinion, cash is a fact" — Cash Flow Statement is a key
  cross-check against Income Statement profit.
- Enron (2001, US) and Satyam (2009, India) scandals: historical
  motivation for genuine financial statement literacy.

## Quiz

   a) Revenue, Expenses, Profit

   a) Profit figures are always fraudulent
   b) Profit involves accounting judgments while cash flow reflects actual movements
   c) Cash flow is always less reliable than profit
   d) They mean exactly the same thing

4. What historical Indian scandal illustrates the danger of misrepresented
   financial statements?
   a) The 2021 Zomato IPO
   b) The Satyam scandal (2009)
   c) The 1875 founding of BSE
   d) The 2023 T+1 transition

5. True or False: A company reporting a profit is automatically in good
   financial health with no further analysis needed.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — The Income Statement, Balance Sheet, and Cash Flow Statement are the three core financial statements.
2. **b)** — The Balance Sheet shows what a company owns and owes at a specific point in time.
3. **b)** — Profit involves accounting judgments/estimates, while cash flow reflects actual, harder-to-manipulate cash movements.
4. **b)** — The Satyam scandal (2009) is India's clearest example of misrepresented financial statements.
5. **b) False** — A full three-statement read is needed; profit alone doesn't guarantee genuine financial health.
