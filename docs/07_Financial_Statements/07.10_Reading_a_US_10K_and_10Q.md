## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **10-K** is a comprehensive annual report US public companies must file
with the **SEC**, containing audited financial statements, detailed
business description, risk factors, and management's discussion and
analysis — structurally analogous to India's Annual Report (`[07.09]`) but
organized under a standardized, numbered "Item" format mandated by SEC
regulation. A **10-Q** is the quarterly equivalent, less comprehensive
(unaudited, though still SEC-reviewed) than the 10-K, analogous to India's
quarterly results. Both are freely accessible via the SEC's **EDGAR
(Electronic Data Gathering, Analysis, and Retrieval)** database.

### Intuition
The 10-K's standardized "Item" structure — mandated identically across
virtually all US public companies — makes it easier to find specific
information consistently across different companies compared to less
rigidly standardized formats, a structural advantage US filings offer over
some other markets' more variably-organized annual reports (including,
to some degree, India's, though Indian filings have also become
increasingly standardized under SEBI requirements).

### Mental Model
```
US 10-K STRUCTURE (key items, standardized across companies)

Part I:
  Item 1: Business (what the company does)
  Item 1A: RISK FACTORS (management's disclosed risks -- READ
            THIS CAREFULLY, often underappreciated by retail
            investors)
  Item 3: Legal Proceedings

Part II:
  Item 7: Management's Discussion & Analysis (MD&A) --
           equivalent to India's MD&A ([07.09])
  Item 8: Financial Statements and Supplementary Data --
           the three statements ([07.01]-[07.04]) + notes
           (equivalent to India's Notes to Accounts)
  Item 9A: Controls and Procedures -- internal control
            assessment (a post-Enron, Sarbanes-Oxley-era
            requirement, [07.06])
```

### Visual Explanation
The 10-K's "Item 1A: Risk Factors" section is one of the most valuable,
often-skipped sections — companies are required to disclose material
risks to their business, providing a genuinely useful, company-specific
risk checklist straight from the source.

## Real World Analogy
Reading a 10-K's standardized Item structure is like using a
well-organized reference textbook with a consistent chapter numbering
system across every edition and subject — once you know "Chapter 7 always
covers financial statements" in one textbook, you can navigate any other
textbook in the same series with the same expectation, saving significant
time compared to a series where each book is organized completely
differently and you must relearn the structure each time.

## Indian Market Example
An Indian investor researching a US-listed company they're considering
for international diversification (a concept developed in `[04.07]`) can
apply the exact same systematic reading approach from `[07.09]` — Auditor's
opinion equivalent, MD&A narrative check, Notes/financial statement
scrutiny — simply adapted to the 10-K's specific Item-numbered structure,
demonstrating that the underlying analytical skills from this entire
module transfer directly across markets, even when the specific document
format differs.

## US Market Example
**Apple Inc.'s 10-K** filings are widely used as a teaching example in
finance education globally, given the company's scale, filing quality, and
freely accessible EDGAR filing history spanning decades — students and
analysts routinely use Apple's (or similarly prominent companies')
historical 10-Ks to practice exactly the systematic reading approach this
lesson and `[07.09]` describe.

## Historical Example
The **Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002**, enacted directly in response to the
Enron scandal (`[07.06]`), significantly strengthened US 10-K disclosure
and internal control requirements — notably requiring CEO and CFO
personal certification of financial statement accuracy (Item 9A-related
requirements) and more rigorous auditor independence rules — a direct,
consequential regulatory response paralleling India's own post-Satyam
regulatory tightening, reinforcing this module's recurring theme of
scandal-driven disclosure reform appearing across multiple major markets,
not uniquely in any one country.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional analysts covering US-listed companies specifically compare
**year-over-year changes in the Risk Factors section (Item 1A)** — a
newly added risk factor, or significantly altered language around an
existing one, is treated as a meaningful signal worth investigating, even
before it appears anywhere else in the filing or in financial media
coverage.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors researching US stocks very commonly rely on financial
news summaries and stock screening apps without ever opening the actual
10-K, particularly missing the Risk Factors section — a genuinely
valuable, company-disclosed risk checklist available for free that most
retail investors never read directly.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts note that despite the 10-K's standardized format
(an advantage over less standardized filings), the **Risk Factors
section's language is often deliberately broad and boilerplate** in
places (covering generic risks that apply to almost any company in the
industry) — the analytical skill lies in identifying which specific risk
factors are genuinely company-specific and material versus generic legal
boilerplate included primarily for liability protection purposes.

## Common Mistakes
- Never opening the actual 10-K/10-Q, relying entirely on financial media
  summaries.
- Skipping the Risk Factors section (Item 1A) entirely.
- Assuming Indian and US filing formats are identical, missing the US
  filing's specific standardized Item-numbering structure.

## Misconceptions
- **"10-Ks are only useful for professional analysts, not retail
  investors."** As with Indian annual reports (`[07.09]`), the analytical
  skills and access are available to any investor willing to invest the
  reading time — no special professional status is required.
- **"All Risk Factors disclosed in a 10-K are equally significant and
  company-specific."** Many risk factors are generic, industry-wide
  boilerplate — the skill lies in distinguishing genuinely material,
  company-specific risks from standard legal-protection language.
- **"Reading a 10-Q gives you the same depth as a 10-K."** 10-Qs are
  less comprehensive (unaudited, though SEC-reviewed) than the full,
  audited annual 10-K.

## Key Takeaways
- The 10-K (annual) and 10-Q (quarterly) are the US equivalents of India's
  annual report and quarterly results, freely accessible via SEC EDGAR.
- The 10-K's standardized Item-numbered structure (especially Item 1A Risk
  Factors and Item 7 MD&A) offers consistent navigation across companies.
- Sarbanes-Oxley (2002), enacted after Enron, significantly strengthened
  US disclosure requirements — paralleling India's post-Satyam reforms.
- The same systematic reading skills from `[07.09]` transfer directly to
  US filings, just adapted to a different structural format.

## Summary
The US 10-K and 10-Q — accessible via SEC EDGAR and organized under a
standardized Item-numbered structure including the valuable, often-
overlooked Risk Factors section — represent the US equivalent of the
Indian annual report and quarterly results covered in `[07.09]`, with the
same underlying three-statement and earnings-quality analytical skills
transferring directly across both markets. This completes Module 07's
comprehensive financial statement literacy foundation — you can now read,
cross-check, and critically assess financial statements from both Indian
and US public companies, setting up Module 08's application of this
literacy to full fundamental analysis.

## Quiz
1. What is a 10-K?
   a) A quarterly unaudited report
   b) A comprehensive annual report filed with the SEC
   c) A type of stock order
   d) A tax form only

2. Where can US 10-K and 10-Q filings be accessed for free?
   a) Nowhere publicly  b) The SEC's EDGAR database  c) Only via paid subscription  d) Only from the company's competitors

3. What does Item 1A in a 10-K cover?
   a) Executive compensation only
   b) Risk Factors — company-disclosed material risks
   c) Stock price history
   d) Marketing budget

4. What regulatory reform strengthened US disclosure requirements after
   the Enron scandal?
   a) SEBI Act, 1992
   b) The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002
   c) The 2023 T+1 settlement transition
   d) The Dodd-Frank Act

5. True or False: A 10-Q provides the same audited depth and comprehensiveness
   as a 10-K.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — A 10-K is a comprehensive annual report filed with the SEC.
2. **b)** — 10-K and 10-Q filings are freely accessible via SEC EDGAR.
3. **b)** — Item 1A covers Risk Factors, company-disclosed material risks.
4. **b)** — The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 strengthened US disclosure requirements after Enron.
5. **b) False** — 10-Qs are less comprehensive (unaudited, though SEC-reviewed) than the full, audited 10-K.
