# [25.04] Risk-Reward Ratio and Expectancy

> Module: Risk Management · Lesson 4 of 8 · Estimated time: 30 minutes

## Learning Objectives

- Understand the mathematical relationship between risk-reward ratio, win rate, and expectancy (`[24.01]`).

- Apply risk-reward thinking to evaluate whether a specific trade setup is worth taking.

## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Risk-reward ratio** compares the distance from entry to stop-loss
(risk) against the distance from entry to profit target (reward),
typically expressed as a ratio like 1:2 or 1:3 (risk:reward). It is the
per-trade, price-distance version of `[24.01]`'s expectancy formula —
combined with win rate, it determines whether a strategy has positive
expectancy. A trade with a 1:3 risk-reward ratio needs to win only 25%
of the time to break even (before costs); a trade with a 1:1 ratio needs
to win more than 50% of the time.

### Intuition
Risk-reward ratio and win rate are two sides of the same expectancy
coin (`[24.01]`) — a strategy doesn't need a high win rate if its
winners are proportionally larger than its losers, and doesn't need
large winners if it wins often enough. Evaluating a setup's risk-reward
ratio *before* entry, using the stop placement from `[25.03]` and a
realistic target, is how a trader checks whether a specific trade is
worth taking independent of gut feeling.

### Mental Model
```
RISK-REWARD RATIO:
  Risk   = Entry Price - Stop-Loss Price
  Reward = Target Price - Entry Price
  Ratio  = Risk : Reward  (e.g., if Risk=10, Reward=30 -> 1:3)

BREAKEVEN WIN RATE (minimum win rate needed, ignoring costs):
  Breakeven Win Rate = Risk / (Risk + Reward)

  Examples:
    1:1 ratio  -> Breakeven Win Rate = 10/(10+10) = 50%
    1:2 ratio  -> Breakeven Win Rate = 10/(10+20) = 33.3%
    1:3 ratio  -> Breakeven Win Rate = 10/(10+30) = 25%

  A strategy's ACTUAL win rate above this breakeven threshold
  produces POSITIVE expectancy, [24.01]; below it, NEGATIVE.
```

### Visual Explanation
Plotting breakeven win rate against risk-reward ratio produces a
downward-sloping curve — as the ratio improves (larger reward relative
to risk), the win rate required to merely break even drops sharply,
visually confirming why `[24.02]`'s trend-following (high ratio, low
win rate needed) and `[24.03]`'s mean-reversion (lower ratio, higher win
rate needed) can both be viable, provided each strategy's actual win
rate clears its own specific breakeven threshold.

## Real World Analogy
Risk-reward ratio and win rate are like a restaurant's average check
size versus how often tables order the expensive special: a restaurant
doesn't need every table to order the special (high win rate) if the
special's margin is large enough (high reward relative to cost) to
offset the many tables that order cheaper items — profitability depends
on both figures together, not either alone.

## Indian Market Example
A trader identifies a Nifty setup with entry at 24,000, a structural
stop at 23,940 (`[25.03]`, 60-point risk), and a realistic target at the
next resistance level of 24,180 (180-point reward) — a 1:3 risk-reward
ratio requiring only a 25% win rate to break even; if the trader's
journaled win rate for similar setups (`[24.12]`) is 40%, the setup is
consistent with meaningfully positive expectancy. **Hypothetical
Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader identifies an SPY setup with a 1:1 risk-reward ratio,
requiring better than a 50% win rate to break even — checking their
trading journal (`[24.12]`) and finding their actual win rate for this
specific setup type is only 45%, correctly concluding the setup as
currently structured has negative expectancy despite "feeling" like a
good trade. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
The risk-reward-ratio-and-win-rate framework this lesson describes
traces directly to the same expectancy formalization (`[24.01]`)
popularized by Van Tharp and other trading psychology/money-management
writers from the 1990s onward — a framework that shifted retail trading
education's focus from "how often do I win" toward the more complete
"what is my actual expected value per trade."

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional quant desks calculate risk-reward and expected win rate
systematically across every strategy, often requiring a minimum
backtested Sharpe ratio (`[27.08]` previewed) or expectancy threshold
before allocating capital — this lesson's manual per-trade calculation
is the individual-trader-scale version of that same discipline.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders frequently take trades based on setup "look" without
calculating the actual risk-reward ratio or checking it against their
own journaled win rate for similar setups — a gap this lesson's
breakeven win rate calculation directly closes with a simple, fast, pre-
trade check.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders emphasize that the *reward* side of the ratio must
be realistic — targeting an arbitrary large multiple of the stop without
a genuine structural basis (e.g., a real resistance level, `[02.04]`)
produces an inflated, unrealistic risk-reward ratio that doesn't reflect
actual achievable outcomes.

## Common Mistakes
- Calculating risk-reward ratio using an unrealistic, arbitrary target
  rather than a genuine structural level likely to be reached.
- Taking trades with a risk-reward ratio below the strategy's own
  journaled win rate's breakeven threshold.
- Ignoring transaction costs and slippage, which raise the effective
  breakeven win rate above the theoretical calculation.

## Misconceptions
- **"A higher risk-reward ratio is always better regardless of win
  rate."** The two must be evaluated together — a very high ratio with
  an unrealistically low actual win rate can still produce negative expectancy.
- **"Any positive risk-reward ratio (reward > risk) guarantees
  profitability."** Only if the actual win rate exceeds the calculated
  breakeven threshold for that specific ratio.
- **"Risk-reward ratio alone determines whether a strategy is valid."**
  It is one input to `[24.01]`'s full expectancy calculation, alongside
  actual, journaled win rate.

## Key Takeaways
- Risk-reward ratio = Risk (entry to stop) : Reward (entry to target).
- Breakeven win rate = Risk / (Risk + Reward) — the minimum win rate
  needed to avoid a loss, ignoring costs.
- A favorable risk-reward ratio lowers the win rate needed for positive
  expectancy, but must be checked against actual, journaled performance.
- Realistic targets (genuine structural levels) are essential — an
  arbitrary target inflates the calculated ratio misleadingly.

## Summary
Risk-reward ratio, combined with a strategy's actual win rate via the
breakeven win rate formula, is the per-trade mechanism that operationalizes
`[24.01]`'s expectancy framework in practice — a favorable ratio lowers
the win rate needed to profit, but only realistic, structurally-grounded
targets and honestly journaled win rates (`[24.12]`) make the calculation
meaningful rather than misleading. The next lesson turns to maximum
drawdown — the cumulative, portfolio-level consequence of how well or
poorly risk-reward and position sizing decisions compound over many trades.

## Quiz
1. What is the risk-reward ratio formula?
   a) Reward ÷ Win Rate  b) Risk (entry-to-stop) : Reward (entry-to-target)  c) Stop ÷ Target only  d) Win Rate × Risk

2. What is the breakeven win rate for a 1:2 risk-reward ratio?
   a) 50%  b) 33.3%  c) 25%  d) 10%

3. Why must profit targets be based on realistic structural levels?
   a) It doesn't matter  b) An arbitrary target inflates the calculated ratio misleadingly  c) Targets are always guaranteed  d) Only stop-losses matter

4. What does a favorable risk-reward ratio do to the win rate needed for positive expectancy?
   a) Increases it  b) Lowers it  c) Has no effect  d) Makes win rate irrelevant

5. True or False: Any risk-reward ratio where reward exceeds risk guarantees a profitable strategy.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Risk (entry-to-stop) : Reward (entry-to-target).
2. **b)** — 33.3%, from Risk/(Risk+Reward) = 1/(1+2).
3. **b)** — An arbitrary target inflates the calculated ratio misleadingly.
4. **b)** — Lowers it, per the breakeven win rate formula.
5. **b) False** — The actual win rate must also exceed the breakeven threshold for that ratio.
