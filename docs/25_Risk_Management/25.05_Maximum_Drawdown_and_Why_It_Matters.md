## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Drawdown** is the decline from an equity curve's most recent peak to
its subsequent trough, typically expressed as a percentage.
**Maximum drawdown (MDD)** is the largest such peak-to-trough decline
observed over a given track record — the single worst cumulative loss a
trader or strategy has experienced (or, in a backtest, would have
experienced) before recovering to a new equity high. MDD captures both
the *magnitude* of a losing sequence and, when paired with **drawdown
duration** (how long it took to recover), reveals how psychologically
and financially demanding a strategy is to actually trade through, not
just its long-run average expectancy (`[24.01]`).

### Intuition
A strategy's expectancy describes its long-run average outcome, but says
nothing about the *path* to that average — two strategies can share
identical long-run expectancy while one experiences a smooth, gradual
equity climb and the other experiences violent 40% drawdowns along the
way. Maximum drawdown quantifies exactly this "path roughness," directly
connecting to `[25.01]`'s recovery math: a strategy with a historical
50% max drawdown requires its account to gain 100% just to reach a new high.

### Mental Model
```
DRAWDOWN CALCULATION:
  Drawdown at any point = (Peak Equity - Current Equity) / Peak Equity

  MAXIMUM DRAWDOWN = the LARGEST such drawdown observed across
  the entire track record/backtest

DRAWDOWN DURATION:
  Time from the peak, through the trough, until equity reaches
  a NEW all-time high again -- can be much longer than the decline
  itself (the "recovery period")

WHY BOTH MATTER TOGETHER:
  A 20% drawdown that recovers in 2 weeks is very different, in
  practice, from a 20% drawdown that takes 18 months to recover from --
  same MAGNITUDE, very different psychological/financial burden
```

### Visual Explanation
An equity curve with its drawdowns shaded typically reveals that the
underwater periods (time spent below the prior peak) occupy far more of
the total timeline than the sharp declines themselves — recovery often
takes meaningfully longer than the original decline, a pattern directly
following from `[25.01]`'s asymmetric recovery math.

## Real World Analogy
Maximum drawdown is like the deepest, longest dip a hiking trail takes
before eventually reaching a higher summit than where it started — a
trail description quoting only "net elevation gain" hides whether the
hike is a gentle, steady climb or includes a brutal, demoralizing
descent partway through that many hikers would turn back from, even if
the trail's overall destination is higher.

## Indian Market Example
A trader backtests a Nifty theta-selling strategy (`[24.09]`) over five
years and finds a strong average annual return, but discovers the
strategy also experienced a 35% maximum drawdown during a period of
sharp, unexpected volatility (`[23.05]`'s regime-shift discussion) that
took over a year to fully recover from — information the average annual
return figure alone would never reveal. **Hypothetical Example** for
illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader compares two backtested strategies with nearly identical
long-run CAGR (compound annual growth rate) but very different maximum
drawdowns — 15% for one, 45% for the other — correctly concluding the
lower-drawdown strategy is meaningfully more practical to actually trade
through, even though the pure return figures look similar.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
The S&P 500 experienced a maximum drawdown of approximately 57% from
its October 2007 peak to its March 2009 trough during the Global
Financial Crisis (`[31.01]` previewed), taking over four years to
reach a new all-time high — a real, well-documented illustration of how
drawdown magnitude and duration together shaped the actual investor
experience of that period, far beyond what the eventual long-run
recovery alone would suggest.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional allocators frequently screen prospective fund managers
using maximum drawdown and drawdown duration as primary risk criteria,
sometimes weighting these figures as heavily as raw returns — a fund
with excellent average returns but an unacceptably large historical
drawdown may fail an allocator's risk tolerance screen entirely.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders commonly focus exclusively on a strategy's average or
total return when evaluating a backtest, overlooking maximum drawdown
entirely — yet drawdown, more than average return, is frequently what
determines whether a trader can psychologically stick with a strategy
long enough for its expectancy to materialize (`[25.01]`).

## Professional Insights
Professional risk managers often model *forward-looking* worst-case
drawdown scenarios beyond what a historical backtest captured, since a
strategy's future maximum drawdown can exceed its historical maximum —
past drawdown provides a floor for expectations, not a guaranteed ceiling.

## Common Mistakes
- Evaluating a strategy purely by average/total return without checking
  maximum drawdown at all.
- Assuming a strategy's future maximum drawdown cannot exceed its
  historical maximum.
- Underestimating drawdown *duration* — focusing only on the percentage
  decline while ignoring how long recovery historically took.

## Misconceptions
- **"A strategy's average return tells the whole story."** Two
  strategies with identical average returns can have vastly different
  practical risk profiles once drawdown is considered.
- **"Historical maximum drawdown is a hard ceiling on future
  drawdown."** It is a useful reference point, not a guarantee — future
  drawdowns can exceed historical ones, particularly during genuinely
  novel market conditions.
- **"Drawdown magnitude alone is sufficient information."** Duration
  (how long recovery took) matters just as much for assessing the
  practical burden of a drawdown.

## Key Takeaways
- Maximum drawdown = largest peak-to-trough equity decline in a track
  record, directly connected to `[25.01]`'s asymmetric recovery math.
- Drawdown duration (time to reach a new equity high) often exceeds the
  decline period itself — both magnitude and duration matter.
- The 2007–2009 S&P 500 drawdown (~57%, over 4 years to recover) is a
  real, concrete illustration of both dimensions together.
- Historical maximum drawdown is a reference point, not a guaranteed
  future ceiling.

## Summary
Maximum drawdown — the largest peak-to-trough decline in an equity
curve, paired with how long recovery took — reveals the practical,
lived experience of trading a strategy in a way average return figures
alone cannot, directly connecting to `[25.01]`'s asymmetric recovery
math and serving as a primary risk screening criterion for both
institutional allocators and disciplined individual traders. The next
lesson turns to correlation risk across positions — how holding multiple
positions that move together can quietly inflate a portfolio's true
drawdown risk beyond what any single position's risk-per-trade rule
would suggest.

## Quiz
1. What is maximum drawdown?
   a) Average return  b) The largest peak-to-trough equity decline in a track record  c) Total profit  d) Win rate

2. What is drawdown duration?
   a) The decline period only  b) Time from the peak until equity reaches a new all-time high  c) Number of trades taken  d) Account size

3. Why might two strategies with identical average returns be very different in practice?
   a) They can't differ  b) They can have very different maximum drawdowns  c) Average return is the only relevant metric  d) Drawdown has no practical impact

4. Approximately how large was the S&P 500's maximum drawdown during the 2007-2009 crisis?
   a) 10%  b) ~57%  c) 5%  d) 100%

5. True or False: Historical maximum drawdown guarantees future drawdowns will not exceed it.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — The largest peak-to-trough equity decline in a track record.
2. **b)** — Time from the peak until equity reaches a new all-time high.
3. **b)** — They can have very different maximum drawdowns, affecting practical tradeability.
4. **b)** — Approximately 57%, from the October 2007 peak to the March 2009 trough.
5. **b) False** — Historical MDD is a reference point, not a guaranteed ceiling on future drawdowns.
