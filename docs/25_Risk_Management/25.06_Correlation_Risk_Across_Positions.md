## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Correlation risk** is the risk that multiple positions, each
individually sized within a disciplined risk-per-trade limit (`[25.02]`),
move together in the same direction during an adverse event — causing
combined losses far larger than any single position's risk limit would
suggest. Positions can be correlated *directly* (e.g., multiple bank
stocks moving together), *sector-wide* (e.g., all IT stocks reacting to
the same currency move), or *market-wide* (e.g., most equities falling
together during a broad risk-off event, `[04.06]`), even when they
appear diversified by name or sector on the surface.

### Intuition
Risking 1% per trade (`[25.02]`) across five *uncorrelated* positions
genuinely limits combined risk to a manageable level even if all five
were simultaneously stopped out. But if those five positions are, in
practice, highly correlated (e.g., five large-cap Nifty stocks during a
broad market decline), a single adverse market move can trigger all
five stops simultaneously — the true combined risk is closer to 5% in a
single adverse event, not five independent 1% risks.

### Mental Model
```
NAIVE (INCORRECT) ASSUMPTION:
  5 positions x 1% risk each = 5% total risk, assuming INDEPENDENCE

REALITY WITH HIGH CORRELATION:
  5 HIGHLY CORRELATED positions, each stopped out by the SAME
  adverse market move = effective risk CLOSER TO the full 5%
  occurring SIMULTANEOUSLY, not spread across independent events

APPROXIMATE COMBINED RISK (simplified, illustrative):
  Combined Risk ~= sqrt( sum of each position's risk^2 +
                          2 x correlation x product of position risks )
  (Full portfolio variance math is beyond this lesson's scope --
   the KEY TAKEAWAY is: higher correlation -> combined risk approaches
   the SUM of individual risks, not a diversified-away smaller figure)

PRACTICAL RULE OF THUMB:
  Group positions by correlation cluster (e.g., "Indian bank stocks,"
  "US mega-cap tech") and cap TOTAL risk per cluster, not just per
  individual position
```

## Real World Analogy
Correlation risk is like insuring five houses built on the same
floodplain against fire, flood, and storm damage separately, believing
the risk is diversified across five different policies — when in
reality a single flood event triggers claims on all five simultaneously,
because the shared underlying vulnerability (the floodplain location)
was never actually diversified away, only the paperwork was split five ways.

## Indian Market Example
A trader holds five separate positions across HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank,
Axis Bank, Kotak Mahindra Bank, and SBI, each individually risked at 1%
of capital, believing this represents diversified risk — but during a
broad banking-sector selloff (e.g., triggered by an RBI policy surprise
affecting the whole sector, `[03.10]`), all five positions likely move
together, producing an effective single-event risk closer to 5%, not
five independent 1% risks. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader holds positions in five different mega-cap technology stocks,
each risked individually at 1%, but during a broad market risk-off
event driven by rising interest rate expectations (`[06.02]`), all five
growth-oriented tech names tend to decline together given their shared
sensitivity to discount rates — again producing effective combined risk
well above what five "independent" 1% positions would suggest.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
During the 2008 Global Financial Crisis (`[31.01]` previewed),
correlations across asset classes and individual stocks that had
historically been relatively uncorrelated rose sharply toward 1 —
a phenomenon researchers and practitioners commonly describe as
"correlation goes to one during a crisis," directly validating this
lesson's warning that correlation risk is often understated precisely
when it matters most.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional risk systems formally calculate portfolio-level Value-at-
Risk (VaR) incorporating the full correlation matrix across all
holdings, explicitly capturing the cluster-level risk this lesson
describes qualitatively — a more rigorous, quantitative version of the
same underlying principle.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders frequently believe they are diversified simply because
they hold multiple different stock names, without checking whether
those names share a common sector, macro driver, or geography — this
lesson's clustering exercise is a practical, accessible way to catch
this common blind spot without requiring institutional-grade risk software.

## Professional Insights
Professional portfolio managers specifically stress-test their book
against historical crisis periods (like 2008 or March 2020) precisely
because normal-period correlation estimates can understate how
correlated positions will actually behave during genuine stress —
consistent with `[23.05]`'s volatility regime discussion applied at the
cross-asset correlation level.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating "different stock names" as equivalent to "diversified risk"
  without checking underlying sector/macro correlation.
- Sizing each position independently per `[25.02]` without also
  checking aggregate cluster-level risk.
- Assuming historically low correlation between positions will persist
  during a genuine market stress event.

## Misconceptions
- **"Holding many different stocks automatically means diversified
  risk."** Diversification requires genuinely low correlation between
  holdings, not merely different company names.
- **"Correlation between positions stays constant over time."** It can
  and does shift, particularly rising sharply toward 1 during genuine
  crisis periods.
- **"Per-position risk limits (`[25.02]`) alone are sufficient risk
  management."** They must be supplemented with cluster/portfolio-level
  risk awareness, as this lesson demonstrates.

## Key Takeaways
- Correlation risk means multiple individually-sized positions can
  produce combined losses far exceeding any single position's risk
  limit if they move together.
- Group positions by likely correlation cluster (sector, macro driver,
  geography) and cap total risk per cluster, not just per position.
- Correlation tends to rise sharply during genuine market stress ("goes
  to one"), compounding drawdown risk exactly when least expected.
- Institutional risk systems formalize this via portfolio-level VaR;
  retail traders can approximate it through disciplined manual clustering.

## Summary
Correlation risk means that individually well-sized positions (per
`[25.02]`'s risk-per-trade rule) can still produce dangerously large
combined losses if they move together during an adverse event — a risk
that becomes especially acute during genuine market stress, when
historically low correlations tend to rise sharply toward one, exactly
as observed during 2008. The final substantive lesson in this module
turns to black swan events and tail risk — the extreme, low-probability
scenarios where correlation risk, drawdown, and stop-loss assumptions
can all break down simultaneously.
