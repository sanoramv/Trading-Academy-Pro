# [25.02] Defining Risk Per Trade (1% Rule and Variants)

> Module: Risk Management · Lesson 2 of 8 · Estimated time: 30 minutes

## Learning Objectives
- Define "risk per trade" precisely as the amount *intended* to be lost if the stop-loss is hit (subject to slippage), not the full position value.
- Apply the 1% rule and understand its common variants (0.5%, 2%).
- Calculate position size from account risk, entry price, and stop-loss distance.
- Understand why risk per trade must scale with account size and strategy characteristics, not stay fixed in currency terms.

## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Risk per trade** is the amount of capital *intended* to be lost if a
position's stop-loss is hit — not the total value of the position itself.
Note that in fast-moving or gapping markets, the stop order may execute
at a worse price than planned (slippage), so actual loss can exceed this
calculated figure; this is a key reason many traders build in a buffer or
use guaranteed stop orders where available (see Common Mistakes below).
The **1% rule** is the most widely referenced risk-per-trade
guideline: risking no more than 1% of total trading capital on any
single trade's stop-loss distance. Variants exist for different risk
tolerances and strategy characteristics — conservative traders or those
running strategies with higher win-rate variance may use 0.5%, while
more aggressive traders with smaller accounts or higher-conviction
systems sometimes use up to 2%, though professional guidance rarely
recommends materially exceeding this range for any single trade.

### Intuition
A ₹10 lakh position isn't necessarily "risking ₹10 lakh" — if the stop-
loss sits 2% below entry, the actual capital at risk is 2% of the
position value, not the full amount. Risk per trade calculations focus
specifically on this stop-loss-defined amount, which is what determines
appropriate position *size* — a tighter stop allows a larger position
for the same fixed percentage of account risked, and a wider stop
requires a smaller position for the same risk.

### Mental Model
```
POSITION SIZE FORMULA (from fixed risk per trade):

  Position Size (units/shares) = (Account Risk in Currency)
                                  / (Entry Price - Stop-Loss Price)

  Example (1% rule):
    Account Size = Rs 10,00,000
    Risk Per Trade (1%) = Rs 10,000
    Entry Price = Rs 500
    Stop-Loss Price = Rs 480 (Rs 20 stop distance)

    Position Size = Rs 10,000 / Rs 20 = 500 shares
    (Position VALUE = 500 x Rs 500 = Rs 2,50,000 -- note this is
     MUCH LARGER than the Rs 10,000 actually AT RISK)

  KEY INSIGHT: Risk per trade (currency at risk if stopped out) is
  DIFFERENT from position value (total capital deployed) -- the 1%
  rule governs the FORMER, not the latter.
```

### Visual Explanation
Two traders with identical account sizes and identical 1% risk rules
can hold very different position *sizes* depending on their stop-loss
distance — a trader using a tight 2% stop can hold a much larger
position (in currency terms) than a trader using a wide 10% stop, while
both risk exactly the same 1% of account capital if stopped out.

### ASCII Diagram
```
  SAME 1% RISK, DIFFERENT STOP DISTANCE -> DIFFERENT POSITION SIZE

  Account = Rs 10,00,000. Risk per trade (1%) = Rs 10,000 (FIXED)

  TIGHT STOP (2% stop distance)        WIDE STOP (10% stop distance)
  Position Value ~ Rs 5,00,000          Position Value ~ Rs 1,00,000
  (LARGER position, tighter risk band)  (SMALLER position, wider risk band)
  ------|##################|            ------|####|
        Rs 10,000 at risk                     Rs 10,000 at risk
        (SAME in both cases)                  (SAME in both cases)
```

### Flowchart
```
[Determine account risk tolerance -- e.g., 1% of total capital]
        |
        v
[Identify entry price AND stop-loss price for this specific trade,
 [25.03] covers HOW to place the stop]
        |
        v
[Calculate stop distance = Entry Price - Stop-Loss Price]
        |
        v
[Position Size = (Account Risk in Rs) / (Stop Distance in Rs)]
        |
        v
[Confirm resulting POSITION VALUE is within available capital/margin
 -- if not, the stop is too tight for this account size, adjust]
        |
        v
[Execute at the calculated size -- risk per trade stays CONSTANT
 across every trade, regardless of conviction level]
```

## Real World Analogy
Defining risk per trade is like a casino setting a fixed maximum bet
size relative to its total bankroll for any single hand, regardless of
how favorable the odds appear on that particular hand — the discipline
protects the bankroll's ability to keep playing across thousands of
hands, exactly mirroring `[25.01]`'s survival-first logic applied at
the individual-trade level.

## Indian Market Example
A trader with a ₹5,00,000 account applies the 1% rule (₹5,000 risk per
trade) to a Nifty Bank stock trade: entry at ₹1,200, stop-loss at
₹1,176 (a ₹24 stop distance) — yielding a position size of ₹5,000 / ₹24
≈ 208 shares, for a total position value of roughly ₹2,49,600.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader with a $50,000 account applies a more conservative 0.5% rule
($250 risk per trade) to an SPY trade: entry at $450, stop-loss at
$445 (a $5 stop distance) — yielding a position size of $250 / $5 = 50
shares, for a total position value of $22,500. **Hypothetical Example**
for illustration only.

## Historical Example
The 1% rule's popularization in retail trading education is widely
attributed to trading psychology and money management writers of the
1990s–2000s, including Alexander Elder (`[24.06]`, `[24.12]`) and Van
Tharp (`[24.01]`, `[25.01]`), both of whom emphasized fixed, small,
consistent risk-per-trade sizing as foundational to long-term survival
— now a near-universal starting reference point across retail trading
education globally.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional risk limits are typically expressed in more sophisticated
terms than a flat percentage — often incorporating Value-at-Risk (VaR)
models and correlation-adjusted position limits (`[25.06]` previewed) —
but the underlying principle of capping potential loss per position
relative to total capital remains conceptually identical to the retail
1% rule.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders frequently violate this principle by conflating position
*value* with risk — believing a ₹50,000 position is "only" 5% of a
₹10,00,000 account when the actual risk (if a 10% stop is used) is
closer to 0.5% of the account, or conversely underestimating risk on a
highly leveraged futures/options position where the true risk-per-trade
can far exceed intended limits without careful calculation.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders emphasize that risk per trade should scale with
strategy characteristics, not stay fixed at 1% universally — a strategy
with well-documented, low variance in outcomes might reasonably use a
slightly higher risk-per-trade, while a strategy with higher win-rate
variance (`[24.09]`'s theta-selling profile) often warrants a more
conservative figure.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating the calculated risk-per-trade figure as a guaranteed maximum
  loss. In fast-moving or gapping markets (routine for NSE/BSE overnight
  sessions, `[24.05]`), a stop order can execute at a worse price than
  the stop level, so actual loss can exceed the planned amount — build in
  a buffer or use guaranteed stop orders where available.
- Confusing position value with risk per trade — these are only equal
  if the stop-loss is 100% of the position (never appropriate).
- Using a fixed currency amount per trade regardless of account size
  changes, rather than a consistent percentage that scales appropriately.
- Increasing risk per trade after a string of wins ("I can afford it
  now") rather than maintaining consistent discipline, connecting
  directly to Module 28's psychology discussion.

## Misconceptions
- **"The 1% rule means only using 1% of account capital per trade."**
  It means risking (potentially losing) 1% if stopped out — the actual
  position value/capital deployed is typically much larger, as this
  lesson's worked examples show.
- **"A higher risk-per-trade percentage always means higher returns."**
  Higher risk-per-trade increases variance and drawdown severity
  (`[25.01]`) without a guaranteed corresponding increase in expectancy.
- **"Risk per trade should stay fixed at 1% for every strategy and
  trader."** It reasonably varies with account size, strategy variance
  characteristics, and individual risk tolerance, within a generally
  conservative range.

## Key Takeaways
- Risk per trade = capital *intended* to be lost if the stop-loss is
  hit, distinct from total position value — actual loss can exceed this
  if the stop suffers slippage in a fast or gapping market.
- Position Size = Account Risk (currency) ÷ Stop Distance (currency) —
  the core sizing formula.
- The 1% rule is the most common reference point; variants (0.5%–2%)
  adjust for risk tolerance and strategy characteristics.
- A tighter stop allows a larger position for the same fixed risk
  percentage; a wider stop requires a smaller position.

## Summary
Defining risk per trade — the capital actually lost if a stop-loss is
hit, calculated via Position Size = Account Risk ÷ Stop Distance —
operationalizes `[25.01]`'s survival-first principle at the individual-
trade level, with the widely referenced 1% rule (and its 0.5%–2%
variants) providing a starting discipline nearly universal across
retail trading education. The next lesson turns to stop-loss placement
methodology itself — determining exactly where that critical stop
distance should sit.

## Quiz
1. What does "risk per trade" refer to?
   a) Total position value  b) Capital lost if the stop-loss is hit  c) Account balance  d) Brokerage fees

2. What is the position sizing formula?
   a) Account Risk × Stop Distance  b) Account Risk ÷ Stop Distance  c) Stop Distance ÷ Account Risk  d) Entry Price × Account Risk

3. What is the most commonly referenced risk-per-trade percentage?
   a) 10%  b) 1%  c) 50%  d) 0.01%

4. For the same fixed risk percentage, does a tighter stop allow a larger or smaller position?
   a) Smaller  b) Larger  c) No effect  d) Position size is unrelated to stop distance

5. True or False: Risking 1% of capital per trade means only 1% of capital is deployed in the position.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Capital lost if the stop-loss is hit.
2. **b)** — Account Risk ÷ Stop Distance.
3. **b)** — The 1% rule.
4. **b)** — Larger, since less risk is taken per unit, allowing more units for the same total risk.
5. **b) False** — Position value (capital deployed) is typically much larger than the 1% actually at risk.
