## Core Concepts

### Definition
The **law of demand** states that, all else equal, as a good's price
rises, the quantity demanded falls (and vice versa) — buyers want more of
something when it's cheaper. The **law of supply** states that, all else
equal, as a good's price rises, the quantity supplied increases (and vice
versa) — sellers are willing to offer more when compensated more.
**Equilibrium** is the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity
supplied — the price where the market "clears," with no persistent
surplus or shortage.

### Intuition
This is precisely the same logic underlying the order book concept from
`[01.04]`: buyers place bids at prices they're willing to pay (demand),
sellers place asks at prices they're willing to accept (supply), and the
market price is where these two forces meet. Demand and supply are not
abstract economic theory disconnected from trading — they are the
first-principles explanation for *why* the order book and price discovery
mechanism work the way they do.

### Mental Model
```
PRICE
  ^
  |     SUPPLY curve (upward sloping:
  |    /  higher price --> more sellers willing to sell)
  |   /
  |  X <-- EQUILIBRIUM (quantity demanded = quantity supplied)
  | / \
  |/   \  DEMAND curve (downward sloping:
  |     \  higher price --> fewer buyers willing to buy)
  +------------------------------> QUANTITY
```

### Visual Explanation
When demand or supply shifts (not just moves along the existing curve, but
the entire curve moves), the equilibrium price and quantity both change —
this distinction between a "shift" and a "movement along" the curve is a
common source of confusion worth getting right.

### ASCII Diagram
```
DEMAND SHIFT EXAMPLE (positive news increases demand for a stock):

  PRICE                          PRICE
    ^                              ^
    |    S                         |    S
    |   /                          |   /
    |  X (old equilibrium)         |  X'(new equilibrium,
    | / \                          | /|\  higher price AND
    |/   \  D (old demand)         |/ | \  higher quantity)
    +------> QUANTITY              |  D' (new, shifted demand,
                                    +--+---> QUANTITY   further right)


SUPPLY SHIFT EXAMPLE (a supply disruption reduces available quantity):

  PRICE                          PRICE
    ^                              ^
    |    S (old supply)            |  S' (new, shifted supply,
    |   /                          | /   further left --
    |  X (old equilibrium)         |/ X' (new equilibrium,
    | / \                          |  |\  higher price, lower
    |/   \  D                      |  | \  quantity)
    +------> QUANTITY              +--+--> QUANTITY
```

### Flowchart
```
[Something changes in the market -- news, policy, external event]
        |
        v
[Does it change how much buyers WANT at each price (demand shift),
 or how much sellers are WILLING/ABLE to offer at each price
 (supply shift), or both?]
        |
        v
[New equilibrium price and quantity form where the (possibly
 shifted) demand and supply curves now intersect]
```

## Real World Analogy
Demand and supply are like two teams pulling a rope in a tug-of-war, where
the rope's resting position (price) reflects the balance of pulling force
(demand) from one side against the resisting force (supply) from the
other. If one team suddenly gets more people (a demand or supply shift),
the rope's resting position moves toward a new equilibrium reflecting the
new balance of forces — not randomly, but predictably based on which side
gained strength.

## Indian Market Example
When **positive earnings surprises** are reported for a widely-held Indian
stock like Infosys, buyer demand for the stock typically increases (more
buyers willing to pay higher prices given improved expectations), shifting
the demand curve rightward and producing a new, higher equilibrium price —
the exact mechanism underlying the sharp, often immediate price reactions
seen around Indian corporate earnings announcements.

## US Market Example
During periods of severe **semiconductor chip shortages** (as seen
notably during 2021–2022), reduced supply of critical components affected
numerous US companies' production capacity, shifting the supply curve for
their finished products leftward (less available at any given price) —
directly illustrating a real-world supply shock's effect on pricing and,
by extension, on the affected companies' stock valuations as investors
priced in reduced near-term output and profitability.

## Historical Example
The **2020 COVID-19 pandemic** produced a textbook dual demand-and-supply
shock simultaneously: demand for certain goods (home office equipment,
essential goods) surged while demand for others (travel, in-person
services) collapsed, while supply chains globally were disrupted
(factory shutdowns, shipping bottlenecks) — a historically unusual,
simultaneous demand-and-supply-shock event that produced highly divergent
sector-specific price and stock performance, some of the sharpest
demand/supply-driven repricing in recent economic history.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional commodity and macro trading desks build detailed **supply
and demand balance models** for specific goods (oil, agricultural
commodities, industrial metals) to forecast likely price direction —
tracking production data, inventory levels, and consumption trends as
direct, quantifiable inputs to the demand/supply framework taught in this
lesson, applied at a sophisticated, data-driven level.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders often intuitively grasp "more buyers than sellers pushes
price up" without connecting this everyday intuition to the formal
demand/supply framework — recognizing that this framework is the
underlying economic logic explaining that intuition helps build a more
rigorous, transferable mental model applicable well beyond stocks, to
commodities, currencies, and any priced good or asset.

## Professional Insights
Professional economists and analysts distinguish carefully between a
**"movement along" a demand/supply curve** (caused purely by a price
change itself, with the underlying curve unchanged) and a **"shift" of the
entire curve** (caused by some external factor changing buyers' or
sellers' underlying willingness/ability at every price level) — conflating
these two distinct concepts is one of the most common analytical errors in
introductory economic reasoning, worth avoiding carefully.

## Common Mistakes
- Confusing a shift of the demand/supply curve with a simple movement along
  an unchanged curve.
- Assuming demand/supply analysis only applies to physical commodities, not
  realizing it's the same underlying logic driving stock, currency, and
  every other priced-asset market.
- Ignoring that both demand AND supply can shift simultaneously (as in the
  2020 COVID example), producing more complex combined effects than a
  single-curve-shift analysis alone would suggest.

## Misconceptions
- **"Demand and supply only apply to goods you can physically touch, like
  vegetables or oil."** The framework applies identically to any priced
  asset, including stocks, bonds, currencies, and derivatives — the order
  book mechanics from `[01.04]` are a direct, real-time application of
  demand and supply.
- **"Prices always immediately and perfectly reach equilibrium."** Real
  markets often show frictions, delays, and temporary imbalances
  (shortages, surpluses) before fully adjusting — equilibrium is a useful
  theoretical reference point, not always an instantaneous real-world
  outcome.
- **"A price increase always means demand increased."** A price increase
  could equally result from a supply *decrease* (with demand unchanged) —
  distinguishing which curve moved (or both) requires additional context
  beyond the price change alone.

## Key Takeaways
- The law of demand: higher price, lower quantity demanded (all else
  equal). The law of supply: higher price, higher quantity supplied.
- Equilibrium is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied — the
  market-clearing price.
- A "shift" of an entire curve (caused by external factors) is distinct
  from a "movement along" an unchanged curve (caused purely by price
  itself).
- This framework is the same underlying logic driving order book price
  discovery (`[01.04]`) in every financial market.

## Summary
Demand and supply — the most foundational framework in all of economics —
directly explains the order book mechanics already covered in `[01.04]`:
buyers' willingness to pay (demand) meeting sellers' willingness to accept
(supply) determines the market-clearing equilibrium price. Understanding
how external events shift these curves (versus simply moving along an
existing curve) is essential groundwork for the next lesson's focus on
inflation — itself fundamentally a demand/supply phenomenon at the
economy-wide level.

## Revision Notes
- Law of demand: price up, quantity demanded down (inverse relationship).
- Law of supply: price up, quantity supplied up (direct relationship).
- Equilibrium: where demand and supply curves intersect — quantity
  demanded = quantity supplied.
- "Shift" (curve moves due to external factor) ≠ "movement along" (curve
  unchanged, price/quantity move along it).
- 2020 COVID: rare example of simultaneous demand AND supply shocks.

## Quiz
1. What does the law of demand state?
   a) Higher price leads to higher quantity demanded
   b) Higher price leads to lower quantity demanded, all else equal
   c) Price has no effect on quantity demanded
   d) Demand only applies to physical goods

2. What is market equilibrium?
   a) The highest possible price
   b) The price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied
   c) The lowest possible price
   d) A price set by the government

3. What causes a "shift" of the demand curve, as opposed to a movement
   along it?
   a) A pure price change alone
   b) An external factor changing buyers' willingness/ability at every price level
   c) Nothing can shift a demand curve
   d) Only supply curves can shift

4. What made the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic an unusual economic event?
   a) It only affected supply, never demand
   b) It produced simultaneous demand and supply shocks across different sectors
   c) It had no economic effect at all
   d) It only affected India

5. True or False: A price increase always means demand increased, never
   that supply decreased.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — The law of demand states an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.
2. **b)** — Equilibrium is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
3. **b)** — A shift is caused by external factors, distinct from movement along an unchanged curve due to price alone.
4. **b)** — COVID-19 produced simultaneous demand and supply shocks, unusual in economic history.
5. **b) False** — A price increase could result from a demand increase, a supply decrease, or both.
