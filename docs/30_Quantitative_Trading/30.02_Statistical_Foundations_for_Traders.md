## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Mean** is the average value of a dataset (e.g., average daily
return). **Variance** measures how spread out values are around the
mean — the average of squared deviations from the mean. **Standard
deviation** is variance's square root, expressed in the same units as
the original data, making it more directly interpretable (this is
precisely what `[23.01]`'s volatility figure represents). The **normal
distribution** (bell curve) is a specific, mathematically convenient
probability distribution widely used to model returns, fully described
by just its mean and standard deviation — but as `[25.07]`
established, real market returns exhibit **fat tails**: more extreme
outcomes than a normal distribution predicts. **Skewness** measures a
distribution's asymmetry (whether extreme outcomes cluster more on one
side), and **kurtosis** measures how "fat" the tails are relative to a
normal distribution — both statistics that quantify exactly how real
market returns deviate from the normal distribution's simplifying assumption.

### Intuition
Mean and variance/standard deviation are the two most basic statistics
summarizing a dataset, but they only fully describe a *normal*
distribution — real market returns require additional statistics
(skewness, kurtosis) to capture how they actually deviate from that
convenient but imperfect assumption. Understanding these deviations is
not merely academic: it directly explains why `[25.07]`'s black swan
events occur more often than naive, normal-distribution-based risk
models predict.

### Mental Model
```
CORE STATISTICS:

  MEAN (average): sum of all values / number of values

  VARIANCE: average of SQUARED deviations from the mean
    (squaring ensures positive/negative deviations don't cancel out)

  STANDARD DEVIATION: SQUARE ROOT of variance -- same UNITS as
    the original data (this IS "volatility," [23.01])

DISTRIBUTION SHAPE BEYOND MEAN/VARIANCE:

  SKEWNESS: measures ASYMMETRY
    Positive skew -> longer/fatter tail on the RIGHT (rare large
                      GAINS more extreme than rare large losses)
    Negative skew -> longer/fatter tail on the LEFT (rare large
                      LOSSES more extreme than rare large gains --
                      common pattern for equity index returns)

  KURTOSIS: measures TAIL FATNESS relative to normal distribution
    Normal distribution: kurtosis = 3 ("mesokurtic")
    Kurtosis > 3 ("leptokurtic", FAT TAILS): MORE extreme outcomes
      than normal distribution predicts -- [25.07]'s core warning
```

### Visual Explanation
Plotting a histogram of real daily equity index returns against an
overlaid normal distribution curve with the same mean and standard
deviation typically shows the real data has a taller, narrower peak
(more days clustered very close to zero) AND fatter tails (more extreme
days) than the smooth normal curve predicts — visually demonstrating
both excess kurtosis and often negative skew simultaneously.

### Worked Example
**Hypothetical Example.** The Mental Model above defines mean, variance,
and standard deviation in words — here is the actual arithmetic, done by
hand on 5 daily returns, so the homework doesn't ask you to calculate
something you haven't already seen worked through once.

```
ASSUMPTIONS -- 5 daily returns (%) for a hypothetical strategy:
  Day 1: +2%   Day 2: -1%   Day 3: +3%   Day 4: 0%   Day 5: -1%

STEP 1 -- Mean (average)
  Mean = (2 - 1 + 3 + 0 - 1) / 5 = 3 / 5 = 0.6%

STEP 2 -- Deviation of each value from the mean, then squared
  Day 1:  2 - 0.6 =  1.4   ->  1.4^2 = 1.96
  Day 2: -1 - 0.6 = -1.6   -> -1.6^2 = 2.56
  Day 3:  3 - 0.6 =  2.4   ->  2.4^2 = 5.76
  Day 4:  0 - 0.6 = -0.6   -> -0.6^2 = 0.36
  Day 5: -1 - 0.6 = -1.6   -> -1.6^2 = 2.56
  Sum of squared deviations = 13.20

STEP 3 -- Variance = average of the squared deviations
  Variance = 13.20 / 5 = 2.64 (in percent-squared units)

STEP 4 -- Standard deviation = square root of variance
  Standard Deviation = sqrt(2.64) = 1.62%
```

That 1.62% is the same quantity `[23.01]` calls "historical volatility"
— this lesson and that one are computing the identical statistic, just
naming it in two different contexts (general statistical foundation here,
option-pricing input there). Notice Day 3 (+3%, the furthest value from
the mean) contributed the single largest term to the variance sum (5.76,
nearly half the total) — this is exactly why standard deviation is
sensitive to outliers, a property worth remembering when a strategy's
track record includes one unusually large day.

## Real World Analogy
Relying on mean and standard deviation alone to describe returns is
like describing a mountain range using only its average elevation and
how much elevation varies typically — useful summary information, but
missing whether the range has one dramatically taller peak sticking out
asymmetrically (skewness) or unusually sudden, sharp cliffs beyond what
the "typical variation" figure would suggest (kurtosis) — details that
matter enormously to a climber assessing real risk.

## Indian Market Example
Analyzing Nifty's daily returns over a multi-year period typically
reveals negative skewness (sharp, large single-day declines like the
March 2020 COVID crash are more extreme than the largest single-day
gains) and excess kurtosis (more days with unusually large moves, in
either direction, than a normal distribution assumption would predict)
— statistical confirmation of `[25.07]`'s qualitative fat-tails warning
using Nifty's own actual historical data. **Hypothetical/illustrative characterization.**

## US Market Example
Academic studies of S&P 500 daily returns over long historical periods
consistently document both negative skewness (crashes are typically
sharper than rallies) and significant excess kurtosis — a well-
established empirical finding in financial econometrics research,
directly underlying why risk models built purely on normal-distribution
assumptions have historically underestimated genuine tail risk.

## Historical Example
The mathematical groundwork for measuring distribution shape beyond
mean and variance — skewness and kurtosis — traces to statistician
Karl Pearson's work in the late 19th century, later becoming standard
tools in financial econometrics from the mid-20th century onward as
researchers increasingly documented that financial returns
systematically violate the normal distribution assumption underlying
much of classical portfolio theory (`[27.03]`'s MPT).

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional risk models increasingly incorporate skewness and
kurtosis directly, or use entirely different distributional assumptions
(such as Student's t-distributions or extreme value theory) specifically
designed to better capture fat-tailed behavior than the classical normal
distribution assumption, addressing `[25.07]`'s black swan concern with more rigorous statistical tools.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders benefit from understanding that any risk figure derived
purely from mean and standard deviation (a common simplification in
many accessible tools) likely understates genuine tail risk for equity
and equity-derivative positions — a qualitative awareness that doesn't
require personally calculating skewness/kurtosis, but should inform
appropriately conservative position sizing (Module 26) regardless.

## Professional Insights
Professional quant researchers often examine a strategy's return
distribution's skewness specifically when evaluating risk — a strategy
with negative skew (frequent small gains, occasional large losses, like
`[24.09]`'s theta-selling profile) carries meaningfully different tail
risk than a strategy with positive skew (occasional large gains,
frequent small losses, like `[24.02]`'s trend-following profile), even
if both show identical mean return and standard deviation.

## Common Mistakes
- Relying solely on mean and standard deviation to fully describe a
  strategy's risk, ignoring skewness and kurtosis.
- Assuming financial returns follow a normal distribution without
  checking for the fat tails and skew well-documented in academic research.
- Confusing a strategy's win rate with its distribution shape — a
  high-win-rate strategy can still have significant negative skew
  (`[24.09]`'s theta-selling pattern).

## Misconceptions
- **"Mean and standard deviation fully describe a return
  distribution."** They fully describe a *normal* distribution only —
  real returns require skewness and kurtosis to capture their actual, non-normal shape.
- **"All financial return distributions look the same."** Different
  strategies exhibit meaningfully different skew (e.g., trend-following's
  positive skew versus theta-selling's negative skew) even with similar mean/variance.
- **"Fat tails are a rare, unusual finding."** Extensive academic
  research has documented fat tails and negative skew as persistent,
  well-established features of equity index returns broadly, not an isolated finding.

## Key Takeaways
- Mean and standard deviation summarize central tendency and spread —
  standard deviation IS what `[23.01]` called volatility.
- The normal distribution assumption, while mathematically convenient,
  is well-documented to underestimate real market tail risk.
- Skewness measures asymmetry; kurtosis measures tail fatness — both
  quantify how real returns deviate from the normal distribution assumption.
- Different strategies exhibit meaningfully different skew (trend-
  following positive, theta-selling negative) even with similar mean/variance.

## Summary
Mean, variance, and standard deviation provide the basic statistical
summary of returns — with standard deviation being exactly what
`[23.01]` called volatility — but skewness and kurtosis are needed to
capture how real market returns deviate from the mathematically
convenient normal distribution assumption, directly quantifying and
explaining `[25.07]`'s fat-tails warning with rigorous statistical
tools. The next lesson applies these foundations to correlation and
cointegration — the statistical relationships underlying pairs trading,
one of quant trading's classic strategies.
