## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Autocorrelation** measures the statistical relationship between a
time series' value at one point in time and its value at an earlier
point — in trading terms, whether today's return tends to predict
tomorrow's return direction. **Positive autocorrelation** means past
returns tend to be followed by returns in the *same* direction — the
statistical signature of **momentum** (`[24.02]`'s trend-following,
`[24.08]`'s momentum investing). **Negative autocorrelation** means past
returns tend to be followed by returns in the *opposite* direction —
the statistical signature of **mean reversion** (`[24.03]`'s framework).
Crucially, academic research has found that autocorrelation's sign and
strength vary significantly by **timescale** — the same instrument can
exhibit short-term mean reversion, medium-term momentum, and long-term
mean reversion simultaneously, depending on which time horizon is measured.

### Intuition
`[24.02]` and `[24.03]` presented trend-following and mean-reversion as
distinct strategy choices requiring different market regimes — this
lesson reveals the deeper statistical reality: both phenomena are
different manifestations of autocorrelation, and the *same* instrument
can exhibit both simultaneously at different timescales. A stock might
show negative autocorrelation over 1-5 day horizons (short-term mean
reversion, `[24.03]`'s territory) while simultaneously showing positive
autocorrelation over 3-12 month horizons (medium-term momentum,
Jegadeesh and Titman's `[24.08]` finding) — reconciling what might
otherwise look like contradictory academic findings.

### Mental Model
```
AUTOCORRELATION ACROSS TIMESCALES (documented academic pattern,
illustrative, NOT universal or guaranteed for every instrument):

  VERY SHORT-TERM (intraday/1-5 days):
    Often NEGATIVE autocorrelation -- MEAN REVERSION tendency
    (connects to [24.03]'s framework, [24.07] scalping timescale)

  MEDIUM-TERM (3-12 months):
    Often POSITIVE autocorrelation -- MOMENTUM tendency
    (Jegadeesh & Titman 1993, [24.08]'s momentum investing)

  LONG-TERM (3-5 years):
    Often NEGATIVE autocorrelation again -- LONG-TERM MEAN REVERSION
    (De Bondt & Thaler's "overreaction" research, 1985)

  KEY INSIGHT: These are documented HISTORICAL TENDENCIES across
  ACADEMIC STUDIES, not guaranteed, universal, or precisely timed
  patterns for any SPECIFIC instrument going forward
```

## Real World Analogy
Mean reversion and momentum coexisting at different timescales is like
a river that shows small, chaotic eddies swirling backward near any
given rock (short-term reversion) while the overall current
unmistakably flows downstream in one direction (medium-term momentum),
and yet the river's *course* across a continent bends and winds back
toward the sea through a circuitous, ultimately mean-reverting path
over the very long run (long-term reversion) — all three patterns
genuinely coexist, simply at different scales of observation.

## Indian Market Example
Empirical analysis of Nifty's return behavior across different
horizons might show short-term (1-5 day) mean-reversion tendencies
useful for a mean-reversion strategy (`[24.03]`) operating on that
timescale, while simultaneously showing medium-term (6-12 month)
momentum tendencies more relevant to a positional/momentum strategy
(`[24.08]`) operating on an entirely different timescale — both
patterns can be statistically genuine for the same underlying index. **Hypothetical/illustrative characterization.**

## US Market Example
Werner De Bondt and Richard Thaler's influential 1985 study "Does the
Stock Market Overreact?" found that US stocks with the worst 3-5 year
performance tended to subsequently outperform stocks with the best 3-5
year performance — a long-term mean-reversion finding that coexists,
without contradiction, alongside Jegadeesh and Titman's later
documented medium-term (3-12 month) momentum finding (`[24.08]`),
precisely because the two operate at different timescales.

## Historical Example
Richard Thaler's broader behavioral finance research program, including
the De Bondt-Thaler overreaction study, contributed significantly to
his 2017 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences — research directly
relevant to this lesson's long-term mean-reversion timescale and
complementary to (rather than contradicting) the medium-term momentum
findings this academy referenced in `[24.08]`.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional quant funds often run multiple strategies simultaneously
targeting *different* autocorrelation timescales on the *same*
underlying instruments — a short-term mean-reversion desk and a
medium-term momentum desk within the same firm aren't necessarily in
conflict, since both can be statistically valid, coexisting phenomena
operating at genuinely different time horizons.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders sometimes debate "does this market trend or mean-
revert?" as if it were a single, timescale-independent question —
this lesson's quant view reframes the question more precisely: at
*which specific timescale*, for *this specific instrument*, has
autocorrelation historically been positive or negative — a more
statistically grounded framing than a single, universal answer.

## Professional Insights
Professional quant researchers formally test for autocorrelation at
multiple timescales before deploying either a mean-reversion or
momentum strategy on a new instrument, recognizing that the
historical academic patterns described in this lesson's mental model
are general tendencies requiring instrument-specific statistical
verification, not universal laws applicable automatically to every
market or period.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating "does this trend or mean-revert" as a single,
  timescale-independent question rather than recognizing both can
  coexist at different horizons.
- Applying `[24.03]`'s mean-reversion logic at a timescale where
  momentum historically dominates, or vice versa.
- Assuming documented academic tendencies (short-term reversion,
  medium-term momentum, long-term reversion) apply universally without
  instrument-specific statistical verification.

## Misconceptions
- **"A market either trends or mean-reverts — it can't do both."**
  Academic research documents both phenomena coexisting for the same
  instrument at different timescales simultaneously.
- **"Momentum and mean-reversion research contradict each other."**
  Jegadeesh-Titman's momentum finding (`[24.08]`) and De Bondt-Thaler's
  long-term reversion finding operate at different timescales and are
  not in conflict.
- **"These documented tendencies apply identically to every instrument
  and period."** They are general, well-documented historical
  tendencies requiring specific statistical verification before being
  relied upon for any particular instrument or strategy.

## Key Takeaways
- Autocorrelation — the statistical relationship between past and
  future returns — underlies both momentum (positive autocorrelation)
  and mean reversion (negative autocorrelation).
- Documented academic tendency: short-term mean reversion, medium-term
  momentum, long-term mean reversion — different timescales, same instrument.
- De Bondt and Thaler's 1985 long-term overreaction study and Jegadeesh-
  Titman's medium-term momentum study are complementary, not
  contradictory, given their different timescales.
- Richard Thaler's broader behavioral finance work (including this
  research) contributed to his 2017 Nobel Memorial Prize.

## Summary
Autocorrelation reframes `[24.02]`'s trend-following and `[24.03]`'s
mean-reversion frameworks as two manifestations of the same underlying
statistical property — positive autocorrelation producing momentum,
negative producing mean reversion — with documented academic research
showing both can coexist for the same instrument across different
timescales: short-term reversion, medium-term momentum, and long-term
reversion again. The next lesson turns to factor investing — a
systematic framework built partly on momentum (one of several
documented factors) alongside value, quality, and low-volatility.

## Quiz
1. What does positive autocorrelation indicate?
   a) Mean reversion  b) Momentum — returns tend to continue in the same direction  c) No relationship  d) Random walk

2. What does negative autocorrelation indicate?
   a) Momentum  b) Mean reversion — returns tend to reverse direction  c) No relationship  d) Guaranteed profit

3. Can momentum and mean reversion coexist for the same instrument?
   a) No, never  b) Yes, at different timescales  c) Only for bonds  d) Only in India

4. Who conducted the influential 1985 study on long-term stock market overreaction?
   a) Jegadeesh and Titman  b) De Bondt and Thaler  c) Markowitz and Sharpe  d) Kelly and Thorp

5. True or False: Momentum research and mean-reversion research fundamentally contradict each other.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Momentum, returns tend to continue in the same direction.
2. **b)** — Mean reversion, returns tend to reverse direction.
3. **b)** — Yes, at different timescales.
4. **b)** — De Bondt and Thaler.
5. **b) False** — They operate at different timescales and are complementary, not contradictory.
