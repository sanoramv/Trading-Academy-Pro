## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Market making** is the practice of continuously quoting both a bid
(buy) price and an ask (offer/sell) price for an instrument, standing
ready to trade with any counterparty at those quoted prices, and
profiting primarily from the **bid-ask spread** — the small difference
between the two quoted prices — captured repeatedly across enormous
trading volume. Market makers take on **inventory risk**: the risk that
their accumulated position (from being hit on one side of their quotes
more than the other) moves against them before it can be offloaded or
hedged. Many formal market makers hold official designations from
exchanges (like NSE's or BSE's market maker schemes for certain
instruments), carrying specific obligations (continuous quoting, maximum
spread limits) in exchange for certain benefits (reduced fees, priority access).

### Intuition
A market maker doesn't bet on direction — they profit from providing a
continuous service (immediate buy/sell availability) that other market
participants value, charging a small fee for that service embedded in
the bid-ask spread, repeated across massive volume. This connects
directly to `[21.06]`'s short option positions (profiting from Theta/
spread rather than direction) and `[29.08]`'s HFT market making
(the same underlying business model, executed at extreme speed and scale).

### Mental Model
```
MARKET MAKING MECHANICS:

  Continuously quote: BID (e.g., buy at Rs 100.00) and
                       ASK (e.g., sell at Rs 100.05)
  SPREAD = Ask - Bid = Rs 0.05 (the market maker's PROFIT MARGIN
           per round-trip trade, captured REPEATEDLY across volume)

INVENTORY RISK:
  If MORE participants SELL to the market maker than BUY from them,
  the market maker accumulates a LONG inventory position -- exposed
  to the underlying's price movement, NOT just spread capture

  Market makers HEDGE this inventory risk using:
    - Offsetting positions in RELATED instruments (correlation, [25.06])
    - Options Greeks-based hedging, [22.07] (Delta-neutral positioning)
    - Rapid position adjustment as inventory builds

REVENUE FORMULA (simplified):
  Market Maker Profit ~= (Spread Captured x Trading Volume)
                          - (Inventory Risk Losses, if hedging
                             is imperfect or a large adverse move occurs)
```

### Visual Explanation
A market maker's quote screen shows two prices updating continuously
throughout the trading session — the bid and ask — narrowing during
calm, liquid conditions (competitive market making, tight spreads) and
widening during volatile or uncertain conditions (market makers demand
more compensation for the increased inventory risk they're accepting).

## Real World Analogy
Market making is like a currency exchange counter at an airport:
the counter continuously quotes both a buy and sell rate for foreign
currency, profiting from the small spread between the two rates on
every transaction, while managing the risk that holding too much of
one currency (inventory) exposes them to unfavorable exchange rate
movements before they can rebalance their holdings.

## Indian Market Example
NSE's market maker scheme for certain less-liquid securities and
options series formally designates registered market makers who commit
to continuous, tight-spread quoting in exchange for specific exchange
benefits — directly connecting to `[21.11]`'s option chain discussion,
since the bid/ask prices visible on any Nifty option chain are, at
least partly, generated by such formal or informal market-making
activity.

## US Market Example
US options market makers on exchanges like CBOE continuously quote
both sides of thousands of option series simultaneously, using
sophisticated real-time Greeks-based hedging (`[22.07]`) to manage the
inventory risk that accumulates as their quotes are hit asymmetrically
by directional order flow throughout the trading session.

## Historical Example
Market making has existed in some form since organized exchanges
began, but its modern, highly automated, quant-driven form developed
significantly from the 1990s-2000s onward alongside electronic trading
infrastructure — firms like Citadel Securities and Jane Street (founded
1999 and 2000 respectively) grew into major global market-making
operations spanning equities, options, and other asset classes using
exactly this lesson's spread-capture-plus-hedging business model at
institutional scale.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional market makers view inventory risk management as their
core operational discipline — sophisticated real-time risk systems
continuously monitor aggregate exposure across thousands of
simultaneously quoted instruments, automatically widening spreads or
reducing quote sizes when inventory risk or market volatility (`[23.05]`) rises.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders indirectly benefit from market maker activity through
tighter bid-ask spreads on liquid instruments — understanding market
making helps retail traders interpret why spreads widen during
volatile periods (`[23.05]`'s connection) and why highly liquid
instruments typically offer better execution prices than illiquid ones.

## Professional Insights
Professional market makers emphasize that pure spread capture alone is
rarely sufficient for profitability at scale — sophisticated inventory
management, informed order flow detection (distinguishing "informed"
traders likely to be right about direction from "noise" traders), and
Greeks-based hedging discipline (`[22.07]`) are all essential
complements to the basic spread-capture business model.

## Common Mistakes
- Assuming market making is a simple, risk-free way to profit from the
  bid-ask spread, without accounting for genuine inventory risk.
- Confusing formal, exchange-designated market makers with informal
  liquidity providers, which carry different obligations and benefits.
- Underestimating how quickly inventory risk can compound during rapid,
  one-directional market moves.

## Misconceptions
- **"Market making is risk-free profit from the bid-ask spread."**
  Inventory risk is genuine and can produce significant losses,
  particularly during rapid, one-directional moves, if hedging is imperfect.
- **"All market makers are formally registered with exchanges."** Many
  participants provide liquidity informally without a formal exchange
  designation, alongside formally registered market makers with specific obligations.
- **"Market making and directional trading are the same activity."**
  Market making profits primarily from spread capture and volume,
  managing inventory risk through hedging, rather than betting on
  directional price movement.

## Key Takeaways
- Market making profits from capturing the bid-ask spread repeatedly
  across high trading volume, not from directional bets.
- Inventory risk — accumulated exposure from asymmetric order flow — is
  the primary risk market makers manage, typically through Greeks-based
  or correlated-instrument hedging.
- Modern quant-driven market making developed significantly from the
  1990s-2000s onward, exemplified by firms like Citadel Securities and Jane Street.
- Spreads widen during volatile conditions as compensation for
  increased inventory risk — directly connecting to `[23.05]`'s
  volatility regime discussion.

## Summary
Market making profits from continuously capturing the bid-ask spread
across enormous trading volume, managing the genuine inventory risk
this creates through sophisticated hedging — the same underlying
business model as `[21.06]`'s short option positions, now applied
systematically and at scale by quant firms like Citadel Securities and
Jane Street, and directly connected to `[29.08]`'s HFT market-making
discussion. The next lesson turns to risk models and portfolio
optimization basics — the quantitative tools institutional desks use to
manage aggregate risk across their entire trading operation, including
market-making inventory.
