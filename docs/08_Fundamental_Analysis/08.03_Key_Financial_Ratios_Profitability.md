## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Profitability ratios** measure how efficiently a company converts its
resources into profit. **Return on Equity (ROE)** = Net Profit /
Shareholders' Equity — measures the return generated on shareholders'
invested capital. **Return on Assets (ROA)** = Net Profit / Total Assets —
measures how efficiently a company uses its total asset base to generate
profit, independent of how those assets are financed (debt vs. equity).
**Return on Capital Employed (ROCE)** = EBIT / (Total Assets − Current
Liabilities) — measures returns on the total long-term capital
(debt + equity) actually deployed in the business, often preferred for
comparing companies with different capital structures.

### Intuition
These ratios answer slightly different but related questions: ROE asks
"how well is management generating returns specifically for
shareholders?" ROA asks "how well is management using the total asset
base, regardless of financing?" ROCE asks "how well is management using
all the long-term capital employed in the business?" A company can show
a high ROE partly through heavy leverage (`[07.07]`) rather than genuinely
efficient operations — which is exactly why comparing ROE alongside ROA
and ROCE together provides a fuller, more honest picture than any single
ratio alone.

### Mental Model
```
ROE  = Net Profit / Shareholders' Equity
       (return specifically for SHAREHOLDERS)

ROA  = Net Profit / Total Assets
       (return on TOTAL ASSETS, regardless of financing)

ROCE = EBIT / (Total Assets - Current Liabilities)
       (return on LONG-TERM CAPITAL EMPLOYED, pre-tax/pre-interest,
        useful for comparing different capital structures)

DUPONT FRAMEWORK (decomposing ROE into 3 drivers):
ROE = Net Margin  x  Asset Turnover  x  Equity Multiplier
      (profitability)  (efficiency,     (leverage,
                         [08.05])         [07.07])
```

### Visual Explanation
The DuPont framework reveals *why* a company's ROE is high or low — a
high ROE driven primarily by heavy leverage (equity multiplier) carries
very different risk implications than a high ROE driven by genuinely
strong margins and efficient asset use.

### ASCII Diagram
```
   DUPONT DECOMPOSITION EXAMPLE (illustrative)

   Company A:                        Company B:
   Net Margin:        10%             Net Margin:        10%
   Asset Turnover:    1.0x            Asset Turnover:    1.0x
   Equity Multiplier: 1.5x            Equity Multiplier: 4.0x
   ------------------------          ------------------------
   ROE = 10% x 1.0 x 1.5 = 15%       ROE = 10% x 1.0 x 4.0 = 40%

   Both show identical margins and asset efficiency, but
   Company B's much higher ROE comes substantially from
   LEVERAGE (a 4.0x equity multiplier means assets are 4x
   equity -- heavily debt-financed), not necessarily superior
   underlying operations -- a critical distinction the DuPont
   framework reveals that ROE alone would hide.
```

## Real World Analogy
Comparing companies using ROE alone is like comparing two athletes' race
times without checking whether one used a shortcut — a runner who
"leverages" a shorter course (heavy debt financing) can post a faster time
(higher ROE) than a runner who ran the full course honestly (primarily
equity-financed) with genuinely superior fitness (operational efficiency)
— the DuPont framework is like reviewing the actual race course each
runner took, revealing whether the impressive time reflects genuine
superior performance or a structural shortcut.

## Indian Market Example
**Indian banking sector** companies typically show meaningfully different
ROE profiles depending on their leverage structure (a structural feature
of the banking business model itself, `[07.07]`) — comparing a
well-capitalized private bank's ROE against a more heavily-leveraged
public sector bank's ROE requires exactly this DuPont-style decomposition
to assess whether differences reflect genuine operational quality
differences or simply differing capital structure choices.

## US Market Example
Comparing **Apple's** historically very high ROE (in part supported by
significant share buybacks that reduce the equity base, mechanically
raising ROE even without proportional profit growth) against a company
with a similar net margin but no buyback program illustrates how
capital allocation decisions, not just operational performance, can
meaningfully shape reported ROE — a nuance the DuPont framework helps
surface.

## Historical Example
Financial institutions with extremely high pre-2008 ROE figures, later
revealed to be substantially driven by unsustainable leverage levels
(`[07.07]`, `[31.01]`) rather than genuinely superior underlying
profitability, illustrate historically why the DuPont decomposition —
distinguishing margin/efficiency-driven ROE from leverage-driven ROE — is
not merely an academic exercise but a genuinely important risk assessment
tool, directly connecting to this module's earlier lessons on capital
structure risk.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional equity analysts routinely present DuPont decomposition
explicitly in their research reports and financial models, since simply
reporting a headline ROE figure without this decomposition is considered
analytically incomplete — professional practice treats the decomposition
as a standard, expected part of profitability analysis, not an optional
advanced technique.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors very commonly compare companies' ROE figures directly
without considering the underlying leverage driving the difference —
building the habit of at least mentally checking a company's debt-to-
equity ratio (`[07.03]`) alongside its ROE meaningfully improves the
quality of profitability comparisons, even without performing the full
formal DuPont calculation.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts specifically favor **ROCE over ROE** when comparing
companies across different capital structures or industries, since ROCE's
use of EBIT (pre-interest) and total capital employed (debt + equity)
removes the leverage distortion that can make ROE comparisons misleading
across companies with meaningfully different financing choices.

## Common Mistakes
- Comparing ROE across companies without checking whether leverage
  differences are driving apparent performance gaps.
- Using ROE alone without considering ROA or ROCE for a fuller,
  cross-checked picture.
- Assuming a rising ROE is always a positive signal, without checking
  whether it's driven by improving operations or simply increasing
  leverage/share buybacks.

## Misconceptions
- **"Higher ROE always means a better-run, superior company."** As the
  DuPont framework and pre-2008 financial institution examples show,
  high ROE can be substantially leverage-driven rather than reflecting
  genuinely superior operations — always decompose before concluding
  superiority.
- **"ROE, ROA, and ROCE all measure essentially the same thing."** Each
  isolates a different aspect (shareholder returns, total asset
  efficiency, total capital efficiency) — using all three together, not
  just one, provides the most complete profitability picture.
- **"Share buybacks always indicate genuine improving profitability."**
  As Apple's example illustrates, buybacks can mechanically raise ROE by
  shrinking the equity base, independent of genuine operational
  improvement — worth distinguishing when assessing ROE trends.

## Key Takeaways
- ROE = Net Profit / Equity; ROA = Net Profit / Total Assets; ROCE =
  EBIT / (Total Assets − Current Liabilities) — each isolates a different
  profitability dimension.
- The DuPont framework decomposes ROE into Net Margin × Asset Turnover ×
  Equity Multiplier, revealing whether high ROE is margin/efficiency-
  driven or leverage-driven.
- ROCE is often preferred for cross-company/cross-industry comparison
  since it removes leverage distortion.
- Pre-2008 financial institutions' leverage-inflated ROE is a stark
  historical example of why this decomposition matters.

## Summary
Profitability ratios — ROE, ROA, and ROCE — together with the DuPont
framework's decomposition of ROE into margin, efficiency, and leverage
components, reveal not just *whether* a company is profitable but *why*,
distinguishing genuinely superior operational performance from leverage-
driven returns that carry meaningfully different risk implications, as
pre-2008 financial institutions starkly demonstrated. This sets up the
next lesson's focus on liquidity and solvency ratios — the risk-side
counterpart to this lesson's return-side profitability analysis.

## Quiz
1. How is Return on Equity (ROE) calculated?
   a) Revenue / Total Assets  b) Net Profit / Shareholders' Equity  c) EBIT / Interest Expense  d) Assets / Liabilities

2. What does the DuPont framework decompose ROE into?
   a) Revenue and Expenses only
   b) Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier
   c) Assets and Liabilities only
   d) Price and Volume

3. Why is ROCE often preferred for comparing companies across different
   capital structures?
   a) It ignores profitability entirely
   b) It uses EBIT and total capital employed, removing leverage distortion
   c) It's easier to calculate than ROE
   d) It only applies to Indian companies

4. What historical example illustrates the risk of leverage-inflated ROE?
   a) The 2021 Zomato IPO
   b) Pre-2008 financial institutions with leverage-driven high ROE
   c) The 1875 founding of BSE
   d) The 2023 T+1 settlement transition

5. True or False: A higher ROE always means a company is objectively
   better-run than a lower-ROE peer.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — ROE = Net Profit / Shareholders' Equity.
2. **b)** — DuPont decomposes ROE into Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier.
3. **b)** — ROCE removes leverage distortion by using EBIT and total capital employed.
4. **b)** — Pre-2008 financial institutions showed leverage-inflated ROE, later exposed as unsustainable.
5. **b) False** — High ROE can be leverage-driven rather than reflecting genuinely superior operations.
