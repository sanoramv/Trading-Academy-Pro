# [08.06] Earnings Per Share, P/E, and Growth Metrics

## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Earnings Per Share (EPS)** = Net Profit / Number of Outstanding
Shares — the portion of a company's profit attributable to each individual
share. **Diluted EPS** accounts for the potential dilution from
convertible securities, employee stock options, and similar instruments
that could increase the share count in the future — generally considered
more conservative and complete than basic EPS. The **Price-to-Earnings
(P/E) Ratio** = Share Price / EPS — expresses how much investors are
currently paying for each rupee/dollar of a company's annual earnings, the
single most widely-referenced valuation multiple in financial media. The
**PEG Ratio** = P/E Ratio / Earnings Growth Rate — adjusts the P/E ratio
for a company's growth rate, useful for comparing companies with different
growth profiles.

### Intuition
EPS translates total company profit into a per-share basis, letting
investors relate profitability directly to what they actually
own (individual shares) and to the price they pay. P/E then expresses
market sentiment about that earnings stream — a high P/E generally
reflects market expectations of strong future growth (investors willing
to pay more per rupee of *current* earnings because they expect much
higher *future* earnings), while a low P/E can reflect either genuine
undervaluation or legitimate concerns about future growth/risk.

### Mental Model
```
EPS = Net Profit / Shares Outstanding
  (profit attributable to EACH share)

P/E = Share Price / EPS
  (how much investors pay per rupee/dollar of CURRENT annual
   earnings -- reflects growth expectations, [09.02])

PEG = P/E / Earnings Growth Rate
  (adjusts P/E for growth -- a "high" P/E may be reasonable
   if growth is correspondingly high; PEG around 1.0 is
   sometimes used as a rough "fairly valued relative to
   growth" heuristic, though this is a simplification, not
   a rigid rule)
```

### Visual Explanation
Two companies with identical P/E ratios can have very different PEG
ratios if their growth rates differ substantially — PEG helps normalize
for this difference, though it remains an imperfect, simplified heuristic
rather than a complete valuation tool (full valuation methodology is
covered in Module 09).

### ASCII Diagram
```
   P/E vs. PEG COMPARISON (illustrative)

   Company A:                        Company B:
   P/E Ratio:          25x            P/E Ratio:          25x
   Earnings Growth:    10%/year       Earnings Growth:    25%/year
   ------------------------          ------------------------
   PEG = 25 / 10 = 2.5x               PEG = 25 / 25 = 1.0x

   Both show IDENTICAL P/E ratios, but Company B's much higher
   growth rate means its PEG suggests it may be more
   reasonably valued RELATIVE TO its growth than Company A,
   despite the identical headline P/E.
```

## Real World Analogy
P/E ratio is like the price-to-annual-rent multiple used to value a rental
property — paying 25 times the property's current annual rental income
(a high multiple) might be entirely reasonable if that rental income is
expected to grow rapidly in a booming neighborhood, but could be
excessive if the neighborhood's rental growth prospects are weak or
declining. The PEG ratio is like adjusting that property price-to-rent
multiple for the neighborhood's actual expected growth rate, giving a
more apples-to-apples comparison across properties in different growth
environments.

## Indian Market Example
Indian growth-oriented sectors like **information technology** and
**consumer discretionary** have historically traded at higher P/E
multiples than more mature, slower-growth sectors like traditional
manufacturing — reflecting market expectations of stronger future
earnings growth in the former, a pattern that makes direct P/E comparison
across these different sectors potentially misleading without growth-
adjusted context (PEG) or sector-relative comparison.

## US Market Example
US technology companies, particularly during periods of strong growth
expectations (such as much of the 2010s and early 2020s for major US tech
firms), have often traded at significantly higher P/E multiples than the
broader market average — a pattern extensively debated among analysts as
to whether such premiums are justified by genuinely superior growth
prospects (supporting a reasonable PEG) or reflect excessive market
optimism (an elevated PEG suggesting overvaluation relative to realistic
growth expectations).

## Historical Example
The **dot-com bubble (1999-2000)**, referenced in `[01.02]`, saw many
internet companies trade at extraordinarily high P/E ratios — in many
cases, companies had no profits at all, making P/E technically
uncalculable (a red flag in itself) — or traded at P/E multiples implying
growth expectations that proved wildly unrealistic in hindsight, a
historical episode widely cited as a cautionary example of P/E-based
valuation excess disconnected from any reasonable growth-adjusted (PEG)
justification.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional analysts rarely rely on P/E in isolation, instead comparing
a company's P/E against its own historical trading range, industry peers'
P/E ratios, and growth-adjusted metrics like PEG — treating P/E as one
input among several within a broader valuation framework (developed fully
in Module 09), not a standalone, sufficient valuation tool.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors very commonly use P/E as a simple, standalone
"cheap versus expensive" heuristic without considering growth context or
comparing against appropriate peers — a stock with a "low" P/E isn't
automatically undervalued if its growth prospects are correspondingly
weak, and a "high" P/E isn't automatically overvalued if growth prospects
genuinely justify it.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts specifically caution that P/E comparisons across
different **accounting standards, industries, and capital structures**
(connecting to `[07.07]`, `[07.10]`) can be misleading without careful
normalization — for example, a highly leveraged company's EPS can be
more volatile than an unleveraged peer's, affecting P/E comparability even
for genuinely similar underlying businesses.

## Common Mistakes
- Using P/E as a standalone "cheap vs. expensive" signal without growth
  context or peer comparison.
- Comparing P/E ratios across companies in fundamentally different
  industries or growth stages without adjustment.
- Ignoring diluted EPS in favor of basic EPS, potentially overstating
  per-share profitability for companies with significant convertible
  securities or stock options outstanding.

## Misconceptions
- **"A low P/E always means a stock is undervalued."** A low P/E can
  reflect genuinely weak growth prospects, elevated risk, or other
  legitimate concerns — it is not an automatic "cheap and safe" signal
  without further analysis.
- **"A high P/E always means a stock is overvalued."** As the strong-
  growth technology sector examples illustrate, a high P/E can be
  justified by genuinely superior growth prospects — PEG and fuller
  valuation analysis (Module 09) are needed to assess this properly.
- **"PEG ratio around 1.0 is a rigid, universal 'fairly valued'
  threshold."** PEG is a useful simplifying heuristic, not a precise,
  universally applicable valuation rule — it doesn't account for risk
  differences, growth sustainability, or many other factors developed
  fully in Module 09.

## Key Takeaways
- EPS translates company profit to a per-share basis; P/E expresses how
  much investors pay per rupee/dollar of current earnings.
- PEG (P/E / Growth Rate) adjusts P/E for growth, enabling more meaningful
  comparison across companies with different growth profiles.
- The dot-com bubble is a stark historical example of P/E-based valuation
  disconnected from realistic growth-adjusted justification.
- Neither P/E nor PEG alone is a sufficient valuation tool — both require
  peer, historical, and fuller valuation context (Module 09).

## Summary
Earnings Per Share, the Price-to-Earnings ratio, and the growth-adjusted
PEG ratio together form the most widely-referenced, media-visible
valuation metrics — translating company profit into per-share terms and
expressing market sentiment about future growth relative to current
earnings — with the dot-com bubble standing as a stark historical warning
against using these metrics naively, disconnected from realistic growth
context. This completes the module's quantitative toolkit, setting up the
next three lessons' shift toward qualitative analysis: competitive moats,
management quality, and industry/sector frameworks.

## Quiz
1. How is EPS calculated?
   a) Revenue / Shares Outstanding
   b) Net Profit / Shares Outstanding
   c) Share Price / Net Profit
   d) Assets / Liabilities

2. How is the P/E Ratio calculated?
   a) EPS / Share Price  b) Share Price / EPS  c) Revenue / EPS  d) Net Profit / Revenue

3. What does the PEG Ratio adjust the P/E ratio for?
   a) Inflation  b) Earnings growth rate  c) Currency exchange rates  d) Interest rates

4. What historical event illustrates P/E-based valuation excess
   disconnected from realistic growth?
   a) The 2021 Zomato IPO
   b) The dot-com bubble (1999-2000)
   c) The 1875 founding of BSE
   d) The 2023 T+1 settlement transition

5. True or False: A low P/E ratio always means a stock is undervalued and
   safe to buy.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — EPS = Net Profit / Shares Outstanding.
2. **b)** — P/E Ratio = Share Price / EPS.
3. **b)** — PEG adjusts P/E for the company's earnings growth rate.
4. **b)** — The dot-com bubble illustrates P/E excess disconnected from realistic growth.
5. **b) False** — A low P/E can reflect genuinely weak growth prospects or elevated risk, not automatic undervaluation.
