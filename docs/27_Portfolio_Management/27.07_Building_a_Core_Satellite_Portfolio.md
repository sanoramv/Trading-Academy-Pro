# [27.07] Building a Core-Satellite Portfolio

- Recognize the structure's practical appeal for individual traders managing multiple strategies from Module 24.

## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **core-satellite portfolio** divides capital into two components: the
**core** — typically a large majority (e.g., 60-80%) of the portfolio,
allocated to broad, diversified, often passively-managed holdings
(index funds, ETFs) reflecting the strategic asset allocation from
`[27.02]` — and **satellites** — smaller, more actively-managed or
tactical positions (e.g., sector rotation bets `[27.06]`, individual
strategy allocations from Module 24, or options income strategies
`[24.09]`) intended to add incremental return or express a specific,
higher-conviction view, without risking the majority of the portfolio
on any single active idea.

### Intuition
Active strategies (sector rotation, individual stock-picking, options
income) carry genuine potential for outperformance but also genuine
risk of underperformance or error — the core-satellite structure limits
the portfolio's total exposure to that active risk by keeping it
confined to a deliberately smaller portion, while the core provides
steady, diversified, lower-cost exposure to the broad market as its
foundation, directly connecting to `[24.11]`'s portfolio-of-systems logic.

### Mental Model
```
CORE-SATELLITE STRUCTURE (illustrative allocation):

  CORE (e.g., 70% of portfolio):
    - Broad index funds/ETFs (Nifty 50, S&P 500, [27.02]'s
      strategic allocation)
    - LOW cost, LOW turnover, DIVERSIFIED, largely PASSIVE
    - Provides the portfolio's baseline risk/return foundation

  SATELLITES (e.g., 30% of portfolio, split across several):
    - Sector rotation tilts, [27.06]                    (e.g., 10%)
    - Individual strategy allocations, Module 24         (e.g., 10%)
      (trend-following, [24.02]; options income, [24.09])
    - High-conviction individual stock picks, [08.10]    (e.g., 10%)
    - HIGHER cost/turnover potential, ACTIVE, TACTICAL

  KEY PRINCIPLE: satellites can UNDERPERFORM significantly without
  threatening the PORTFOLIO'S OVERALL foundation, since they represent
  only a MINORITY of total capital
```

### Visual Explanation
A core-satellite portfolio's pie chart shows one large, dominant slice
(the core) surrounded by several smaller slices (the satellites) —
visually representing how the structure concentrates most capital in
diversified, lower-risk exposure while confining active, higher-
conviction bets to a clearly bounded, smaller portion.

## Real World Analogy
A core-satellite portfolio is like a company's business model combining
a stable, reliable core product line (steady revenue, lower margin,
lower risk) with a smaller portfolio of experimental new ventures
(higher potential, higher risk) — the core sustains the business
regardless of how any single venture performs, while the ventures
provide upside potential without threatening the company's overall
survival if any individual one fails.

## Indian Market Example
An Indian investor allocates 70% of their portfolio to a Nifty 50 index
fund and government bonds (the core), then allocates the remaining 30%
across three satellites: a sector rotation tilt toward banking stocks
during an anticipated recovery phase (`[27.06]`), a small allocation to
a systematic Nifty theta-selling strategy (`[24.09]`), and a handful of
individually researched, high-conviction stock picks (`[08.10]`).
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A US investor allocates 75% to a combination of S&P 500 and bond index
funds (the core), then allocates the remaining 25% across a
momentum-based satellite (`[24.08]`), a tactical sector tilt toward
technology during a perceived mid-cycle expansion (`[27.06]`), and a
small options income sleeve (`[24.09]`). **Hypothetical Example** for
illustration only.

## Historical Example
The core-satellite approach gained significant traction in institutional
and later retail portfolio management from the 1990s-2000s onward,
paralleling the broader rise of low-cost index fund investing (pioneered
at scale by Vanguard's John Bogle from the 1970s) — the core-satellite
structure offered a practical way to capture index investing's low-cost,
diversification benefits while still accommodating investors' and
managers' desire to express specific, higher-conviction active views.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional asset managers frequently market core-satellite products
explicitly, using low-cost index or factor-based funds for the core
component and higher-fee, actively-managed or alternative strategies
for the satellite component — a structure that also conveniently aligns
with differentiated fee structures across the two components.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders who have worked through this entire academy's Module 24
strategy frameworks can use the core-satellite structure specifically to
house those individual strategies (trend-following, options income,
event-driven) as bounded satellites around a diversified core, directly
operationalizing `[24.11]`'s portfolio-of-systems principle within a
concrete allocation framework.

## Professional Insights
Professional practitioners often size satellites according to their
own individually-assessed conviction and risk-adjusted expected value —
a satellite strategy with a longer track record and higher confidence
might warrant a larger allocation than a newer, less-proven tactical idea,
within the overall satellite budget.

## Common Mistakes
- Allowing satellite allocations to grow disproportionately large over
  time (e.g., after a winning streak), effectively abandoning the
  core's protective, bounded structure.
- Choosing satellites that are highly correlated with the core or with
  each other, reducing the genuine diversification benefit `[27.04]`
  emphasized.
- Neglecting to apply Module 24's journaling discipline (`[24.12]`)
  separately to each satellite strategy's own performance.

## Misconceptions
- **"Core-satellite means passive investing is always superior."** It
  is a structural framework accommodating both passive and active
  approaches deliberately, not an argument that either is universally superior.
- **"Satellites should be sized based on excitement or recent
  performance."** Satellite sizing should follow the same disciplined
  risk management (Module 25/26) as any individual strategy — allowing
  size to grow reactively undermines the structure's protective purpose.
- **"A core-satellite portfolio requires many satellites to be
  effective."** Even one or two well-chosen, genuinely diversifying
  satellites around a solid core can meaningfully implement this structure.

## Key Takeaways
- Core-satellite portfolios combine a large, diversified, largely
  passive core with smaller, bounded, active/tactical satellites.
- Satellites can express high-conviction views (sector rotation, Module
  24 strategies, individual stock picks) without threatening the
  portfolio's overall foundation if any single one underperforms.
- Rooted in the broader rise of low-cost index investing (Vanguard/
  Bogle, 1970s onward) combined with continued demand for active
  strategy expression.
- Directly operationalizes `[24.11]`'s portfolio-of-systems principle
  within a concrete, bounded allocation framework.

## Summary
The core-satellite structure — a large, diversified, largely passive
core surrounded by smaller, bounded, active or tactical satellites —
provides a practical framework for incorporating sector rotation
(`[27.06]`), Module 24's individual strategies, and high-conviction
stock picks without over-committing the portfolio's overall foundation
to any single active view, directly operationalizing `[24.11]`'s
portfolio-of-systems principle in concrete allocation terms. The final
lesson in this module turns to performance measurement — the standard
metrics (Sharpe, Sortino, Alpha, Beta) used to evaluate how well both
the core and the satellites are actually performing on a risk-adjusted basis.
