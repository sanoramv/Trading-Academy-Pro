## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Rebalancing** is the periodic process of realigning a portfolio's
actual asset allocation back to its intended target weights (`[27.02]`),
which drift over time as different assets grow or decline at different
rates. **Calendar-based rebalancing** rebalances at fixed intervals
(e.g., quarterly, annually) regardless of how far allocations have
drifted. **Threshold-based rebalancing** rebalances only when an asset
class's actual weight deviates from its target by more than a
predefined amount (e.g., 5 percentage points), regardless of how much
time has passed.

### Intuition
If a portfolio starts at 70% equity / 30% debt and equities
significantly outperform over a year, the portfolio might drift to 80%
equity / 20% debt — a materially different, and likely riskier,
allocation than originally intended, entirely as a side effect of
market movement rather than a deliberate decision. Rebalancing
mechanically sells some of the outperforming asset (equity, now
overweight) and buys more of the underperforming asset (debt, now
underweight) — a disciplined process that happens to enforce "sell
high, buy low" behavior systematically, without requiring the
psychologically difficult judgment call `[27.06]`/Module 28 discuss.

### Mental Model
```
REBALANCING MECHANISM (illustrative):

  TARGET allocation: 70% Equity / 30% Debt
  AFTER a strong equity rally, ACTUAL drifts to: 80% Equity / 20% Debt

  REBALANCING ACTION: SELL enough equity and BUY enough debt to
  return to the 70/30 TARGET -- mechanically "selling high" (equity,
  now elevated) and "buying low" (debt, now relatively cheaper)

CALENDAR-BASED:                    THRESHOLD-BASED:
  Rebalance every [quarter/year]     Rebalance whenever ANY asset
  REGARDLESS of drift magnitude      class drifts >[X]% from target,
  -- simple, predictable schedule    REGARDLESS of time elapsed
                                      -- responds to ACTUAL drift size
```

### Visual Explanation
A portfolio's allocation chart over time, without rebalancing, typically
shows the outperforming asset class's slice steadily growing beyond its
original target — rebalancing periodically resets this drift back to
the original target proportions, producing a sawtooth-like pattern of
gradual drift followed by periodic correction.

## Real World Analogy
Rebalancing is like periodically re-leveling a garden bed that
naturally settles unevenly over time due to rain and foot traffic —
without periodic re-leveling, the bed gradually becomes lopsided in
ways nobody deliberately chose; a scheduled or threshold-triggered
re-leveling restores the originally intended, even shape.

## Indian Market Example
An Indian investor's portfolio, initially allocated 60% equity mutual
funds / 40% debt funds, drifts to 72% equity / 28% debt after a strong
multi-year equity bull run. Applying an annual calendar-based
rebalancing rule, the investor sells a portion of equity holdings and
adds to debt funds to restore the original 60/40 target, systematically
locking in some equity gains and adding to the relatively underweighted
debt allocation. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A US investor using a 5-percentage-point threshold-based rule notices
their target 65% equity / 35% bond allocation has drifted to 71% equity
/ 29% bonds after a strong quarter — triggering a rebalancing action
before the next scheduled calendar review, since the threshold was
breached ahead of the routine schedule. **Hypothetical Example** for
illustration only.

## Historical Example
Rebalancing discipline gained particular institutional prominence
through the widespread adoption of formalized Investment Policy
Statements (`[27.02]`'s institutional perspective) from the 1970s-80s
onward, with academic research since consistently finding that
disciplined rebalancing, particularly through periods of significant
market volatility like 2008-2009 and 2020, meaningfully affected
long-run portfolio outcomes compared to a "buy and never rebalance" approach.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional pension funds and endowments typically formalize
rebalancing rules directly within their Investment Policy Statements,
often using threshold-based triggers combined with a maximum calendar
review interval as a backstop — ensuring rebalancing occurs whichever
condition (drift magnitude or elapsed time) triggers first.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors frequently neglect rebalancing entirely, allowing
allocations to drift significantly over years — a passive failure that
can materially change a portfolio's actual risk profile away from what
was originally, deliberately chosen, without any conscious decision to
accept that change.

## Professional Insights
Professional advisors emphasize that rebalancing decisions must weigh
transaction costs and, in taxable accounts, capital gains tax
implications against the diversification/risk-control benefit — overly
frequent rebalancing in a high-transaction-cost or tax-sensitive account
can erode more value than the drift-correction benefit provides.

## Common Mistakes
- Neglecting to rebalance at all, allowing significant, unintentional
  allocation drift over time.
- Rebalancing too frequently in a taxable or high-transaction-cost
  account, eroding value through costs that exceed the benefit.
- Rebalancing emotionally in response to market moves rather than
  following a predefined calendar or threshold rule.

## Misconceptions
- **"Rebalancing guarantees higher returns."** Its primary purpose is
  risk control (maintaining the intended allocation), not return
  maximization — though its "sell high, buy low" mechanism has
  historically provided modest additional benefit in some studies.
- **"More frequent rebalancing is always better."** Transaction costs
  and tax implications must be weighed — overly frequent rebalancing
  can erode more value than it preserves.
- **"Calendar-based and threshold-based rebalancing produce identical
  results."** They respond to different triggers (time vs. drift
  magnitude) and can produce meaningfully different rebalancing
  frequency and timing in practice.

## Key Takeaways
- Rebalancing restores a portfolio's actual allocation to its intended
  target weights, which drift naturally as assets grow/decline at
  different rates.
- Calendar-based rebalancing uses fixed intervals; threshold-based uses
  drift-magnitude triggers — institutions often combine both.
- Rebalancing mechanically enforces "sell high, buy low," but its
  primary purpose is risk control, not guaranteed outperformance.
- Transaction costs and tax implications must be weighed against
  rebalancing's benefits, particularly in taxable accounts.

## Summary
Rebalancing restores a portfolio's allocation to its intended targets as
market movement causes natural drift over time, using either calendar-
based or threshold-based triggers (or both, as many institutions do),
mechanically enforcing a disciplined "sell high, buy low" pattern while
requiring transaction costs and tax implications to be weighed against
the benefit. The next lesson turns to sector rotation and business
cycle investing — a more active approach to shifting allocation
deliberately based on where the economy sits in its cycle, rather than
simply restoring a fixed target.
