## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Sharpe ratio**, developed by William Sharpe (`[27.03]`'s Nobel co-
laureate), measures risk-adjusted return: excess return over the
risk-free rate, divided by the portfolio's total volatility (standard
deviation). **Sortino ratio** refines this by dividing excess return
only by *downside* volatility (harmful volatility, ignoring upside
swings), addressing Sharpe's treatment of upside volatility as equally
"risky" as downside volatility. **Beta** measures a portfolio or
stock's sensitivity to overall market movement (systematic risk,
`[27.01]`) — a Beta of 1 moves with the market, above 1 amplifies
market moves, below 1 dampens them. **Alpha** measures the excess return
a portfolio or manager generated *beyond* what Beta and overall market
movement alone would predict — the closest single-number approximation
of genuine, skill-based outperformance.

### Intuition
Raw return alone says nothing about how much risk was taken to achieve
it — a 15% return achieved with wild volatility is a very different
accomplishment than the same 15% achieved smoothly. These four metrics
each answer a related but distinct question: Sharpe asks "return per
unit of total risk taken," Sortino refines this to "return per unit of
*harmful* risk taken," Beta asks "how much does this move with the
market," and Alpha asks "how much return came from something *beyond*
just market exposure" — together providing a far more complete picture
than return alone.

### Mental Model
```
SHARPE RATIO:
  Sharpe = (Portfolio Return - Risk-Free Rate) / Portfolio Std. Deviation
  Higher Sharpe = more return per unit of TOTAL risk taken

SORTINO RATIO:
  Sortino = (Portfolio Return - Risk-Free Rate) / DOWNSIDE Deviation only
  Higher Sortino = more return per unit of HARMFUL (downside) risk taken
  (does NOT penalize upside volatility the way Sharpe does)

BETA:
  Beta = 1.0  -> moves WITH the market
  Beta > 1.0  -> AMPLIFIES market moves (more volatile than market)
  Beta < 1.0  -> DAMPENS market moves (less volatile than market)

ALPHA (simplified CAPM-based framing):
  Expected Return (CAPM) = Risk-Free Rate + Beta x (Market Return - Risk-Free Rate)
  Alpha = ACTUAL Return - EXPECTED Return (per CAPM)

  Positive Alpha = outperformed what Beta/market exposure alone predicted
  Negative Alpha = underperformed what Beta/market exposure alone predicted
```

### Visual Explanation
Comparing two portfolios with identical raw returns but different
Sharpe ratios typically shows one with a smooth, steadily rising equity
curve (higher Sharpe, less volatility per unit of return) and the other
with a choppier, more volatile curve reaching the same endpoint (lower
Sharpe) — visually demonstrating why risk-adjusted metrics reveal
meaningfully more than terminal return alone.

## Real World Analogy
These four metrics are like a car's complete performance review rather
than just its top speed: Sharpe is fuel efficiency per mile (return per
unit of total effort/risk), Sortino specifically measures efficiency
during rough terrain (downside conditions only), Beta measures how much
the car's speed changes relative to traffic flow (market sensitivity),
and Alpha measures whether the car actually arrived faster than traffic
conditions alone would predict — together giving a complete picture no
single figure (like top speed/raw return alone) can provide.

## Indian Market Example
Two Nifty-benchmarked mutual funds both return 14% over a year — Fund A
with a Sharpe ratio of 1.2 and Beta of 0.9, Fund B with a Sharpe ratio
of 0.6 and Beta of 1.4. An investor comparing risk-adjusted performance
would recognize Fund A achieved its return more efficiently (higher
Sharpe) while taking on less market sensitivity (lower Beta) than Fund
B, despite both showing identical raw returns. **Hypothetical Example**
for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A US hedge fund reports a 12% annual return with a positive Alpha of
3% relative to the S&P 500 given its Beta of 0.7 — meaning the fund
generated 3 percentage points of return beyond what its market
sensitivity alone (per CAPM) would have predicted, a commonly cited
indicator of genuine manager skill rather than simply amplified or
dampened market exposure. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
William Sharpe introduced the Sharpe ratio in 1966 (originally called
the "reward-to-variability ratio"), building directly on his earlier
Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) work from the early 1960s, which
itself extended Markowitz's MPT (`[27.03]`) — Sharpe shared the 1990
Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences alongside Markowitz and
Merton Miller, cementing this entire performance measurement framework's
close theoretical lineage back to `[27.01]`'s and `[27.03]`'s foundations.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional allocators use Sharpe, Sortino, Alpha, and Beta as
standard, near-universal criteria when evaluating and comparing fund
managers, often requiring minimum thresholds on these metrics (not just
raw return) before allocating capital — directly connecting back to
`[25.05]`'s emphasis that risk-adjusted, not raw, performance is what
matters for genuine evaluation.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors frequently compare funds or strategies by raw return
alone, missing that a lower-return option with a substantially higher
Sharpe or Sortino ratio may represent a genuinely better risk-adjusted
choice — this lesson's four metrics provide accessible, calculable
tools for making that comparison more rigorously.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts often prefer Sortino over Sharpe specifically for
strategies with asymmetric return distributions (e.g., trend-following's
occasional large wins, `[24.02]`, or options strategies with capped
upside, `[21.10]`), since Sharpe's treatment of upside volatility as
equally "risky" as downside volatility can understate these strategies'
true risk-adjusted appeal.

## Common Mistakes
- Comparing strategies or funds by raw return alone, ignoring risk-
  adjusted metrics entirely.
- Using Sharpe ratio for strategies with highly asymmetric returns
  (e.g., trend-following) without also checking Sortino, which handles
  upside volatility differently.
- Interpreting a positive Alpha as guaranteed, permanent skill rather
  than a historical, period-specific measurement subject to change.

## Misconceptions
- **"Higher raw return always means a better investment."** Risk-
  adjusted metrics (Sharpe, Sortino) frequently reveal that a lower-
  return option achieved its return more efficiently, a meaningfully
  different comparison.
- **"Beta measures total risk."** Beta measures only systematic
  (market-related) risk sensitivity — it says nothing about
  unsystematic risk (`[27.01]`) or downside-specific risk (Sortino's focus).
- **"A positive Alpha guarantees the manager will continue
  outperforming."** Alpha is a historical, period-specific measurement —
  it does not guarantee persistence, similar to `[24.12]`'s caution
  about extrapolating past performance indefinitely.

## Key Takeaways
- Sharpe ratio: excess return per unit of total risk (volatility).
- Sortino ratio: excess return per unit of downside (harmful) risk only —
  better suited to asymmetric-return strategies.
- Beta: sensitivity to overall market movement (systematic risk).
- Alpha: return beyond what Beta/market exposure alone would predict —
  the closest single-number proxy for genuine skill-based outperformance.
- All four metrics, developed by Sharpe (1966, building on Markowitz's
  MPT, `[27.03]`), together provide a far more complete performance
  picture than raw return alone.

## Summary
Sharpe, Sortino, Beta, and Alpha together provide the standard
risk-adjusted performance measurement toolkit — extending Markowitz's
MPT foundation (`[27.03]`) through William Sharpe's own 1966 and earlier
CAPM work — allowing a portfolio or strategy to be evaluated by how
efficiently and skillfully it generated return relative to the risk
taken, rather than by raw return alone. This completes Module 27's
treatment of portfolio management; Module 28 now turns to trading
psychology — the behavioral discipline required to actually implement
everything from strategy selection (Module 24) through risk management
(Module 25), sizing (Module 26), and portfolio construction (this
module) consistently, especially under emotional pressure.

   a) Raw return only  b) Excess return per unit of total risk  c) Market capitalization  d) Dividend yield

2. How does the Sortino ratio differ from the Sharpe ratio?
   a) They are identical  b) Sortino uses only downside volatility, not total volatility  c) Sortino ignores risk entirely  d) Sortino only applies to bonds

3. What does Beta measure?
   a) Downside risk only  b) Sensitivity to overall market movement  c) Dividend policy  d) Interest rate risk

4. What does a positive Alpha indicate?
   a) Guaranteed future outperformance  b) Return beyond what Beta/market exposure alone would predict, historically  c) High Beta  d) Nothing meaningful

5. True or False: A higher raw return always indicates a better risk-adjusted investment.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Excess return per unit of total risk (volatility).
2. **b)** — Sortino uses only downside volatility, not total volatility.
3. **b)** — Sensitivity to overall market movement (systematic risk).
4. **b)** — Return beyond what Beta/market exposure alone would predict, historically — not a guarantee of future persistence.
5. **b) False** — Risk-adjusted metrics (Sharpe, Sortino) often reveal a different, more complete comparison than raw return alone.
