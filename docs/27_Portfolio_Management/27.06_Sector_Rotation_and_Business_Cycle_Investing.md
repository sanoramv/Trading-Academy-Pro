## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Sector rotation** is the active investment strategy of shifting
portfolio weight between sectors based on where the broader economy
sits within its business cycle (`[05.08]`), based on the observation
that different sectors have historically tended to outperform or
underperform at different cycle stages. **Business cycle investing**
more broadly incorporates macro cycle awareness (recession, recovery,
expansion, peak) into portfolio construction decisions, of which sector
rotation is the most common practical implementation.

### Intuition
Different sectors respond differently to the same macro conditions:
consumer discretionary and technology spending tends to expand during
economic growth, while consumer staples and utilities (less
discretionary, "defensive" demand) tend to hold up relatively better
during downturns when discretionary spending contracts. Sector rotation
attempts to systematically shift allocation toward the sectors expected
to benefit most from the current or approaching cycle stage.

### Mental Model
```
ILLUSTRATIVE SECTOR TENDENCIES BY BUSINESS CYCLE STAGE
(historical tendencies, [05.08] -- NOT guaranteed, NOT investment advice):

  EARLY RECOVERY (post-trough):
    Tends to favor: Financials, Consumer Discretionary, Industrials
    (rate cuts and improving credit conditions historically benefit
     these sectors first)

  EXPANSION (mid-cycle):
    Tends to favor: Technology, Industrials, Materials
    (broad growth historically benefits cyclical, growth-sensitive sectors)

  LATE-CYCLE/PEAK:
    Tends to favor: Energy, Commodities
    (inflationary pressures often peak late-cycle)

  RECESSION/CONTRACTION:
    Tends to favor: Consumer Staples, Utilities, Healthcare
    ("defensive" sectors -- demand less sensitive to economic conditions)

  THESE ARE HISTORICAL TENDENCIES, NOT GUARANTEED PATTERNS --
  every cycle differs in cause, duration, and sector behavior
```

## Real World Analogy
Sector rotation is like a farmer adjusting which crops to prioritize
based on the season — certain crops thrive in spring conditions, others
in summer heat, others in autumn — but unlike calendar seasons, economic
"seasons" (cycle stages) don't arrive on a fixed, predictable schedule,
making the farming analogy's biggest limitation directly relevant:
knowing exactly which season it currently is remains genuinely difficult
in real time.

## Indian Market Example
An Indian investor practicing sector rotation might increase allocation
to banking and financial services stocks during an early recovery phase
following an RBI rate-cutting cycle (`[06.02]`), then shift toward FMCG
and pharma (relatively defensive sectors in the Indian market context)
if leading indicators suggest an approaching slowdown. **Hypothetical
Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A US investor practicing sector rotation might overweight technology
and consumer discretionary during a clear mid-cycle expansion, then
shift toward utilities and consumer staples if yield curve inversion
(`[06.07]`) or other leading indicators suggest an approaching recession.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
Sector rotation strategies gained particular institutional prominence
through Sam Stovall's widely referenced work at Standard & Poor's from
the 1990s onward, formalizing the "sector rotation clock" framework
this lesson's mental model draws on — though Stovall and subsequent
researchers have also documented that individual cycles frequently
deviate from the idealized pattern, especially cycles driven by unusual
causes (e.g., the 2020 COVID recession's unusually rapid, externally-
triggered nature compared to typical credit-cycle-driven recessions).

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional macro and multi-asset desks employ dedicated economists
and quantitative models specifically to assess current business cycle
positioning, recognizing that sector rotation's practical value depends
heavily on accurately identifying the current cycle stage — a
genuinely difficult forecasting problem even with substantial resources.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors attempting sector rotation should recognize the same
timing difficulty applies to them as to well-resourced institutions —
cycle stage identification in real time is imprecise, and a retail
sector rotation strategy built on lagging, backward-looking cycle
confirmation may miss much of the anticipated rotation's benefit.

## Professional Insights
Professional practitioners often blend sector rotation with a
core-satellite structure (`[27.07]` next lesson) — maintaining a
diversified core allocation while using a smaller satellite portion for
tactical sector tilts, limiting the potential damage from a mistimed
cycle call while still capturing some rotation benefit if the call proves correct.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating historical sector tendencies as guaranteed, precise rules
  rather than general, imperfect historical patterns.
- Overcommitting a large portion of a portfolio to a single cycle-stage
  bet, given genuine, well-documented difficulty in timing cycle transitions.
- Ignoring that individual cycles can differ substantially in cause and
  duration, limiting the applicability of any single historical template.

## Misconceptions
- **"Sector rotation reliably predicts which sectors will outperform
  next."** It reflects historical *tendencies*, not guaranteed,
  precisely repeating patterns — every cycle differs somewhat in cause and behavior.
- **"Identifying the current business cycle stage is straightforward."**
  Even well-resourced institutional economists face genuine, well-
  documented difficulty timing cycle transitions accurately in real time.
- **"Sector rotation and rebalancing (`[27.05]`) are the same thing."**
  Rebalancing restores a FIXED target allocation; sector rotation
  actively SHIFTS the target itself based on a cycle view.

## Key Takeaways
- Sector rotation shifts portfolio weight toward sectors historically
  favored at the current business cycle stage (`[05.08]`).
- Historical tendencies (financials/discretionary early-cycle,
  tech/industrials mid-cycle, energy late-cycle, staples/utilities
  during recession) are patterns, not guarantees.
- Sam Stovall's work at S&P (1990s onward) formalized the widely
  referenced "sector rotation clock" framework.
- Accurately timing cycle transitions remains genuinely difficult, even
  for well-resourced institutions — a core practical limitation.

## Summary
Sector rotation actively shifts portfolio weight toward sectors
historically favored at the current business cycle stage, building
directly on `[05.08]`'s business cycle framework and Sam Stovall's
widely referenced sector rotation clock — but its practical value
depends on accurately timing cycle transitions, a genuinely difficult
forecasting challenge that limits how heavily any single cycle-stage
bet should be sized. The next lesson turns to the core-satellite
portfolio structure — a practical framework for incorporating tactical
strategies like sector rotation without over-committing the entire
portfolio to any single active view.
