## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Asset allocation** is the decision of how to divide portfolio capital
across broad asset classes — equity, debt/fixed income, cash, commodities,
real estate, and derivatives (`[01.03]`) — as distinct from **security
selection**, which decides *which specific* stocks, bonds, or
instruments to hold *within* each asset class. Research widely cited in
institutional portfolio management (notably the Brinson, Hood, and
Beebower studies of the late 1980s) has found that asset allocation
explains the large majority of variability in a diversified portfolio's
returns over time — more than the specific security selection decisions
made within each asset class.

### Intuition
Deciding "how much in stocks versus bonds versus cash" has historically
mattered more for long-run portfolio outcomes than deciding "which
specific stocks" — because different asset classes behave very
differently across market and economic cycles (`[05.08]`), the *mix*
between them shapes the portfolio's overall risk and return profile
before individual security choices are even considered.

### Mental Model
```
ASSET ALLOCATION FRAMEWORK (illustrative, not personalized advice):

  KEY INPUTS:
    1. TIME HORIZON: longer horizon -> generally more capacity
       for equity's higher volatility, since more time to recover
       from drawdowns, [25.05]
    2. RISK TOLERANCE: psychological/financial capacity to withstand
       drawdown -- connects directly to Module 28's psychology theme
    3. GOALS: capital preservation vs. growth vs. income

  ILLUSTRATIVE AGE-BASED STARTING POINT (one common heuristic,
  NOT personalized advice, many variations exist):
    Equity % ~= 100 - Age  (a simplified, widely-cited rule of thumb)

    Age 25: ~75% equity, ~25% debt/cash (LONG horizon, higher
            capacity for volatility)
    Age 55: ~45% equity, ~55% debt/cash (SHORTER horizon, lower
            capacity for volatility, income needs approaching)

  This is a STARTING HEURISTIC only -- actual allocation should
  reflect individual risk tolerance, goals, and circumstances
```

### Visual Explanation
A series of pie charts across different life stages typically shows the
equity slice shrinking and the debt/cash slice growing as time horizon
shortens — visually representing the standard, though highly
simplified, glide-path logic behind age-based allocation heuristics.

## Real World Analogy
Asset allocation is like deciding a meal's overall macronutrient
balance (proportions of protein, carbohydrate, and fat) before choosing
specific dishes — the macronutrient mix shapes the meal's overall
nutritional character far more than which specific protein source or
vegetable is chosen within that established framework.

## Indian Market Example
A 30-year-old Indian investor with a long time horizon toward
retirement might allocate roughly 70% to Indian and global equities
(via direct stocks, mutual funds, or index funds), 25% to debt
instruments (PPF, bonds, debt mutual funds), and 5% to cash/liquid
funds — only then selecting specific stocks or funds within the equity
allocation using fundamental (`[08.10]`) or momentum (`[24.08]`)
criteria. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A 55-year-old US investor approaching retirement might shift toward a
more conservative 45% equity, 45% bonds, 10% cash allocation,
reflecting a shorter time horizon and reduced capacity to withstand a
significant equity drawdown before needing to draw on the portfolio for
income. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
The landmark studies by Gary Brinson, Randolph Hood, and Gilbert
Beebower, published in the Financial Analysts Journal in 1986 and 1991
("Determinants of Portfolio Performance"), found that asset allocation
policy explained the substantial majority of variation in returns
across a sample of large pension fund portfolios over time — a
frequently cited (though also subsequently debated and refined) finding
that shaped institutional portfolio management practice toward
prioritizing allocation decisions.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional pension funds and endowments formalize asset allocation
through an "Investment Policy Statement" specifying target allocations
and acceptable ranges across asset classes, treating this document as
the primary governance mechanism — individual security selection
decisions operate within, and are subordinate to, this predetermined
allocation framework.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors frequently spend disproportionate time researching
individual stock picks while giving comparatively little deliberate
thought to their overall asset allocation — the Brinson-Hood-Beebower
research suggests this prioritization may be inverted relative to what
actually drives long-run portfolio outcomes.

## Professional Insights
Professional advisors caution against treating any single allocation
heuristic (like "100 minus age") as a complete answer — genuine risk
tolerance, specific goals, and individual financial circumstances often
warrant meaningful deviation from simplified, generic rules of thumb.

## Common Mistakes
- Focusing heavily on individual security selection while neglecting
  deliberate asset allocation decisions.
- Applying a generic age-based heuristic without adjusting for
  individual risk tolerance and specific goals.
- Failing to revisit allocation as time horizon shortens or
  circumstances change over the years.

## Misconceptions
- **"Individual stock-picking skill matters more than asset allocation
  for long-run outcomes."** Influential research (Brinson, Hood,
  Beebower) suggests allocation policy explains the majority of return
  variability for diversified institutional portfolios, though this
  finding has also been debated and refined in subsequent research.
- **"Asset allocation is a one-time decision."** It should be revisited
  as time horizon, risk tolerance, and goals evolve over an investor's lifetime.
- **"An age-based heuristic is personalized financial advice."** It is
  a simplified, generic starting point — genuine allocation decisions
  should reflect individual circumstances beyond age alone.

## Key Takeaways
- Asset allocation (how much across equity/debt/cash/other) is distinct
  from security selection (which specific holdings within each class).
- Time horizon, risk tolerance, and goals are the three primary inputs
  shaping allocation decisions.
- Brinson, Hood, and Beebower's research (1986, 1991) found allocation
  policy explains the majority of return variability for the pension
  funds studied — a widely cited, though debated, finding.
- Allocation should be revisited periodically, not treated as a
  permanent, one-time decision.

## Summary
Asset allocation — the decision of how much capital to place across
equity, debt, cash, and other broad asset classes, shaped by time
horizon, risk tolerance, and goals — has been found, in influential
institutional research, to explain the majority of long-run portfolio
return variability, ahead of individual security selection decisions
made within each asset class. The next lesson turns to Modern Portfolio
Theory, providing the mathematical framework (building on `[27.01]`'s
Markowitz foundation) for optimizing exactly how that allocation should
balance risk and return.
