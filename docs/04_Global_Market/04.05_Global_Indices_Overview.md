# [04.05] Global Indices Overview (S&P 500, Dow, Nasdaq, DAX, Nikkei)

- Understand differing index construction methodologies across major indices.
- Recognize why the S&P 500 is often called the best proxy for the US economy.
- Compare index sector composition and its effect on behavior.

## Core Concepts

### Definition
Major global indices each track a specific basket of stocks, but differ in
construction methodology and scope:
- **S&P 500** (US): 500 large-cap US companies, free-float market-cap
  weighted — widely considered the best single proxy for the overall US
  stock market and economy.
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA)** (US): 30 large, established US
  companies, **price-weighted** (not market-cap weighted) — an older,
  narrower, methodologically distinct index.
- **Nasdaq Composite** (US): thousands of companies listed on NASDAQ,
  heavily weighted toward technology.
- **DAX** (Germany): major German companies, representing the largest
  European economy.
- **Nikkei 225** (Japan): 225 major Japanese companies, also
  **price-weighted** like the Dow, an important methodological similarity
  worth noting.

### Intuition
Different indices exist because no single basket of stocks perfectly
represents "the market" — analysts and investors need different lenses for
different purposes: broad economic proxy (S&P 500), a long-running
historical benchmark (Dow), a technology-sector proxy (Nasdaq), and
country-specific economic gauges (DAX, Nikkei) — each answering a slightly
different question about market or economic conditions.

### Mental Model
```
BREADTH:  Nasdaq Composite (thousands) > S&P 500 (500) > Nikkei 225 (225)
          > Dow Jones (30)

WEIGHTING METHOD:
  Free-float market cap: S&P 500, Nifty 50, Sensex, DAX
  Price-weighted: Dow Jones Industrial Average, Nikkei 225
  (price-weighting means a higher-PRICED stock has more index influence,
  regardless of the company's actual total market cap -- a methodological
  quirk covered in [03.04])
```

### Visual Explanation
Sector composition varies dramatically across indices — the Nasdaq
Composite is heavily technology-weighted, while the DAX has historically
carried more industrial/manufacturing weight, shaping how each index reacts
to different types of news.

## Real World Analogy
Using only the Dow Jones to judge the entire US economy is like judging an
entire country's cuisine by visiting only 30 specific, older, well-
established restaurants — informative about a certain slice of tradition,
but missing the broader, more representative picture that a 500-restaurant
survey (S&P 500) or a technology-focused survey (Nasdaq) would offer.
Each index is a different, deliberately-scoped "survey" of the market, not
a single universal truth.

## Indian Market Example
Indian financial media and traders very commonly reference the **S&P 500**
and **Nasdaq Composite's** overnight performance each morning (alongside
GIFT Nifty, per `[04.03]`) as a leading indicator for Nifty 50 sentiment —
a habit reflecting the practical reality that US index performance is one
of the most consistently-watched overnight inputs shaping the Indian
trading day, more so than most other global indices.

## US Market Example
The **Dow Jones Industrial Average's** price-weighting quirk means a
single very high-priced stock (regardless of the underlying company's
actual total market capitalization) can have an outsized effect on the
Dow's point movement compared to a much larger company with a lower share
price — a structural oddity that has led many professional analysts to
consider the S&P 500 a more methodologically sound and representative
gauge of the broader US market, despite the Dow's greater historical name
recognition and longer trading history (dating to 1896).

## Historical Example
The **Nikkei 225's** 1989 peak near 38,915, followed by a decades-long
decline and eventual 2024 recovery (`[04.01]`), stands as one of the most
significant cautionary examples in global index history — illustrating
that even a major, well-diversified developed-market index can experience
an extraordinarily prolonged drawdown, a historical data point every
student of index investing (Module 09) should internalize rather than
assume "stocks always recover quickly in the long run" as a universal law.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutions use different indices as **benchmarks** for different mandate
types — a US large-cap fund manager is typically measured against the S&P
500, a US technology-focused fund against the Nasdaq-100, and a global
macro fund might track relative performance across the S&P 500, DAX, and
Nikkei 225 simultaneously to assess relative regional strength — benchmark
selection is a deliberate, meaningful institutional decision, not an
arbitrary choice.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors sometimes conflate "the Dow" with "the US stock market"
broadly, due to its historical prominence in media reporting, without
realizing its narrower scope (30 stocks) and unusual price-weighting
methodology make it a less representative gauge than the S&P 500 for most
analytical purposes.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts specifically track **index concentration** —
how much of an index's total weight sits in its largest few constituents
— since a highly concentrated index (where a handful of mega-cap stocks
dominate) behaves quite differently from a more evenly distributed one,
even if both nominally track "the broad market." This concentration
dynamic (noted for both the S&P 500 and Nifty 50 in `[03.04]`) has become
an increasingly discussed topic in recent years across multiple major
global indices.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating "the Dow" as synonymous with "the US stock market" without
  understanding its narrower, differently-weighted construction.
- Not accounting for sector tilt differences (e.g., Nasdaq's heavy tech
  weighting) when comparing index performance across different indices.
- Assuming all major indices use the same free-float market-cap weighting
  methodology, missing the Dow and Nikkei's distinct price-weighting
  approach.

## Misconceptions
- **"A higher-priced stock is automatically a 'better' or 'bigger'
  company."** In price-weighted indices like the Dow, a stock's index
  influence depends on its share price, not its total company size (market
  cap) — a $500 stock has more index weight than a $50 stock in the Dow
  regardless of which company is actually larger by total value.
- **"All major indices will show similar percentage returns over any given
  period."** Differing sector composition and weighting methodology can
  produce meaningfully different returns from broadly similar underlying
  economies over the same period.
- **"The Nikkei 225's decades-long stagnation could never happen to other
  major indices."** While not a certainty, the Nikkei's history is a
  documented, real example that broad developed-market indices are not
  immune to multi-decade structural declines, a humility worth carrying
  into long-term investment planning.

## Key Takeaways
- Major global indices differ in scope (number of stocks), weighting
  methodology (free-float vs. price-weighted), and sector composition.
- The S&P 500 is widely considered the most representative single US
  market proxy, more so than the narrower, price-weighted Dow.
- The Dow and Nikkei 225 share the same distinctive price-weighting
  methodology.
- Index concentration and sector tilt meaningfully shape how different
  indices behave, even when tracking broadly similar economies.

## Summary
Major global indices — the S&P 500, Dow Jones, Nasdaq Composite, DAX, and
Nikkei 225 — each offer a differently-scoped, differently-weighted lens on
their respective markets and economies, and understanding these
construction differences (echoing the free-float methodology from
`[03.04]`) prevents common misreadings, like treating the narrow,
price-weighted Dow as a complete US market proxy. This survey of global
indices sets up the next lesson's deeper focus on how these markets
actually correlate with each other and with India.

## Quiz
1. Which US index is widely considered the best broad market proxy?
   a) Dow Jones  b) S&P 500  c) Nasdaq-100 only  d) Russell 2000 only

2. What weighting methodology does the Dow Jones Industrial Average use?
   a) Free-float market cap  b) Price-weighting  c) Equal-weighting  d) Volume-weighting

3. Which major index shares the Dow's price-weighting methodology?
   a) S&P 500  b) Nikkei 225  c) DAX  d) Nifty 50

4. What historical event illustrates a major developed-market index's
   multi-decade decline?
   a) The S&P 500 in 2022  b) The Nikkei 225's 1989-to-2024 trajectory  c) The Dow in 2020  d) The DAX in 2008

5. True or False: All major global indices use identical weighting
   methodologies.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — The S&P 500 is widely considered the most representative broad US market proxy.
2. **b)** — The Dow Jones uses price-weighting, unlike most other major indices.
3. **b)** — The Nikkei 225 also uses price-weighting, like the Dow.
4. **b)** — The Nikkei 225's 1989 peak wasn't reclaimed until 2024, a 35-year cycle.
5. **b) False** — Methodologies differ meaningfully (free-float vs. price-weighted) across major indices.
