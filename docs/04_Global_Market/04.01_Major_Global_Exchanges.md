# [04.01] Major Global Exchanges

> Module: Global Market · Lesson 1 of 8 · Estimated time: 30 minutes

## Learning Objectives
- Identify the world's major stock exchanges by region and relative size.
- Understand why exchange location and time zone matter for trading strategy.
- Recognize the difference between an exchange's home market and its listed
  companies' global operations.
- Build a mental map of where global capital concentrates.

## Core Concepts

### Definition
Beyond India's NSE and BSE, the major global exchanges every trader should
recognize include: the **New York Stock Exchange (NYSE)** and **NASDAQ**
(United States), the **London Stock Exchange (LSE)** (United Kingdom), the
**Tokyo Stock Exchange (TSE)** (Japan), the **Hong Kong Stock Exchange
(HKEX)** (Hong Kong/China gateway), the **Shanghai and Shenzhen Stock
Exchanges** (mainland China), and the **Euronext** group (pan-European,
covering Paris, Amsterdam, and other European markets). Together, these
exchanges host the vast majority of the world's publicly traded equity
value.

### Intuition
Global capital doesn't distribute evenly — it concentrates heavily in a
handful of major financial centers, each anchored by deep, liquid
exchanges and strong legal/regulatory infrastructure. Understanding this
concentration (the US alone represents a very large share of total global
equity market capitalization) helps explain why US market moves have
outsized global influence, a theme developed fully in `[04.03]` and
`[04.06]`.

### Mental Model
Group major exchanges by region and trading day sequence — since markets
open and close at different times globally, they form a rough
**"trading day relay"** that circles the globe:

```
Asia-Pacific opens first --> Europe opens next (overlapping late Asia) -->
US opens last (overlapping late Europe) --> Asia-Pacific opens again
(next calendar day)
```

### Visual Explanation
Each major region's exchanges tend to react first to news breaking during
their own session, then hand off influence to the next region opening.

## Real World Analogy
Global exchanges operating across time zones are like a 24-hour relay of
news anchors handing off a live broadcast around the world — each anchor
(regional exchange) picks up the story where the previous one left off,
adds their own region's reaction and analysis, and hands it off to the
next, so that by the time the broadcast comes back around to the first
anchor, an entire day's worth of accumulated global reaction has occurred.

## Indian Market Example
Indian traders routinely check the **previous night's US market close**
(NYSE/NASDAQ, via S&P 500 and Nasdaq Composite performance) and the
**early Asian session** (Nikkei, Hang Seng) before Nifty 50 opens at 9:15
AM IST, since both sessions' overnight moves often meaningfully shape
Indian market sentiment at the open — a habit directly connecting this
lesson to the practical, everyday workflow of Indian market participants.

## US Market Example
The **NYSE and NASDAQ**, both located in New York, represent the world's
largest and second-largest exchanges respectively by total market
capitalization of listed companies, hosting the vast majority of the
world's largest publicly traded companies by value — a scale advantage
that gives US market moves outsized global influence, a theme this module
returns to repeatedly.

## Historical Example
The **Tokyo Stock Exchange's** trajectory offers a useful long-term
historical lesson: Japan's Nikkei 225 index reached an all-time high near
38,915 in December 1989, then entered a multi-decade decline and
stagnation (Japan's so-called "Lost Decades"), only reclaiming and
surpassing that 1989 high in **2024** — a 35-year round trip illustrating
that even major, developed-market exchanges can experience extraordinarily
long structural cycles, a sobering historical data point relevant to
long-term investing assumptions (Module 08–09).

## Institutional Perspective
Global institutional investors build **regional/country allocation
frameworks** explicitly informed by exchange location, market
capitalization, liquidity, and regulatory environment — decisions about how
much capital to allocate to developed markets (US, Europe, Japan) versus
emerging markets (India, and others covered in `[04.07]`) are core,
deliberate portfolio construction decisions, not incidental outcomes.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders, especially those trading Indian markets exclusively, often
underappreciate how much overnight global exchange activity shapes their
own market's opening behavior — building the habit of checking major
global exchange closes (per the Indian Market Example above) before each
trading session is a simple, high-value addition to a daily routine.

## Professional Insights
Professional global macro traders track **exchange-specific liquidity
patterns** — for example, Japanese markets have historically shown
distinctive behavior around their fiscal year-end (March), and Chinese
mainland exchanges (Shanghai/Shenzhen) have unique characteristics due to
capital controls and a still-evolving foreign investor access framework
(via mechanisms like Stock Connect) — nuances that matter for anyone
trading or analyzing those specific markets directly, beyond just their
broad index-level performance.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating "the global stock market" as a single, homogeneous thing rather
  than a collection of distinct exchanges with their own regulatory
  regimes, participant bases, and behavioral quirks.
- Ignoring overnight developments in other time zones before a domestic
  market session begins.
- Assuming all major exchanges are equally accessible to foreign retail
  investors — access rules vary significantly (e.g., mainland Chinese
  A-shares have historically had more restricted foreign access than most
  developed market exchanges).

## Misconceptions
- **"All major exchanges move in lockstep."** While global markets show
  meaningful correlation (`[04.06]`), each exchange also has genuinely
  distinct, sometimes divergent behavior driven by local economic and
  political conditions.
- **"An exchange's location determines where its listed companies actually
  do business."** Many companies listed on a given exchange (e.g., NYSE-
  listed multinationals) generate significant revenue globally, not just
  domestically — the exchange listing location and the company's actual
  business footprint are related but distinct concepts.
- **"Older, more established exchanges always outperform newer ones."**
  Market performance depends on the underlying economy and companies, not
  an exchange's age or history — Tokyo's multi-decade stagnation despite
  being a long-established, major exchange is a clear counterexample.

## Key Takeaways
- Major global exchanges include NYSE/NASDAQ (US), LSE (UK), TSE (Japan),
  HKEX (Hong Kong), Shanghai/Shenzhen (China), and Euronext (Europe),
  alongside India's NSE/BSE.
- Global trading follows a rough time-zone relay: Asia-Pacific, then
  Europe, then the US, repeating daily.
- The US exchanges represent an outsized share of global market
  capitalization, giving US market moves disproportionate global influence.
- Access rules and market behavior vary meaningfully across exchanges —
  they are not interchangeable or uniform.

## Summary
The world's major exchanges — concentrated heavily in the US, alongside
significant hubs in Europe and Asia — form a rough time-zone relay that
shapes how information and sentiment flow around the globe each trading
day, directly influencing how Indian markets open each morning. Building a
mental map of these exchanges, their relative scale, and their sequencing
sets up the next lesson's precise focus on trading hours and session
overlaps.

## Quiz
1. What is the rough sequence of global market opens through a trading day?
   a) US, then Europe, then Asia
   b) Asia-Pacific, then Europe, then US
   c) All markets open simultaneously worldwide
   d) Europe, then US, then Asia

2. Which two exchanges are both located in New York?
   a) LSE and TSE  b) NYSE and NASDAQ  c) HKEX and Euronext  d) Shanghai and Shenzhen

3. When did Japan's Nikkei 225 first reclaim its December 1989 all-time
   high?
   a) 1995  b) 2010  c) 2024  d) It has never reclaimed that high

4. Why do US market moves tend to have outsized global influence?
   a) The US exchanges are the oldest in the world
   b) US exchanges represent an outsized share of global market capitalization
   c) All other exchanges are closed when the US trades
   d) It's a legal requirement

5. True or False: All major global exchanges move in perfect lockstep with
   each other at all times.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — The trading day relay runs Asia-Pacific, then Europe, then the US.
2. **b)** — NYSE and NASDAQ are both located in New York.
3. **c)** — The Nikkei 225 reclaimed its 1989 high in 2024, after 35 years.
4. **b)** — US exchanges' outsized share of global market cap drives their disproportionate influence.
5. **b) False** — Markets show meaningful correlation but also genuine, sometimes divergent, local behavior.
