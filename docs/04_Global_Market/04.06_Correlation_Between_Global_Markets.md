## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Correlation** measures the degree to which two markets or assets move
together, expressed on a scale from -1 (perfectly inverse — one always
moves opposite the other) to +1 (perfectly aligned — both always move
together), with 0 meaning no consistent relationship. Global equity
markets, including Nifty 50 and major US/European/Asian indices, have
generally shown **positive correlation** — tending to move in the same
broad direction, especially during major global events — though the
*strength* of this correlation varies significantly over time and across
different market regimes.

### Intuition
Markets correlate because they share common underlying drivers: global
liquidity conditions (set substantially by the US Federal Reserve, Module
06), global risk sentiment (fear vs. greed, operating across borders),
and increasingly interconnected trade, capital flows, and multinational
corporate earnings. But correlation is not fixed or perfect — country-
specific factors (an Indian election result, a company-specific scandal,
domestic policy changes) can and do cause meaningful divergence from global
trends at times.

### Mental Model
```
HIGH CORRELATION periods: major global risk-off events (a global
  recession fear, a systemic financial crisis, a pandemic shock) --
  "everything sells off together" as global capital broadly de-risks.

LOWER CORRELATION periods: calmer markets where country-specific and
  company-specific factors have more room to drive DIVERGENT
  performance between markets.
```
This is sometimes summarized as **"correlations go to 1 in a crisis"** —
a widely observed pattern where diversification benefits (assets moving
differently from each other) tend to weaken precisely when investors need
them most.

### Visual Explanation
During major global stress events, previously loosely-correlated markets
often move sharply in the same direction simultaneously; during calmer
periods, markets show more independent, idiosyncratic behavior.

### ASCII Diagram
```
CALM PERIOD (lower correlation, more idiosyncratic behavior):

  Nifty 50:     /\      /\/\        /\
  S&P 500:        \    /    \  /\  /
  Nikkei 225:   /\  \  /      \/  \/
               (each market shows its own distinct pattern)


CRISIS PERIOD (correlation spikes toward +1, "everything sells off together"):

  Nifty 50:     \
  S&P 500:        \
  Nikkei 225:       \
  DAX:                \
               (all major markets decline sharply and simultaneously,
                e.g., March 2020 COVID crash)
```

### Flowchart
```
[Is a major global risk event occurring (recession fear, systemic
 financial stress, pandemic, major geopolitical shock)?]
   Yes -> [Expect HIGHER correlation across global markets --
           diversification benefits weaken; most markets likely
           move in the same direction]
   No  -> [Expect relatively LOWER correlation -- more room for
           country/company-specific factors to drive divergent
           performance]
```

## Real World Analogy
Global market correlation is like a group of boats on the open ocean.
During calm weather, each boat's captain makes independent decisions,
following their own course based on their own destination and conditions
(idiosyncratic, lower correlation). But during a major storm, every boat,
regardless of the captain's individual skill or destination, gets tossed
by the same massive waves simultaneously — the storm (a global crisis)
overwhelms individual differences and forces highly correlated movement
across all the boats at once.

## Indian Market Example
During the **March 2020 COVID crash**, Nifty 50 fell roughly 38% from its
January 2020 high to its March 2020 low, closely tracking the timing and
severity of declines in the S&P 500, Nikkei 225, DAX, and virtually every
other major global index simultaneously — a textbook example of the
"correlations go to 1 in a crisis" pattern, driven by a shared global
shock (pandemic-driven economic shutdown fears) overwhelming any
country-specific differences.

## US Market Example
Conversely, during calmer periods — such as much of 2023, when Indian
markets showed relative resilience partly due to strong domestic DII flows
(`[03.07]`) even as global markets showed mixed performance — Nifty 50 and
the S&P 500 displayed more divergent, less tightly correlated behavior,
illustrating that correlation strength genuinely varies by market regime
rather than remaining fixed.

## Historical Example
The **2008 Global Financial Crisis** (covered in full case-study depth in
`[31.01]`) offers perhaps the most dramatic historical illustration of
global correlation spiking during systemic crisis — virtually every major
global equity market, including Sensex (falling from roughly 21,000 in
January 2008 to around 8,000 by late 2008/early 2009), declined sharply
and roughly simultaneously, as the crisis originated in the US banking
system but rapidly transmitted globally through interconnected capital
flows, trade linkages, and shared risk sentiment.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional portfolio managers explicitly model and monitor
**cross-market correlation** as a core input to diversification strategy
(Module 27) — understanding that relying on geographic diversification
alone (e.g., holding both US and Indian equities) provides less protection
during a genuine global crisis than during calmer periods, and building
additional diversification (across asset classes, not just geographies)
to address this limitation.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors sometimes assume international diversification (owning
both Indian and US/global stocks) provides substantial protection during
any market downturn, without realizing that this protection is
meaningfully weaker specifically during the large, systemic crises when
protection would matter most — an important, sometimes counterintuitive
nuance for anyone building a genuinely risk-aware portfolio (Module 27).

## Professional Insights
Professional quantitative analysts often track **rolling correlation
statistics** (correlation calculated over a moving recent window, e.g., the
past 60 or 90 trading days) rather than relying on a single, static
historical correlation figure — since, as this lesson emphasizes,
correlation is dynamic and regime-dependent, not a fixed constant.

## Common Mistakes
- Assuming a fixed, unchanging correlation figure applies at all times,
  rather than recognizing correlation as dynamic and regime-dependent.
- Believing international diversification alone fully protects a portfolio
  during any downturn, without accounting for correlation spiking during
  genuine systemic crises.
- Ignoring correlation entirely when constructing a portfolio, missing
  valuable diversification opportunities available during calmer periods.

## Misconceptions
- **"Global markets are either always correlated or never correlated."**
  Correlation is a matter of degree and varies meaningfully across time and
  market regime — it's neither a constant 0 nor a constant 1.
- **"Diversification is useless since correlations spike during crises
  anyway."** Diversification still provides meaningful benefit during
  calmer periods and can reduce (though not eliminate) losses even during
  crises, particularly when diversifying across asset classes (equities,
  bonds, commodities, per `[01.03]`) rather than just across geographies.
- **"India is now fully immune to global market correlation due to strong
  domestic flows."** Strong DII flows (`[03.07]`) have provided a
  meaningful buffer in some periods, but India remains subject to
  correlation spikes during genuine severe global systemic crises, as March
  2020 demonstrated even with a growing domestic investor base.

## Key Takeaways
- Correlation measures the degree to which markets move together, ranging
  from -1 to +1, and is dynamic, not fixed.
- Global markets tend to show higher correlation during major crises
  ("correlations go to 1") and lower, more idiosyncratic correlation during
  calmer periods.
- Geographic diversification alone provides weaker protection specifically
  during the large systemic crises when protection matters most.
- Both the March 2020 COVID crash and the 2008 Global Financial Crisis
  illustrate correlation spiking during genuine global systemic stress.

## Summary
Global markets, including Nifty 50, show meaningful but dynamic
correlation with other major indices — generally positive, and
particularly strong during major systemic crises, when "correlations go to
1" and diversification benefits weaken precisely when they're needed most.
Understanding this regime-dependent nature of correlation is essential for
realistic portfolio construction (Module 27) and sets up the final two
lessons of this module: the emerging-versus-developed market distinction,
and how to read a global macro calendar.

## Quiz
1. What does a correlation of +1 between two markets mean?
   a) They always move in opposite directions
   b) They move perfectly together in the same direction
   c) There's no relationship between them
   d) One market causes the other

2. What does "correlations go to 1 in a crisis" describe?
   a) Markets become completely unrelated during crises
   b) Previously less-correlated markets tend to move together sharply during major stress
   c) Only bond markets are affected during crises
   d) Correlation is always exactly 1 at all times

3. Which historical event is a clear example of global correlation spiking?
   a) The 2021 Zomato IPO
   b) The March 2020 COVID crash
   c) The 1875 founding of BSE
   d) A routine, calm trading day

4. What is a key limitation of relying solely on geographic diversification?
   a) It always fully protects against any downturn
   b) It provides weaker protection specifically during major systemic crises
   c) It's illegal in India
   d) It has no limitations at all

5. True or False: Correlation between global markets is a fixed constant
   that never changes.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — A correlation of +1 means two markets move perfectly together in the same direction.
2. **b)** — This describes previously less-correlated markets moving together sharply during crises.
3. **b)** — The March 2020 COVID crash is a clear, dramatic example of global correlation spiking.
4. **b)** — Geographic diversification alone provides weaker protection specifically during major systemic crises.
5. **b) False** — Correlation is dynamic and regime-dependent, varying over time.
