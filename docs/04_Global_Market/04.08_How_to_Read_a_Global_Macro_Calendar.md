## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **global macro calendar** (also called an economic calendar) is a
scheduled listing of upcoming economic data releases, central bank
decisions, and other market-moving events across major economies, usually
organized by date, time, country, and expected market impact ("high,"
"medium," "low"). Key recurring high-impact events include: **US Federal
Reserve FOMC meetings**, **US Non-Farm Payrolls (jobs report)**, **US CPI
(inflation) data**, **RBI MPC meetings** (`[03.10]`), **India's CPI and
IIP data**, and major **corporate earnings season** windows.

### Intuition
Markets are forward-looking (a theme recurring throughout this module and
developed fully in Modules 05–06), meaning prices already reflect
*expected* outcomes for known, scheduled events. This is why the actual
market reaction to a scheduled event depends far more on how the outcome
compares to **consensus expectations** than on whether the outcome is
"good" or "bad" in some absolute sense — a concept introduced concretely in
`[03.10]` and formalized here as a general macro-calendar-reading skill.

### Mental Model
```
BEFORE the event: market has already priced in a CONSENSUS expectation
  (e.g., "analysts expect US CPI at 3.2% year-over-year")

AT the event: actual data is released and compared to consensus
  Actual = Consensus     --> often muted reaction ("priced in")
  Actual > Consensus     --> often triggers repricing (direction
                              depends on what a "higher" reading
                              implies -- e.g., higher inflation is
                              often read as hawkish/negative for
                              risk assets)
  Actual < Consensus     --> often triggers the opposite repricing
```

### Visual Explanation
A simple weekly routine — checking the calendar for the days ahead every
Sunday or Monday — builds macro-awareness into a trader's regular workflow
rather than being caught off guard by scheduled events.

### ASCII Diagram


### Flowchart
```
[Start of trading week]
        |
        v
[Check global macro calendar for scheduled high-impact events
 this week: Fed/RBI meetings, major inflation/jobs data, earnings]
        |
        v

        |

        |

 market view based on the SURPRISE, not the raw number alone]
```

## Real World Analogy
Reading a macro calendar is like knowing a sports team's full season
schedule in advance, including which games are against tough rivals (high
impact) versus weaker opponents (low impact) — a well-prepared fan (trader)
plans their attention and expectations around the big games specifically,
rather than being equally surprised by every single game regardless of its
actual significance.

## Indian Market Example
Indian traders build their weekly routine around events like the **RBI MPC
meeting calendar** (`[03.10]`), **India's monthly CPI inflation release**,
**IIP (Index of Industrial Production) data**, and, during the relevant
season, **quarterly corporate earnings releases** for major Nifty 50
constituents — treating each as a scheduled, anticipatable event requiring
deliberate pre-event risk planning rather than a random surprise.

## US Market Example
US markets organize substantial trading activity around the **FOMC
meeting calendar** (eight scheduled meetings per year), the **monthly
Non-Farm Payrolls report** (typically released the first Friday of each
month), and **monthly CPI data** — each with well-established historical
patterns of elevated volatility in the surrounding hours, patterns
professional traders explicitly plan around using the same consensus-
versus-actual framework taught in this lesson.

## Historical Example
The **May 2022 RBI surprise rate hike** (`[03.10]`) is a particularly
instructive historical example specifically *because* it was an
**off-calendar, unscheduled** event — most macro calendar events are
scheduled well in advance, allowing pre-event positioning, but this
episode illustrates that central banks retain the ability to act outside
the normal calendar during periods of acute stress, an important reminder
that a macro calendar reduces, but never eliminates, event-driven
surprise risk.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional trading desks maintain dedicated **event risk calendars**
integrated directly into their position sizing and risk management
systems (Module 25–26) — automatically flagging positions that carry
elevated exposure heading into a known high-impact event, and often
systematically reducing risk or hedging ahead of the highest-impact,
most binary events (like FOMC or RBI decisions) rather than relying on
manual, ad-hoc awareness.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders who don't check a macro calendar are frequently caught off
guard by sudden volatility that professional and institutional
participants had fully anticipated and positioned for in advance — building
the simple, low-effort habit of a weekly calendar check (illustrated in
this lesson's ASCII diagram) meaningfully closes this awareness gap at
minimal cost.

## Professional Insights
Professional macro traders specifically distinguish between **"priced in"**
events (where consensus expectations are so well-established that even a
confirmed outcome produces minimal market reaction) and **genuine
surprises** (where actual results diverge meaningfully from consensus,
triggering sharp repricing) — and use the size of the *implied volatility*
priced into options markets (previewed for full treatment in Module 23)
ahead of major events as a real-time gauge of how much uncertainty the
market currently attaches to a given upcoming release.

## Common Mistakes
- Not checking the macro calendar at all, being repeatedly surprised by
  predictable, scheduled volatility.
- Reacting to a data release's raw number without checking what the
  consensus expectation actually was beforehand.
- Treating every calendar event as equally important, rather than
  distinguishing genuinely high-impact events (Fed/RBI decisions, major
  jobs/inflation data) from lower-impact, routine releases.

## Misconceptions
- **"A macro calendar lets you predict exactly how the market will react."**
  It tells you *when* elevated volatility is likely and *what* consensus
  expectations are — it does not predict the actual outcome or guarantee a
  specific market reaction, which still depends on the surprise element and
  broader context.
- **"Only professional traders need to track macro events."** Any trader
  or investor holding positions through a high-impact event window is
  exposed to that event's risk, regardless of their sophistication level —
  awareness benefits everyone, not just professionals.
- **"Off-calendar surprises like May 2022's RBI hike make calendar-tracking
  pointless."** Scheduled events remain the vast majority of significant
  macro catalysts; off-calendar surprises are the exception that proves the
  rule, not evidence the entire practice is futile.

## Key Takeaways
- A global macro calendar tracks scheduled high-impact events: Fed/RBI
  decisions, major inflation/jobs data, and earnings season.
- Market reaction depends primarily on the outcome relative to consensus
  expectations, not the raw outcome in isolation.
- A simple weekly calendar-check habit meaningfully reduces the risk of
  being blindsided by predictable, scheduled volatility.
- Off-calendar surprises (like May 2022's RBI hike) remain possible and
  are a reminder that calendar awareness reduces, but never eliminates,
  event risk.

## Summary
Building the habit of checking a global macro calendar — noting scheduled
high-impact events, their consensus expectations, and adjusting risk
accordingly — is one of the highest-leverage, lowest-effort practices a
trader at any level can adopt, directly applying the "surprise relative to
expectations" framework introduced in `[03.10]` to the full global
calendar. This completes Module 04's tour of global market structure,
exchanges, hours, US-India linkages, currencies, indices, correlation, and
market classification — grounding you for Module 05's deeper dive into the
economics that drive all of these events in the first place.

   b) The outcome relative to consensus expectations
   c) The day of the week
   d) The color of the release document

   a) The Union Budget
   b) FOMC meetings
   c) Diwali trading holidays
   d) NSE circuit filter reviews

4. What does the May 2022 RBI surprise rate hike illustrate about macro
   calendars?
   a) Calendars are completely useless
   b) Off-calendar surprises remain possible even with good calendar awareness
   c) RBI never acts outside its scheduled calendar
   d) Only the Fed can surprise markets

5. True or False: A macro calendar guarantees you can predict the exact
   market reaction to every event.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — A macro calendar lists scheduled high-impact economic events.
2. **b)** — Market reaction depends primarily on the outcome relative to consensus expectations.
3. **b)** — FOMC meetings are a recurring high-impact US macro calendar event.
4. **b)** — It shows off-calendar surprises remain possible despite calendar awareness.
5. **b) False** — A calendar shows timing/expectations, not a guaranteed prediction of reaction.
