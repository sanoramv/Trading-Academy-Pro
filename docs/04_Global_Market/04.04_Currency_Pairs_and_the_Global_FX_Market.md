## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **currency pair** (e.g., **USD/INR**) expresses the value of one currency
(the **base currency**, listed first) in terms of another (the **quote
currency**, listed second). USD/INR at 83.00 means 1 US Dollar equals 83.00
Indian Rupees. The **foreign exchange (FX) market** is the global,
decentralized, over-the-counter (recall `[01.04]`'s OTC concept) market
where currencies are traded, and by traded value, it is by far the
**largest financial market in the world**, dwarfing global equity market
trading volume.

### Intuition
Currencies need a market because virtually all international trade,
investment, and travel requires converting one currency into another. An
Indian company importing oil priced in US dollars needs to convert rupees
into dollars; a US investor buying Indian stocks needs to convert dollars
into rupees. The sheer scale and constancy of this global need — happening
24 hours a day across every time zone (extending `[04.02]`'s session
overlap concept to its most continuous form) — makes FX the largest, most
liquid market in the world.

### Mental Model
```
USD/INR = 83.00 means: 1 USD = 83.00 INR

If USD/INR RISES (e.g., 83.00 --> 84.00): the Rupee has WEAKENED
  (it now takes MORE rupees to buy one dollar)

If USD/INR FALLS (e.g., 83.00 --> 82.00): the Rupee has STRENGTHENED
  (it now takes FEWER rupees to buy one dollar)
```

### Visual Explanation
The US Dollar's role as the world's primary reserve and trade-invoicing
currency means USD-paired quotes (USD/INR, EUR/USD, USD/JPY) dominate
global FX trading volume and macro discussion.

## Real World Analogy
Currency pairs are like a seesaw between two currencies — one side going up
necessarily means the other side is going down, since a currency pair only
measures *relative* value, not absolute value. If USD/INR rises, it's not
that "the dollar became more valuable in some absolute sense" — it's that
the dollar became more valuable *specifically relative to the rupee*,
which could happen because the dollar strengthened, the rupee weakened, or
some combination of both.

## Indian Market Example
The **USD/INR exchange rate** is one of the most closely watched macro
indicators by Indian traders and policymakers, since rupee weakness
directly raises import costs (particularly crude oil, one of India's
largest import categories, priced in dollars) and affects inflation, while
also affecting the reported rupee-denominated earnings of Indian IT
companies (which earn substantial revenue in dollars) — a currency move
with genuinely wide-reaching effects across the Indian economy and market,
as previewed in `[01.03]`'s Taper Tantrum example.

## US Market Example
The **US Dollar Index (DXY)**, which measures the dollar's value against a
basket of major currencies (Euro, Yen, Pound, and others), is closely
watched by US and global traders as a broad gauge of dollar strength — a
rising DXY often coincides with pressure on emerging market currencies
(including the rupee) and commodities priced in dollars (like gold and
oil), illustrating the dollar's central, connective role across global
asset classes.

## Historical Example
The **2013 Taper Tantrum** (`[01.03]`, `[02.08]`, `[03.07]`) remains one of
the clearest historical illustrations of currency market dynamics
affecting India directly: the mere signal of reduced US bond-buying caused
the US dollar to strengthen broadly, and the Indian rupee depreciated
sharply — from around ₹55–56/USD to over ₹68/USD by August 2013 — directly
illustrating how US monetary policy expectations transmit through the FX
market into direct, significant effects on Indian import costs, inflation,
and equity market sentiment.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutions — particularly banks, which historically dominate FX market
making — trade currencies not only for client conversion needs but for
proprietary macro speculation, carry trades (borrowing in a low-interest-
rate currency to invest in a higher-interest-rate currency, an advanced
topic connecting to Module 06's interest rate coverage), and hedging
cross-border investment exposure — the FX market's institutional structure
is dominated by a relatively small number of very large banks acting as
primary liquidity providers, a structurally different participant mix than
equity markets.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders in India can access currency derivatives (like USD/INR
futures) on NSE's currency derivatives segment, though direct retail spot
FX trading of pairs like EUR/USD is generally not permitted for Indian
residents under RBI's foreign exchange regulations (FEMA) outside specific
regulated channels — an important compliance distinction for Indian
traders curious about FX trading, worth understanding before assuming all
global FX products are equally accessible.

## Professional Insights
Professional FX traders pay close attention to **interest rate
differentials** between two currencies (a core driver of currency value
covered more fully in Module 06) — generally, a currency offering higher
interest rates tends to attract capital seeking yield, all else equal,
supporting that currency's relative value, though this relationship can be
overwhelmed by other factors (risk sentiment, current account balances,
political stability) during periods of stress, as the 2013 Taper Tantrum
demonstrated.

## Common Mistakes
- Confusing which currency is the base versus the quote in a pair, leading
  to backwards interpretation of whether a currency strengthened or
  weakened.
- Assuming retail Indian traders can freely trade any global spot FX pair
  without understanding RBI/FEMA restrictions on permitted currency
  trading channels.
- Treating USD/INR moves as purely a "rupee story," ignoring that broad
  dollar strength/weakness (affecting many currencies simultaneously) is
  often the primary driver, not India-specific factors alone.

## Misconceptions
- **"A weakening currency is always bad for a country."** A weaker currency
  can benefit export-oriented sectors (making a country's goods cheaper for
  foreign buyers) even while raising import costs — the net effect depends
  on a country's specific trade structure and is genuinely mixed, not
  uniformly negative.
- **"The FX market has a central exchange like NSE or NYSE."** The global
  spot FX market is predominantly over-the-counter (OTC), traded directly
  between banks and other participants rather than through a single
  centralized exchange, though exchange-traded currency derivatives (like
  NSE's USD/INR futures) do exist alongside this OTC structure.
- **"USD/INR only reflects India-specific factors."** It reflects the
  relative strength of both currencies — broad dollar strength/weakness
  (driven by US-specific factors like Fed policy) is often as important a
  driver as India-specific factors.

## Key Takeaways
- A currency pair expresses the base currency's value in terms of the
  quote currency; USD/INR rising means the rupee is weakening.
- The FX market is the largest financial market in the world by traded
  value, operating OTC across global banks.
- USD/INR is a critical Indian macro indicator, affecting import costs,
  inflation, and IT sector earnings.
- Indian retail FX access is regulated under RBI/FEMA rules, distinct from
  unrestricted global spot FX access in some other countries.

## Summary
Currency pairs, and the vast global FX market underlying them, connect
every other asset class and every national economy together — a rising
USD/INR directly affects Indian import costs, inflation, and corporate
earnings, making currency literacy essential even for traders who never
directly trade FX instruments. Understanding base/quote currency mechanics
and the dollar's central global role sets up the next lesson's broader
survey of global equity indices.

## Quiz
1. In USD/INR, which currency is the base currency?
   a) INR  b) USD  c) Neither  d) Both equally

2. What does a rising USD/INR indicate?
   a) The rupee is strengthening  b) The rupee is weakening  c) No change occurred  d) The dollar has been abolished

3. Is the global spot FX market centralized on a single exchange like NSE?
   a) Yes, always  b) No — it's predominantly OTC, traded between banks  c) Only on weekends  d) Only in India

4. What historical event clearly illustrates US policy driving rupee
   weakness via the FX market?
   a) The 2021 Zomato IPO  b) The 2013 Taper Tantrum  c) The 1875 founding of BSE  d) The 2023 T+1 transition

5. True or False: A weakening currency is always unambiguously bad for a
   country's economy.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — USD is the base currency in USD/INR.
2. **b)** — A rising USD/INR means it takes more rupees to buy a dollar — the rupee is weakening.
3. **b)** — The global spot FX market is predominantly OTC, not centralized on a single exchange.
4. **b)** — The 2013 Taper Tantrum is the lesson's clearest example of this transmission mechanism.
5. **b) False** — Currency weakness has mixed effects, potentially benefiting exporters while raising import costs.
