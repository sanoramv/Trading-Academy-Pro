## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **portfolio of systems** runs multiple distinct trading strategies
simultaneously, allocating capital across them deliberately, with the
specific goal of reducing overall equity curve volatility by combining
strategies whose returns are not highly correlated with each other. This
is distinct from simply "knowing several strategies" — it requires an
explicit capital allocation across strategies and awareness of how each
strategy's return pattern relates to the others', mirroring
diversification principles Module 27 develops fully at the portfolio level.

### Intuition
`[24.02]`'s trend-following and `[24.03]`'s mean-reversion have
structurally opposite performance patterns: trend-following performs
best in strongly trending markets and struggles in choppy ranges, while
mean-reversion performs best in genuine ranges and struggles in strong
trends. Running both simultaneously, sized appropriately, means one
strategy's weak period often coincides with the other's strong period —
smoothing the combined equity curve more than either strategy would
achieve alone.

```
  Breakout [24.04]  <-SIMILAR TO->  Momentum/Positional [24.08]
    (both benefit from the SAME "underlying keeps moving" condition)

SIMPLE ALLOCATION FRAMEWORK:
  1. List candidate strategies + their typical market-condition fit
  2. Identify which pairs are COMPLEMENTARY (different condition fit)
     vs. DUPLICATIVE (same condition fit)
  3. Allocate capital across complementary strategies, not
     concentrated in one, and not spread evenly across duplicates
  4. Track EACH strategy's expectancy [24.01] separately, plus the
     COMBINED portfolio's overall equity curve
```

individual strategy's curve in isolation.

## Real World Analogy
A portfolio of systems is like a restaurant running both an indoor
dining room and an outdoor patio: on a rainy day the patio underperforms
but the dining room fills up, and on a beautiful day the reverse
happens — the *combined* business is steadier than either section
would be if it were the restaurant's only offering, precisely because
the two sections respond oppositely to the same underlying condition
(weather).

## Indian Market Example
A trader runs a Nifty trend-following system (`[24.02]`) alongside a
weekly options theta-selling program (`[24.09]`), allocating capital to
both — during a strongly trending Nifty rally, the trend-following
system captures the move while the theta-selling program may see
smaller, more frequent gains from the calmer implied volatility a
steady uptrend often brings; during a choppy, range-bound period, the
theta-selling program tends to perform more consistently while the
trend-following system experiences more whipsaw losses.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
An investor combines a positional/momentum equity strategy (`[24.08]`)
with a defined-risk options income strategy (`[24.09]`) on a broad
index, tracking each strategy's individual expectancy while also
monitoring how their combined equity curve behaves across different
market regimes (`[23.05]`) over multiple years. **Hypothetical Example**
for illustration only.

## Historical Example
The "risk parity" and multi-strategy fund structures popularized by
firms like Bridgewater Associates from the 1990s onward institutionalized
this lesson's core principle at scale — deliberately combining
strategies and asset classes with low return correlation to produce a
smoother overall portfolio outcome than any single strategy could
achieve alone, a principle Module 27 (Portfolio Management) develops
fully at the broader asset-allocation level.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional multi-strategy funds formally track the correlation
matrix between their constituent strategies' returns, actively adjusting
capital allocation away from strategies whose correlation to the rest
of the portfolio has drifted upward over time — treating correlation
itself, not just individual strategy performance, as an ongoing
monitoring input.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders often unintentionally run several *correlated* strategies
simultaneously (e.g., multiple momentum-style approaches across
different stocks) believing they've diversified, when in fact all their
strategies tend to lose money in the same type of market condition —
this lesson's complementary-versus-duplicative distinction directly
addresses that common oversight.

## Professional Insights
Professional multi-strategy managers caution that correlation between
strategies is not static — two strategies that were historically
uncorrelated can become correlated during genuine market stress (a
phenomenon sometimes called "correlation going to one" during crises),
meaning diversification benefits can shrink precisely when they are
needed most.

## Common Mistakes
- Combining strategies that appear different in name or technique but
  actually perform well under the same market conditions (duplicative,
  not complementary).
- Allocating capital evenly across strategies without considering their
  actual correlation or relative expectancy strength.
- Assuming a historically low correlation between two strategies will
  hold during a genuine crisis period.

## Misconceptions
- **"Running more strategies always means better diversification."**
  Only if the strategies are genuinely complementary (different
  condition fit) — duplicative strategies add limited benefit.
- **"A portfolio of systems eliminates drawdowns entirely."** It
  smooths and reduces drawdown severity on average, but does not
  eliminate the possibility of a broad, correlated stress period
  affecting most strategies simultaneously.
- **"Correlation between strategies is fixed and permanent."** It can
  and does shift over time, particularly during crisis periods —
  requiring ongoing monitoring, not a one-time assessment.

## Key Takeaways
- A portfolio of systems deliberately combines multiple strategies with
  low return correlation to smooth the overall equity curve.
- Complementary pairs (different market-condition fit, e.g., trend-
  following + mean-reversion) diversify more effectively than
  duplicative pairs (same condition fit).
- Track each strategy's individual expectancy (`[24.01]`) alongside the
  combined portfolio's overall performance.
- Correlation between strategies can shift over time, especially during
  crises — ongoing monitoring is required, not a one-time setup.

## Summary
Combining multiple, genuinely complementary strategies — ones that
perform well under different market conditions rather than the same
ones — into a deliberately allocated portfolio of systems smooths the
overall equity curve more effectively than running any single strategy
from this module alone, a principle institutionalized at scale by
multi-strategy funds and directly connected to Module 27's broader
portfolio diversification framework. The final lesson in this module
turns to strategy journaling — the discipline of tracking and iterating
on both individual strategies and the combined portfolio over time.

## Quiz
1. What makes two strategies "complementary"?
   a) They have the same name  b) They perform well under different market conditions  c) They both use options  d) They were both mentioned in this module

2. Why combine strategies with low return correlation?
   a) It guarantees higher returns  b) It smooths the overall equity curve  c) It has no real benefit  d) It's required by SEBI

3. Does the correlation between two strategies always stay the same over time?
   a) Yes, always fixed  b) No, it can shift, especially during crises  c) Only for options strategies  d) Correlation is irrelevant to trading

4. What should be tracked alongside the combined portfolio's performance?
   a) Nothing else is needed  b) Each individual strategy's own expectancy  c) Only the largest strategy  d) Only recent trades

5. True or False: Combining any two different-sounding strategies automatically improves diversification.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — They perform well under different market conditions.
2. **b)** — It smooths the overall equity curve by offsetting weak periods.
3. **b)** — No, correlation can shift, particularly rising during crisis periods.
4. **b)** — Each individual strategy's own expectancy, alongside combined performance.
5. **b) False** — Strategies must have genuinely low return correlation, not just different names, to diversify effectively.
