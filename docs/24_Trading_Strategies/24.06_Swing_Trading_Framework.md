## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Swing trading** holds positions for several days to several weeks,
aiming to capture a single "swing" — one leg of a larger trend or one
full move within a range — rather than either the very short intraday
moves scalping targets (`[24.07]`) or the multi-month-to-multi-year
holds positional investing targets (`[24.08]`). A complete swing trading
framework specifies: a higher-timeframe bias (daily/weekly, `[02.08]`),
a lower-timeframe entry trigger (e.g., a 1-hour or 4-hour pattern
aligned with that bias), a stop-loss sized for multi-day volatility, and
a target based on the next significant structural level.

### Intuition
Swing trading exploits the practical reality that most traders cannot
watch markets continuously throughout the day but can check charts once
or twice daily — the multi-timeframe framework (`[02.08]`) lets a swing
trader use a higher timeframe to establish directional bias (checked
infrequently) while using a lower timeframe only for entry timing
(checked briefly), balancing thoroughness against the time commitment
intraday strategies (`[24.07]`) require.

### Mental Model
```
SWING TRADING FRAMEWORK:
  1. HIGHER TIMEFRAME BIAS (Daily/Weekly): Identify overall trend/range
     context, [02.08]'s three-tier bias/setup/entry-timing model
  2. LOWER TIMEFRAME SETUP (4H/1H): Identify a specific pattern aligned
     with the higher-timeframe bias (e.g., pullback to support in an
     uptrend, [13.05])
  3. ENTRY TRIGGER: A confirming signal on the lower timeframe
     (e.g., bullish engulfing at support, [11.04])
  4. STOP-LOSS: Sized to accommodate normal multi-day volatility --
     typically wider than an intraday strategy's stop
  5. TARGET: Next significant structural level (prior swing high/low,
     [02.07]) -- typically held for DAYS to WEEKS
```

### Visual Explanation
A swing trade's chart typically shows a clear higher-timeframe trend or
range context, with the actual entry marked by a smaller, lower-
timeframe pattern nested within a pullback or retracement — the trade
is then held through the position's natural day-to-day noise, exiting
only once the higher-timeframe target or invalidation level is reached.

## Real World Analogy
Swing trading is like planning a multi-day road trip using both a
country-wide map (higher timeframe bias — which general direction to
head) and a local street map only when navigating the actual turns near
each stop (lower timeframe entry timing) — a traveler doesn't need to
watch every meter of road continuously, only check in periodically at
key decision points.

## Indian Market Example
A swing trader identifies Nifty in a clear daily uptrend (`[02.08]`
bias), then waits for a pullback to the daily 20-EMA, zooming into the
1-hour chart to find a bullish reversal candle at that level before
entering — holding the position for one to two weeks toward the prior
swing high as a target. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A swing trader identifies a US stock consolidating just below a
multi-month resistance level on the daily chart, then monitors the
4-hour chart for a volume-confirmed breakout trigger (`[24.04]`) before
entering, planning to hold for several weeks if the breakout continues,
consistent with swing trading's characteristic holding period.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
Swing trading as a distinct, named discipline gained particular
prominence in retail trading education literature from the 1990s
onward, popularized by authors such as Alexander Elder ("Trading for a
Living," 1993) and Oliver Velez, whose multi-timeframe approaches
directly parallel the framework this lesson describes.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional swing-style strategies exist within broader systematic
frameworks (Module 29/30 previewed) as one holding-period bucket among
several — often run alongside faster and slower strategies
simultaneously to diversify a fund's overall return stream across
different time horizons (`[24.11]` previewed).

## Retail Perspective
Swing trading is frequently recommended for retail traders who hold
full-time jobs, since it doesn't require continuous intraday monitoring
(unlike scalping, `[24.07]`) while still offering more frequent trading
opportunities than long-term positional investing (`[24.08]`) —
practical, lifestyle-driven considerations matter directly in choosing
between strategy styles.

## Professional Insights
Professional swing traders emphasize that the higher-timeframe bias is
the dominant filter — a compelling lower-timeframe entry signal that
contradicts the higher-timeframe bias is generally a lower-quality
trade than a similar signal aligned with it, even if the lower-timeframe
pattern looks identical in isolation.

## Common Mistakes
- Entering on a lower-timeframe signal without checking higher-timeframe
  alignment first, effectively trading against the larger context.
- Setting a stop-loss too tight for the multi-day holding period,
  getting stopped out by normal daily noise before the swing develops.
- Checking charts too frequently during the hold, leading to premature
  exits driven by short-term fluctuations irrelevant to the swing thesis.

## Misconceptions
- **"Swing trading requires watching charts all day."** Its defining
  practical advantage is that it does NOT require continuous intraday
  monitoring, unlike scalping.
- **"A lower-timeframe entry signal is sufficient on its own."** Without
  higher-timeframe bias alignment (`[02.08]`), a lower-timeframe signal
  carries meaningfully lower reliability.
- **"Swing trading and positional investing are the same thing."** They
  differ meaningfully in typical holding period (days-to-weeks versus
  months-to-years) and entry frequency.

## Key Takeaways
- Swing trading holds positions for days to weeks, combining a higher-
  timeframe bias with a lower-timeframe entry trigger (`[02.08]`).
- The higher-timeframe bias is the dominant filter — align lower-
  timeframe entries with it, not against it.
- Practical/lifestyle factors (not requiring continuous monitoring)
  make swing trading well-suited to traders with limited intraday
  availability.
- Popularized in modern retail trading education by figures like
  Alexander Elder from the 1990s onward.

## Summary
Swing trading combines a higher-timeframe directional bias with a
lower-timeframe entry trigger to capture single, multi-day-to-multi-
week price swings — a practical middle ground between the continuous
monitoring scalping requires (`[24.07]`) and the infrequent activity of
long-term positional investing (`[24.08]`). The next lesson turns to
scalping — the fastest-paced framework in this module, requiring
continuous, active intraday attention.

## Quiz
1. What is the typical holding period for a swing trade?
   a) Seconds to minutes  b) Several days to several weeks  c) Several years  d) One trading session only

2. What two timeframes does a swing trading framework typically combine?
   a) Only one timeframe  b) A higher timeframe for bias and a lower timeframe for entry timing  c) Two identical timeframes  d) Only tick charts

3. Which timeframe should dominate as the filter in swing trading?
   a) The lower timeframe alone  b) The higher timeframe bias  c) Neither matters  d) Only news headlines

4. Why is swing trading often recommended for traders with full-time jobs?
   a) It requires constant monitoring  b) It doesn't require continuous intraday monitoring  c) It's illegal otherwise  d) It guarantees profit

5. True or False: A lower-timeframe entry signal is sufficient without checking the higher-timeframe bias.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Several days to several weeks.
2. **b)** — A higher timeframe for bias and a lower timeframe for entry timing.
3. **b)** — The higher timeframe bias is the dominant filter.
4. **b)** — It doesn't require continuous intraday monitoring.
5. **b) False** — Higher-timeframe alignment meaningfully improves signal reliability.
