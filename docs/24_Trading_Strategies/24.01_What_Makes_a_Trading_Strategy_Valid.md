# [24.01] What Makes a Trading Strategy "Valid"

> Module: Trading Strategies · Lesson 1 of 12 · Estimated time: 30 minutes

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the essential components every valid strategy must specify.
- Understand positive expectancy and why win rate alone is meaningless without it.
- Recognize the difference between a strategy that "worked once" and one that is statistically valid.

## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **trading strategy** is a complete, testable set of rules specifying:
entry conditions, exit conditions (both profit target and stop-loss),
position sizing method, and the market/timeframe conditions under which
it applies. A strategy is considered **valid** not because it produces
winning trades, but because it has **positive expectancy** — the
average expected profit per trade, accounting for both win rate and
the size of wins versus losses, is greater than zero over a
statistically meaningful sample of trades, not just a handful of
favorable examples.

### Intuition
"Buy when RSI is oversold" is not a strategy — it's a fragment. A real
strategy must answer: oversold on which timeframe, in which broader
market context, with what stop-loss, what profit target, and how much
capital risked per trade? Without every component specified in advance,
there's no way to test the idea objectively or apply it consistently —
and without positive expectancy, even a strategy with a high win rate
can lose money if losses are disproportionately large.

### Mental Model
```
EXPECTANCY FORMULA:
  Expectancy = (Win Rate x Average Win) - (Loss Rate x Average Loss)

  Example A: 70% win rate, Avg Win = 1, Avg Loss = 3
    Expectancy = (0.70 x 1) - (0.30 x 3) = 0.70 - 0.90 = -0.20 (NEGATIVE)
    -> A HIGH win rate strategy that LOSES money on average

  Example B: 40% win rate, Avg Win = 3, Avg Loss = 1
    Expectancy = (0.40 x 3) - (0.60 x 1) = 1.20 - 0.60 = +0.60 (POSITIVE)
    -> A LOW win rate strategy that MAKES money on average

REQUIRED STRATEGY COMPONENTS:
  1. Entry conditions (specific, objective, repeatable)
  2. Exit conditions (BOTH profit target AND stop-loss)
  3. Position sizing method (Module 26 previewed)
  4. Applicable market/timeframe conditions (when NOT to use it)
```

### Visual Explanation
Two equity curves can look identical in total profit over a backtest
period, but one comes from a high-win-rate, small-win/large-loss
strategy (choppy, with occasional sharp drawdowns) while the other
comes from a low-win-rate, large-win/small-loss strategy (steadier,
more frequent small dips) — expectancy explains the underlying math
behind both shapes, something win rate alone cannot.

## Real World Analogy
Judging a strategy by win rate alone is like judging a insurance
company's underwriting purely by how often policyholders *don't* file a
claim — a company could approve policies that rarely pay out small
claims but occasionally face one catastrophic payout large enough to
erase years of premium income; sound underwriting (like sound trading)
requires weighing frequency *and* magnitude together.

## Indian Market Example
A trader backtests a Nifty strategy: buying when price closes above the
20-day moving average (`[10.06]`) with a 1% stop-loss and 3% target,
tested across 200 historical signals over several years, finding a 45%
win rate but positive expectancy due to the 3:1 reward-to-risk ratio
(`[25.04]` previewed) — a specified, testable framework, unlike a vague
"buy strength" impulse. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader backtests an SPY breakout strategy (`[24.04]` previewed) across
five years of daily data, discovering that while the strategy wins only
38% of the time, its average winning trade is nearly triple its average
losing trade — yielding positive expectancy despite the sub-50% win
rate, directly illustrating this lesson's core formula.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
The systematic, rules-based approach to strategy validation this lesson
describes owes much to the broader rise of quantitative and systematic
trading from the 1970s–1980s onward (Module 29/30 previewed), including
figures like Ed Seykota and later Renaissance Technologies, who
emphasized rigorous, statistically tested rules over discretionary
pattern recognition alone — a philosophy this lesson's expectancy
framework formalizes.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional quant desks require a strategy to demonstrate statistically
significant positive expectancy across large, out-of-sample datasets —
often including transaction costs, slippage, and multiple market regimes
— before allocating meaningful capital, a far higher bar than a
retail trader's typical handful of anecdotal winning trades.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders frequently judge a strategy (or a tip, or an indicator
signal) by whether the last few trades worked, without ever calculating
expectancy across a larger sample — a pattern directly connected to
`[21.12]`'s theta decay trap discussion of confusing being "right
sometimes" with having a statistically sound edge.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders track expectancy continuously in a trading journal
(`[24.12]` previewed), updating it as new trades accumulate, rather than
calculating it once from a backtest and assuming it holds indefinitely
— market conditions and a strategy's edge can both drift over time.

## Common Mistakes
- Judging a strategy by win rate alone, ignoring the size of wins versus losses.
- Testing a strategy across too few trades to draw statistically
  meaningful conclusions.
- Failing to specify exit rules in advance, leading to inconsistent,
  emotionally-driven exits that undermine the strategy's tested expectancy.

## Misconceptions
- **"A strategy with a high win rate is automatically good."** Without
  accounting for the size of wins versus losses (expectancy), a high
  win rate can still be a losing strategy.
- **"If a strategy worked the last 5 times, it's valid."** A small
  sample size cannot establish statistical validity — meaningful
  testing requires many more trades, ideally across multiple market
  regimes.
- **"A backtested strategy will always perform identically going
  forward."** Past expectancy is informative but not guaranteed —
  ongoing monitoring (`[24.12]`) remains necessary.

## Key Takeaways
- A valid strategy specifies entry, exit (both target and stop-loss),
  position sizing, and applicable conditions — not just an entry signal.
- Expectancy = (Win Rate × Avg Win) − (Loss Rate × Avg Loss) — the true
  measure of a strategy's edge, not win rate alone.
- A low win rate strategy with strong risk-reward can outperform a high
  win rate strategy with poor risk-reward.
- Validity requires testing across a statistically meaningful sample,
  not a handful of anecdotal trades.

## Summary
A trading strategy earns the label "valid" not by winning often, but by
demonstrating positive expectancy — average win size and frequency
outweighing average loss size and frequency — across a statistically
meaningful, fully specified rule set covering entry, exit, and position
sizing. With this foundation established, the next lesson begins working
through specific strategy frameworks in detail, starting with
trend-following — one of the oldest and most widely studied systematic
approaches.

## Quiz
1. What makes a trading strategy "valid"?
   a) A high win rate alone  b) Positive expectancy across a statistically meaningful sample  c) Winning the last 3 trades  d) Using a popular indicator

2. What is the expectancy formula?
   a) Win rate alone  b) (Win Rate × Avg Win) − (Loss Rate × Avg Loss)  c) Average loss only  d) Number of trades taken

3. Can a strategy with a 40% win rate be profitable?
   a) Never  b) Yes, if average wins are large enough relative to average losses  c) Only with leverage  d) Only in bull markets

4. What four components must a valid strategy specify?
   a) Only entry rules  b) Entry, exit (target + stop-loss), position sizing, applicable conditions  c) Only a stop-loss  d) Only a profit target

5. True or False: A high win rate always means a profitable strategy.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Positive expectancy across a statistically meaningful sample.
2. **b)** — (Win Rate × Avg Win) − (Loss Rate × Avg Loss).
3. **b)** — Yes, if average wins are large enough relative to average losses.
4. **b)** — Entry, exit (target + stop-loss), position sizing, and applicable conditions.
5. **b) False** — A high win rate with poor risk-reward can still produce negative expectancy.
