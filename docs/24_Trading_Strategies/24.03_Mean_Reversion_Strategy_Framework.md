## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Mean-reversion** is a strategy family that bets an asset's price,
after moving unusually far from its recent average or range, will tend
to revert back toward that average rather than continue moving in the
same direction. A complete framework specifies: a range/regime filter
(confirming the market is genuinely range-bound, not trending, `[02.02]`),
an entry trigger (e.g., an oscillator extreme like RSI, `[10.07]`, at a
established support/resistance boundary), a fixed profit target (typically
the range's midpoint or opposite boundary), and a tight stop-loss just
beyond the range boundary.

### Intuition
Where trend-following bets that movement will continue, mean-reversion
bets the opposite: that a stretched, extended move is more likely to
snap back than extend further, within a genuinely range-bound
environment. This produces the inverse expectancy shape of `[24.02]`'s
trend-following: a higher win rate (reversions are common within a true
range) with smaller average wins (capped at the range boundary) and
occasional larger losses when the range breaks.

### Mental Model
```
MEAN-REVERSION FRAMEWORK:
  1. REGIME FILTER: Confirm RANGE-BOUND conditions -- flat/sideways
     structure, [02.02]; AVOID during confirmed trends, [24.02]
  2. ENTRY TRIGGER: Price reaches range boundary (support/resistance,
     [02.04]) WITH an oscillator extreme (e.g., RSI < 30 near support,
     [10.07])
  3. PROFIT TARGET: Range midpoint or opposite boundary (FIXED target,
     unlike trend-following's trailing exit)
  4. STOP-LOSS: Just beyond the range boundary (defined risk if the
     range breaks)
  5. APPLICABLE CONDITIONS: Range-bound markets ONLY -- fails badly
     during genuine trend breakouts, [24.04]
```

### Visual Explanation

occasional sharp losses (a range boundary breaking into a genuine
trend) — the mirror image of trend-following's equity curve shape from
`[24.02]`.

## Real World Analogy
Mean-reversion resembles a rubber band stretched between two fixed
points: the further it's pulled from center, the stronger the pull back
toward center becomes — profitable as long as the band (the range)
stays intact, but if the band snaps (the range breaks into a trend),
the strategy's core assumption fails entirely, and losses can be sharp.

## Indian Market Example
A trader identifies Nifty consolidating in a defined 500-point range
over several weeks and sells near resistance when RSI exceeds 70,
buying near support when RSI falls below 30, targeting the range
midpoint each time — profitable repeatedly while the range holds, until
a strong macro catalyst (e.g., an RBI policy surprise, `[03.10]`) breaks
the range, at which point the strategy's stop-loss rules become critical.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader applies the same framework to a large-cap US stock trading in
a defined channel for several months, buying near the lower channel
boundary and selling near the upper boundary — a workable approach
until an earnings surprise breaks the stock decisively out of the
range, at which point continuing to "buy the dip" using mean-reversion
logic would work directly against the new trend. **Hypothetical
Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
Mean-reversion as a systematic strategy gained significant institutional
traction through statistical arbitrage approaches pioneered by firms
like Morgan Stanley's APT desk in the 1980s and later refined by quant
funds — demonstrating that mean-reversion, applied with rigorous regime
filtering, can be a durable statistical edge distinct from, and in some
ways complementary to, trend-following.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional mean-reversion desks invest heavily in regime detection —
statistically distinguishing genuine range-bound conditions from a
temporary pause within a larger trend — since misapplying mean-reversion
logic during an actual trend is the strategy's single largest source of
losses.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders are often drawn to mean-reversion ("buy the dip," "sell
the rip") instinctively, but frequently apply it without confirming a
genuine range first — "buying the dip" during a confirmed downtrend is
mean-reversion logic misapplied to trend-following conditions, a
common and costly conflation.

## Professional Insights
Professional mean-reversion traders treat a broken range as an
immediate, non-negotiable signal to stop trading the strategy on that
instrument — unlike trend-following's trailing stop, which lets a
position run, mean-reversion's edge depends entirely on the range
holding, making regime awareness the strategy's single most important input.

## Common Mistakes
- Applying mean-reversion logic during a confirmed trend ("buying the
  dip" in a downtrend), directly working against the prevailing move.
- Widening the stop-loss when a range boundary is tested repeatedly,
  rather than respecting the original, tighter risk definition.
- Ignoring oscillator extremes' context — an oversold reading during a
  strong downtrend can remain oversold much longer than expected.

## Misconceptions
- **"Buying oversold conditions always works."** It works specifically
  within a genuine range — during a strong trend, "oversold" can persist
  and deepen for an extended period.
- **"Mean-reversion and trend-following can use the same rules with
  opposite direction."** They require fundamentally different regime
  filters, exit structures (fixed target vs. trailing stop), and risk
  profiles — not simply mirror-image rules.
- **"A range will always eventually break in the direction it entered
  from."** Ranges can break in either direction — the strategy's stop-
  loss must account for a break in either direction, not just one.

## Key Takeaways
- Mean-reversion: bet that a stretched move within a genuine range will
  revert toward the range's center, using a fixed profit target.
- Requires confirming range-bound conditions FIRST — fails badly if
  misapplied during a trend.
- Produces a higher win rate with smaller average wins — the inverse
  expectancy shape of trend-following (`[24.02]`).
- A broken range is an immediate signal to stop applying the strategy,
  not to widen stops and continue.

## Summary
Mean-reversion builds a complete strategy around betting that
stretched moves within a genuine trading range will revert toward the
center, using oscillator extremes at established boundaries as entries
and a fixed profit target — producing a higher win rate but smaller
average wins than trend-following, and requiring rigorous regime
confirmation to avoid the strategy's single largest risk: misapplication
during an actual trend. The next lesson turns to breakout trading — a
strategy explicitly designed to catch the moment a range finally breaks.

## Quiz
1. What must be confirmed before applying a mean-reversion strategy?
   a) A strong trend  b) Genuine range-bound conditions  c) High volatility only  d) Nothing, it works everywhere

2. What kind of exit does mean-reversion typically use?
   a) A trailing stop, no fixed target  b) A fixed profit target at the range midpoint/opposite boundary  c) No exit rule  d) Time-based exit only

3. What win rate pattern is typical for mean-reversion strategies?
   a) Low win rate, large wins  b) Higher win rate, smaller average wins  c) Always 50%  d) Irrelevant

4. What should happen if the range breaks?
   a) Widen the stop and continue  b) Stop using the strategy on that instrument immediately  c) Double the position size  d) Ignore it

5. True or False: Mean-reversion and trend-following use identical exit structures.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Genuine range-bound conditions, confirmed before entry.
2. **b)** — A fixed profit target at the range midpoint or opposite boundary.
3. **b)** — Higher win rate, smaller average wins per trade.
4. **b)** — Stop using the strategy on that instrument immediately.
5. **b) False** — Trend-following uses a trailing stop; mean-reversion uses a fixed target.
