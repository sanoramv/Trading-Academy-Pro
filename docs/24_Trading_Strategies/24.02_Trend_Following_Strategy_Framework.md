## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Trend-following** is a strategy family that enters positions in the
direction of an established trend (`[02.02]`) and holds until the trend
shows clear signs of reversing, rather than predicting tops or bottoms.
A complete framework specifies: a trend-identification rule (e.g., price
above a rising moving average, or a sequence of higher highs/higher
lows), an entry trigger (e.g., a pullback and resumption), a trailing
stop-loss that widens profit protection as the trend extends, and no
fixed profit target — the trend itself defines the exit.

### Intuition
Trend-following accepts a specific trade-off: many small, quick losses
from trends that fail to develop, offset by a small number of large
wins from trends that run far — consistent with `[24.01]`'s expectancy
principle, where a comparatively low win rate is acceptable if the
average win substantially exceeds the average loss.

### Mental Model
```
TREND-FOLLOWING FRAMEWORK:
  1. TREND FILTER: Price above rising 50-day (or 200-day) MA, [10.06]
  2. ENTRY TRIGGER: Pullback to the MA, then a resumption candle/break
     of the pullback's high, [13.05]
  3. INITIAL STOP: Below the pullback's low (defined risk per trade)
  4. EXIT: TRAILING stop only (e.g., moves up with each new swing low,
     [02.07]) -- NO fixed profit target; let winners run
  5. APPLICABLE CONDITIONS: Trending markets only -- [24.01]'s
     "when NOT to use it" requirement; underperforms in ranges, [24.03]
```

### Visual Explanation
A trend-following equity curve typically shows a series of small,
frequent dips (failed trend attempts stopped out quickly) punctuated by
occasional steep upward legs (the rare trend that runs far and is held
via trailing stop) — the shape directly reflecting the low-win-rate,
high-reward-per-win expectancy structure from `[24.01]`.

## Real World Analogy
Trend-following resembles riding a wave as a surfer: the surfer doesn't
predict exactly how far the wave will carry them, they simply catch it
early, ride as long as it continues, and only step off once it clearly
breaks down — accepting that many attempted waves fizzle quickly (small
losses) in exchange for occasionally catching one that carries far
(a large win).

## Indian Market Example
A trader applies this framework to Nifty using the 50-day EMA as the
trend filter: entering long on pullback resumptions during the sustained
2020–2021 post-COVID recovery rally (`[31.02]` previewed) and trailing
the stop upward throughout, capturing a large portion of the multi-month
uptrend while any earlier, failed pullback attempts during choppier
periods were stopped out for small, defined losses.
**Hypothetical/illustrative characterization of a real market period.**

## US Market Example
A trader applies the same framework to a strong single-stock trend, such
as a mega-cap technology stock during a multi-year uptrend, entering on
each pullback-to-MA resumption and trailing stops throughout — the
approach's central bet is that a small number of these extended trends
produce enough profit to offset numerous smaller, failed attempts along
the way. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
Trend-following was popularized systematically by the "Turtle Traders"
experiment led by Richard Dennis and William Eckhardt in the early
1980s, which trained novice traders in rules-based trend-following and
produced substantial documented returns over the following years —
widely cited as one of the clearest historical demonstrations that a
mechanical trend-following framework, consistently applied, can produce
a durable statistical edge.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional Commodity Trading Advisors (CTAs) and managed futures
funds run systematic trend-following strategies across dozens of global
futures markets simultaneously, diversifying the "many small losses,
few large wins" pattern across markets and timeframes to smooth the
overall equity curve.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders applying trend-following often struggle psychologically
with its low win rate — frequent small losses can feel discouraging
even when the strategy's expectancy is positive, a discipline challenge
Module 28 (Trading Psychology) addresses directly.

## Professional Insights
Professional trend-followers emphasize that the "let winners run"
principle (no fixed profit target) is the strategy's core edge — cutting
winners early to "lock in gains" undermines the exact asymmetry
(`[24.01]`) that makes trend-following profitable over time.

## Common Mistakes
- Setting a fixed profit target, which caps the rare large wins the
  strategy depends on for positive expectancy.
- Applying trend-following in a clearly range-bound market (`[24.03]`
  territory), where whipsaws generate repeated small losses without the
  offsetting large wins.
- Abandoning the system after a string of small losses, even though
  such strings are expected and consistent with the strategy's design.

## Misconceptions
- **"Trend-following requires predicting when a trend will start."** It
  requires only *recognizing* a trend already underway and following it,
  not predicting its origin.
- **"A trend-following strategy with a 35% win rate is broken."** A low
  win rate is expected and consistent with the strategy's design, per
  `[24.01]`'s expectancy framework.
- **"Trend-following works equally well in all market conditions."** It
  specifically underperforms in range-bound, choppy markets — a
  limitation the strategy's own rules should explicitly acknowledge.

## Key Takeaways
- Trend-following: trade in the direction of an established trend,
  using a trailing stop rather than a fixed profit target.
- Accepts a low win rate in exchange for large average wins — matching
  `[24.01]`'s expectancy math.
- The Turtle Traders experiment (1980s) provides a well-documented
  historical proof of concept.
- Explicitly underperforms in range-bound markets — applicable
  conditions must be part of the strategy's rules.

## Summary
Trend-following builds a complete strategy around riding established
trends via a trailing stop rather than a fixed target, deliberately
accepting many small losses in exchange for occasional large wins — an
approach with a well-documented historical track record (the Turtle
Traders) but one that requires psychological discipline to withstand its
low win rate. The next lesson turns to the opposite philosophy — mean-
reversion — betting that extended moves will revert rather than continue.

## Quiz
1. What defines the exit in a trend-following strategy?
   a) A fixed profit target  b) A trailing stop, no fixed target  c) A time-based exit only  d) No exit rule at all

2. What win rate pattern is typical for trend-following strategies?
   a) High win rate, small average win  b) Low win rate, large average win  c) Always 50%  d) Win rate is irrelevant

3. Who is credited with the "Turtle Traders" trend-following experiment?
   a) Warren Buffett  b) Richard Dennis and William Eckhardt  c) Benjamin Graham  d) Harry Markowitz

4. In what market condition does trend-following typically underperform?
   a) Strong trending markets  b) Range-bound, choppy markets  c) It performs equally in all conditions  d) Only in bear markets

5. True or False: Setting a fixed profit target improves a trend-following strategy.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — A trailing stop, with no fixed profit target.
2. **b)** — Low win rate, offset by large average wins.
3. **b)** — Richard Dennis and William Eckhardt.
4. **b)** — Range-bound, choppy markets.
5. **b) False** — A fixed target caps the large wins the strategy's expectancy depends on.
