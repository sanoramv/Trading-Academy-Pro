## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **trading journal** is a structured, consistently maintained record
of every trade taken — including the strategy used, entry/exit
rationale, size, result, and post-trade reflection — that enables a
trader to calculate real, ongoing expectancy (`[24.01]`) rather than
relying on memory or impression. **Iteration** means updating a
strategy's rules based on accumulated journal data and statistically
meaningful evidence, as distinct from abandoning or changing a strategy
impulsively after a single loss or short losing streak, which Module 28
(Trading Psychology) identifies as a common emotional trap.

### Intuition
Every strategy framework in this module (`[24.02]`–`[24.10]`) is only as
good as the discipline behind its ongoing evaluation — a strategy tested
once in a backtest can still drift, degrade, or simply reveal its true
variance only after live trades accumulate. The journal is the
mechanism that turns "I think this strategy works" into "here is the
actual, current expectancy across N trades," closing the loop this
entire module opened with `[24.01]`'s validity criteria.

### Mental Model
```
TRADING JOURNAL — MINIMUM REQUIRED FIELDS PER TRADE:
  1. Date, Instrument, Strategy Used (which framework, [24.02]-[24.10])
  2. Entry price, Exit price, Position size
  3. Planned stop-loss AND planned target (recorded BEFORE the trade)
  4. Result (P&L, in both absolute and % risk terms)
  5. Rule adherence (did the trade follow the strategy's rules exactly,
     or was there deviation?)
  6. Post-trade note (what worked, what didn't, any emotional factor)

ITERATION DECISION RULE:
  Small sample (a handful of losses)     -> Likely NORMAL VARIANCE;
                                              do NOT change the strategy yet
  Large sample, expectancy trending      -> Possible GENUINE EDGE DECAY;
  negative over many trades                 review and consider revision
  Rule DEVIATION causing losses          -> NOT a strategy problem --
                                              a DISCIPLINE problem, [28.06]
```

### Visual Explanation
A well-maintained journal makes it possible to plot rolling expectancy
over time (e.g., a 30-trade rolling average) — a stable or improving
line supports continuing the strategy as-is, while a persistently
declining line across a statistically meaningful sample is the actual,
data-driven signal to review and potentially revise the strategy's rules.

## Real World Analogy
A trading journal functions like a pilot's flight log combined with an
aircraft's black box: it doesn't just record that a flight happened, it
records exactly how it was flown, so that patterns — a recurring
technique error, a specific condition that reliably causes trouble —
can be identified and corrected systematically, rather than relying on
the pilot's fallible memory of "how things generally went."

## Indian Market Example
A trader running the theta-selling framework (`[24.09]`) on weekly
Nifty options logs every position's entry Delta, IV regime at entry
(`[23.05]`), and outcome — after 100 logged trades, discovers that
positions entered when India VIX was below its 20-day average had
meaningfully worse expectancy than positions entered above it, leading
to a data-driven rule refinement rather than a guess. **Hypothetical
Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader running the breakout framework (`[24.04]`) logs whether each
breakout was volume-confirmed or not, discovering after sufficient
trades that unconfirmed breakouts had substantially worse expectancy —
directly validating the volume-confirmation rule `[24.04]` recommended,
using their own live data rather than relying on the lesson's guidance alone.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
The trading journal as a formal discipline is heavily associated with
Dr. Alexander Elder's trading psychology and methodology work (also
referenced in `[24.06]`'s historical example), and more broadly with
Van Tharp's position-sizing and expectancy-tracking framework
(`[24.01]`'s historical foundation) — both treat rigorous, ongoing
self-data-collection as inseparable from strategy validity itself, not
an optional add-on.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional trading desks maintain far more granular journaling
equivalents — post-trade transaction cost analysis (TCA), strategy
attribution reports, and periodic performance reviews — formalizing at
scale exactly what this lesson recommends an individual trader do
manually via a personal journal.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders very commonly skip journaling entirely, relying on
memory and impression to judge whether a strategy "is working" — a
pattern that leaves them vulnerable to recency bias (Module 28
previewed), where a few recent losses (or wins) disproportionately
color their perception of the strategy's actual, longer-run expectancy.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders often distinguish "P&L" from "process" in their
journal review — grading whether the *rules* were followed correctly
independent of whether the individual trade won or lost, since a
well-executed trade that loses (normal variance) and a poorly-executed
trade that wins (a rule violation that got lucky) both require different
responses despite superficially opposite outcomes.

## Common Mistakes
- Not journaling consistently, making it impossible to calculate real,
  ongoing expectancy.
- Changing strategy rules impulsively after a small number of losses,
  before enough data exists to distinguish normal variance from genuine
  edge decay.
- Conflating discipline failures (not following the plan) with strategy
  failures (the plan itself not working) — these require entirely
  different fixes.

## Misconceptions
- **"A losing streak means the strategy is broken."** Small losing
  streaks are statistically expected even for strategies with genuine
  positive expectancy — journaling over a larger sample is required to
  distinguish variance from actual edge decay.
- **"Journaling is only useful for reviewing losses."** Reviewing wins
  with equal rigor — especially checking whether a win resulted from
  disciplined rule-following or a lucky rule violation — is equally
  important.
- **"A backtest result is sufficient; live journaling is unnecessary."**
  Live market conditions, execution quality, and a trader's own
  discipline all introduce factors a backtest alone cannot capture.

## Key Takeaways
- A trading journal must capture entry/exit, size, planned stop/target,
  rule adherence, and post-trade reflection for every trade.
- Iteration should be driven by statistically meaningful data trends,
  not by emotional reaction to a small number of recent losses.
- Distinguish discipline failures (rule deviation) from genuine strategy
  edge decay — they require entirely different responses.
- Journaling closes the loop this module opened with `[24.01]`: turning
  "I think this works" into ongoing, measured, real expectancy.

## Summary
A trading journal — capturing every trade's rules, size, outcome, and
rule adherence — is what makes ongoing, data-driven strategy iteration
possible, distinguishing genuine edge decay from normal variance and
discipline failures from strategy failures, closing the loop this
