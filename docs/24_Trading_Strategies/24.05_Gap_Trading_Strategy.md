## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **gap** is a price discontinuity where an asset opens meaningfully
above or below its prior close, with no trading occurring at the prices
in between. **Common gaps** occur within an established range and tend
to fill quickly (price returns to the pre-gap level). **Breakaway
gaps** occur at the start of a new trend, often at a range boundary
(`[24.04]` overlap), and tend NOT to fill. **Runaway (continuation)
gaps** occur mid-trend, reflecting strong ongoing momentum, and tend
not to fill either. **Exhaustion gaps** occur late in an extended
trend and often signal an approaching reversal, frequently filling
quickly as the trend runs out of momentum.

### Intuition
Gaps happen because new information (overnight news, an earnings
surprise, a macro announcement) arrives while a market is closed,
forcing the next open to reprice discontinuously rather than gradually.
Since Indian equity markets (`[03.05]`) have a defined closed period
overnight (unlike 24-hour markets such as forex), gap risk is a
structural, routine feature of NSE/BSE trading that every strategy must
account for, not just an occasional anomaly.

### Mental Model
```
GAP TYPE IDENTIFICATION:
  COMMON gap      -> within an established range          -> tends to FILL
  BREAKAWAY gap   -> at a range boundary, new trend starts -> tends NOT to fill
  RUNAWAY gap     -> mid-trend, strong momentum            -> tends NOT to fill
  EXHAUSTION gap  -> late in an extended trend             -> tends to FILL
                                                               (often reversal signal)

GAP-FILL STRATEGY FRAMEWORK:
  1. Classify the gap type using context (where in the trend/range?)
  2. COMMON/EXHAUSTION gaps: consider fading (trading AGAINST the gap
     direction, betting on a fill)
  3. BREAKAWAY/RUNAWAY gaps: consider trading WITH the gap direction
     (similar entry logic to [24.04]'s breakout framework)
  4. STOP-LOSS: beyond the gap's open (fade) or beyond the gap's low/
     high (continuation)
```

### Visual Explanation
A common or exhaustion gap typically shows price drifting back to close
the gap within the same or following sessions, while a breakaway or
runaway gap typically shows price continuing away from the pre-gap
level with the gap itself acting as a new support/resistance zone that
holds rather than fills.

## Real World Analogy
Trading a gap is like reacting to a sudden overnight price change on a
commodity everyone trades daily: sometimes the jump reflects a
temporary overreaction that corrects during the day (common/exhaustion
gap, tends to fill), and sometimes it reflects genuinely new, lasting
information that permanently reprices the asset (breakaway/runaway gap,
tends not to fill) — telling the two apart in the moment is the entire
skill this lesson teaches.

## Indian Market Example
Nifty frequently gaps at the open following overnight moves in US
markets or GIFT Nifty (`[04.03]`), given the roughly 12–13 hour closed
window between the NSE's prior close and next open (`[03.05]`); a
trader observing a gap-down that occurs mid-range (not at a clear
breakdown level) with no major news catalyst might reasonably classify
it as a common gap likely to partially fill during the session.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A US stock gaps up sharply following a strong earnings beat,
opening well above its prior range's resistance level — a trader
classifying this as a likely breakaway gap (new information, at a
structural boundary) would consider trading with the gap direction
rather than fading it, unlike a gap occurring for no clear reason
within an established range. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration
only.

## Historical Example
Gap analysis as a formal trading discipline has long roots in classical
technical analysis literature, with gap classification (common,
breakaway, runaway, exhaustion) appearing in widely referenced texts
such as Edwards and Magee's "Technical Analysis of Stock Trends"
(first published 1948) — a framework that has remained largely intact
in modern trading education, including this lesson's structure.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional desks account for gap risk explicitly in overnight
position sizing — a large position held through an Indian market close
carries gap risk that a purely intraday position doesn't, a factor
directly relevant to Module 25's risk management and Module 26's
position sizing discussions.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders frequently react emotionally to gaps — chasing a gap-up
immediately at the open without classifying its type first, or panic-
selling into a gap-down that may be a common gap likely to partially
fill — where a brief, disciplined classification step (this lesson's
framework) would improve decision quality.

## Professional Insights
Professional gap traders wait for the first 15–30 minutes of price
action to confirm gap behavior (does it immediately start filling, or
does it hold and extend?) rather than committing capital at the open
itself, when volatility and spreads are typically at their widest.

## Common Mistakes
- Fading every gap indiscriminately, without checking whether it occurs
  at a structural boundary (likely breakaway) versus within a range
  (likely common).
- Trading immediately at the open without waiting for confirming price
  action, when volatility is highest and signals least reliable.
- Ignoring the news/catalyst context — a gap driven by major, lasting
  news behaves differently than one with no clear cause.

## Misconceptions
- **"All gaps eventually fill."** Breakaway and runaway gaps frequently
  do not fill for extended periods, sometimes never, if they mark a
  genuine trend shift.
- **"Gap trading is purely mechanical, ignoring context."** Classifying
  a gap correctly requires reading the surrounding structure (`[02.02]`)
  and news context, not applying one rule blindly to every gap.
- **"Indian markets gap the same way as 24-hour markets."** The defined
  overnight closed window (`[03.05]`) makes gap risk a structurally
  routine, near-daily feature of NSE/BSE trading in a way continuous
  markets don't experience identically.

## Key Takeaways
- Gap types: common (fills), breakaway (doesn't fill, new trend),
  runaway (doesn't fill, mid-trend), exhaustion (fills, often reversal).
- Classification depends on where the gap occurs relative to structure
  (`[02.02]`) — not the gap alone.
- Fade common/exhaustion gaps (bet on fill); trade with breakaway/
  runaway gaps (bet on continuation).
- Indian markets' defined overnight close makes gap risk a routine,
  near-daily structural feature (`[03.05]`).

## Summary
Gap trading builds a strategy around classifying overnight price
discontinuities — common, breakaway, runaway, or exhaustion — based on
where they occur relative to the underlying's recent structure, then
either fading (betting on a fill) or trading with the gap (betting on
continuation) accordingly, a discipline especially relevant to Indian
markets' routine overnight closed window. The next lesson turns to swing
trading — a broader multi-day holding framework that often incorporates
gap awareness as one input among several.

## Quiz
1. Which gap type tends to fill quickly within an established range?
   a) Breakaway gap  b) Common gap  c) Runaway gap  d) None fill

2. Which gap type marks the start of a new trend at a structural boundary?
   a) Common gap  b) Exhaustion gap  c) Breakaway gap  d) None

3. Which gap type often signals an approaching trend reversal?
   a) Runaway gap  b) Exhaustion gap  c) Common gap  d) Breakaway gap

4. Why is gap risk especially structurally routine for Indian markets?
   a) NSE trades 24 hours  b) NSE/BSE have a defined overnight closed window  c) Gaps never occur in India  d) Only futures gap, not equities

5. True or False: All gaps eventually fill.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — A common gap, occurring within an established range.
2. **c)** — A breakaway gap, occurring at a structural boundary.
3. **b)** — An exhaustion gap, occurring late in an extended trend.
4. **b)** — NSE/BSE have a defined overnight closed window (~12–13 hours), unlike continuous markets.
5. **b) False** — Breakaway and runaway gaps frequently do not fill for extended periods.
