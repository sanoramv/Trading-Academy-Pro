# [21.08] Spreads — Bull Call Spread, Bear Put Spread

## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **spread** combines buying one option and selling another option of
the same type (both calls, or both puts) on the same underlying and
expiry, but at different strikes. A **bull call spread** buys a lower-
strike call and sells a higher-strike call — a defined-risk, defined-
reward bet that the underlying rises, but not necessarily without limit.
A **bear put spread** buys a higher-strike put and sells a lower-strike
put — a defined-risk, defined-reward bet that the underlying falls. Both
reduce net premium paid (versus buying the single option alone, `[21.05]`)
by collecting premium from the sold leg, at the cost of capping maximum
profit.

### Intuition
A trader with a moderately bullish view might hesitate to pay full
premium for a long call with unlimited-but-improbable upside beyond a
certain point. Selling a further-OTM call against it recovers some of
that premium — funding the trade partly with someone else's money — in
exchange for giving up gains beyond the short strike, gains the trader
may not have realistically expected to capture anyway.

### Mental Model
```
BULL CALL SPREAD = Long Call (lower strike) + Short Call (higher strike)
  Net Premium Paid = Premium(long call) - Premium(short call)  [a net DEBIT]
  Max Profit = (Higher Strike - Lower Strike) - Net Premium Paid
  Max Loss   = Net Premium Paid
  Breakeven  = Lower Strike + Net Premium Paid

BEAR PUT SPREAD = Long Put (higher strike) + Short Put (lower strike)
  Net Premium Paid = Premium(long put) - Premium(short put)  [a net DEBIT]
  Max Profit = (Higher Strike - Lower Strike) - Net Premium Paid
  Max Loss   = Net Premium Paid
  Breakeven  = Higher Strike - Net Premium Paid
```

### Visual Explanation
Both spreads produce a payoff diagram shaped like a diagonal ramp between
two flat segments — flat at the maximum loss on the unfavorable side,
rising through breakeven, then flat again at the maximum profit once the
underlying passes the second strike. This is the long single-leg diagram
from `[21.05]` with its uncapped tail chopped off and partially monetized.

### Flowchart
```
[Moderately bullish or bearish view -- not expecting an extreme move]
        |
        v
[Bullish?] --YES--> [Buy lower-strike call, sell higher-strike call -> BULL CALL SPREAD]
        | NO (bearish)
        v
[Buy higher-strike put, sell lower-strike put -> BEAR PUT SPREAD]
        |
        v
[Net Premium Paid = Long leg premium - Short leg premium]
        |
        v
[Max Profit = Strike Width - Net Premium; Max Loss = Net Premium;
 Breakeven = adjust the lower/higher strike by the net premium]
```

## Real World Analogy
A bull call spread is like pre-booking a hotel room at today's rate
while simultaneously agreeing to sublet it to someone else if the
nightly rate rises above a certain ceiling — you profit from the rate
rising up to that ceiling, and the sublet income partly offsets your
booking cost, but you give up any gain beyond the ceiling since you've
already agreed to hand the room over.

## Indian Market Example
A trader buys a Nifty 24,000 call for ₹150 and sells a Nifty 24,200 call
for ₹70, a net debit of ₹80 (illustrative figures). Maximum profit =
(24,200 − 24,000) − 80 = ₹120 if Nifty expires at or above 24,200;
maximum loss = ₹80 if Nifty expires at or below 24,000 — versus paying
the full ₹150 premium (and needing Nifty above 24,150) for the long call
alone per `[21.05]`. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader buys a SPY 450 put for $6 and sells a SPY 440 put for $2.50, a
net debit of $3.50 (illustrative figures). Maximum profit = (450 − 440)
− 3.50 = $6.50 if SPY expires at or below 440; maximum loss = $3.50 if
SPY expires at or above 450. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration
only.

## Historical Example
Ahead of scheduled binary events with well-known dates — such as US
Federal Reserve rate decisions (`[06.02]`) or the Indian Union Budget
(`[03.10]`) — traders have long used defined-risk spreads rather than
naked long options specifically because implied volatility (and
therefore option premium) typically rises into the event and collapses
afterward; a spread's short leg partially offsets that volatility cost,
a dynamic Module 23 (Volatility) develops fully.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional desks favor spreads over naked long options when a
directional view exists but conviction about the *magnitude* of the move
is limited — the defined maximum loss makes position sizing and margin
usage far more predictable than an unhedged long option, and the reduced
net premium means less capital tied up in time value that will decay
(`[21.04]`) regardless of direction.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders are drawn to spreads because the "cost is lower" than
buying a single option outright — but should recognize this comes with
a capped upside; a trader with genuinely strong conviction about an
unlimited-upside move may prefer the single long call from `[21.05]`
despite its higher cost.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders often select the short leg's strike based on where
they believe probability of a further move meaningfully drops off,
effectively "selling" the tail of the distribution they don't expect to
need — a judgment that improves with the probability concepts Module 30
(Quantitative Trading) introduces.

## Common Mistakes
- Choosing strikes too close together, resulting in a very small max
  profit relative to the risk taken (a poor risk-reward ratio, `[25.04]`
  previewed).
- Forgetting that both legs must share the same expiry and underlying —
  mismatched expiries create an entirely different (calendar spread)
  strategy, beyond this lesson's scope.
- Ignoring that both legs need to be closed (or expire) together;
  closing only one leg early re-exposes the trader to the naked risk
  profile of the remaining leg (`[21.05]`/`[21.06]`).

## Misconceptions
- **"A spread has no risk since it's 'hedged.'"** The maximum loss (net
  premium paid) is smaller than the naked long option's premium, but it
  is not zero.
- **"Spreads always cost less than buying options outright."** They
  cost less than the *corresponding single long option*, not necessarily
  less than every alternative strategy.
- **"The short leg's premium is pure profit."** It directly funds and is
  netted against the long leg's cost — it isn't separate, isolated
  income.

## Key Takeaways
- Bull call spread = Long lower-strike call + Short higher-strike call.
- Bear put spread = Long higher-strike put + Short lower-strike put.
- Both: Max Loss = Net Premium Paid. Max Profit = Strike Width − Net
  Premium Paid.
- Spreads trade unlimited upside (single-leg long options, `[21.05]`)
  for a lower net cost and a defined, smaller maximum loss.

## Summary
Bull call spreads and bear put spreads combine a long option with a
short option at a different strike to reduce net premium cost and define
maximum risk, at the expense of capping maximum profit — a natural
evolution from the single-leg payoffs of `[21.05]` toward strategies
suited to a directional view without extreme-move conviction. The next
lesson extends multi-leg construction further into non-directional
strategies — straddles and strangles — designed to profit from movement
regardless of direction.

## Quiz
1. A bull call spread consists of:
   a) Two long calls  b) Long lower-strike call + short higher-strike call  c) Long put + short call  d) Two short calls

2. What is the maximum loss on a bull call spread?
   a) Unlimited  b) The strike width  c) The net premium paid  d) Zero

3. What is the maximum profit formula for a bear put spread?
   a) Unlimited  b) Strike width minus net premium paid  c) Net premium paid  d) Zero

4. Why might a trader choose a spread over a single long call?
   a) Higher potential loss  b) Lower net cost and defined risk, at the cost of capped upside  c) It removes all risk  d) It guarantees profit

5. True or False: Closing only one leg of a spread early leaves the trader with the original, defined-risk position.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Long lower-strike call + short higher-strike call.
2. **c)** — The net premium paid, the maximum possible loss.
3. **b)** — Strike width minus net premium paid.
4. **b)** — Lower net cost and defined maximum risk, trading away unlimited upside.
5. **b) False** — Closing one leg re-exposes the trader to the naked risk of the remaining leg.
