# [21.06] Option Payoff Diagrams — Short Call, Short Put

> Module: Options · Lesson 6 of 12 · Estimated time: 35 minutes

## Learning Objectives

- Calculate breakeven price, maximum profit, and maximum loss for each position.
- Understand why short option positions have inverted risk/reward relative to `[21.05]` — capped profit, uncapped (or large) loss.
- Recognize the seller's obligation (vs. the buyer's right) established in `[21.02]` as the mechanical source of this asymmetry.

## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **short call** (selling/writing a call) is the exact mirror image of
`[21.05]`'s long call: the seller collects the premium upfront and
profits if the underlying stays at or below the strike, but faces
theoretically unlimited loss if the underlying rises far above the
strike, since the seller is obligated (not merely entitled, per
`[21.02]`) to deliver the underlying at the strike price if assigned. A
**short put** (selling/writing a put) mirrors `[21.05]`'s long put: the
seller collects the premium upfront and profits if the underlying stays
at or above the strike, but faces a large (though capped, since the
underlying floor is zero) loss if the underlying falls far below the
strike. In both cases, the payoff is the exact negative of the
corresponding long position's payoff.

### Intuition
Every option contract has exactly one buyer and one seller — the
premium the buyer pays is the premium the seller receives (`[21.02]`).
Since a long call's payoff is `max(0, Underlying - Strike) - Premium`,
the short call's payoff is precisely the negative of that expression:
`Premium - max(0, Underlying - Strike)`. This isn't a separate formula
to memorize — it's the same relationship viewed from the other side of
the trade. The seller's maximum gain is capped at the premium received
(the best case is the option expiring worthless), while their loss
grows without the buyer's protection of "walking away," because the
seller doesn't have the choice to decline exercise/assignment.

### Mental Model
```
SHORT CALL payoff at expiry (per share/unit):
  Payoff = Premium Received - max(0, Underlying - Strike)

SHORT PUT payoff at expiry (per share/unit):
  Payoff = Premium Received - max(0, Strike - Underlying)

Both positions:
  Maximum Profit  = Premium Received (fixed, known in advance)
  Breakeven       = Call: Strike + Premium
                     Put:  Strike - Premium
  Maximum Loss    = Call: Theoretically unlimited (underlying can rise indefinitely)
                     Put:  Large but capped (underlying can only fall to zero)
```

## Real World Analogy
Selling a call resembles being the party on the other end of the house-
reservation deposit from `[21.05]`'s analogy: the seller pockets the
deposit upfront, and if the house's value stays flat or falls, they
keep the full deposit as profit. But if the house's value rises sharply,
the seller is *obligated* to sell at the old, now-below-market price —
an obligation with no upper limit on how costly it can become, unlike
the buyer, who could simply forfeit their deposit and walk away.

## Indian Market Example
A trader sells one Nifty 24,000 call expiring in a week and collects a
premium of ₹150 (illustrative figure; lot size ignored). If Nifty
expires at 23,900 (at or below strike), the option expires worthless;
payoff = +₹150, the maximum possible profit. If Nifty expires at 24,500,
intrinsic value = max(0, 24,500 − 24,000) = 500; payoff = 150 − 500 =
−₹350 — and this loss grows further, without limit, the higher Nifty
rises beyond 24,500. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader sells one AAPL 190 put and collects a premium of $4
(illustrative figure). If AAPL expires at $195 (at or above strike), the
option expires worthless; payoff = +$4, the maximum possible profit. If
AAPL expires at $170, intrinsic value = max(0, 190 − 170) = $20; payoff
= 4 − 20 = −$16 per share — a loss that grows as AAPL falls further,
though it is bounded since AAPL cannot fall below $0. **Hypothetical
Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
The 2007–2008 subprime-linked derivatives losses at several global
financial institutions illustrated short-option-style risk at scale:
firms that had effectively sold protection against adverse outcomes (in
various derivative forms) collected steady premium income during calm
periods but faced losses far larger than any premium collected once
conditions moved sharply against them — the same capped-gain,
open-ended-loss shape this lesson's short call payoff diagram shows in
miniature, though 2007-08 involved credit derivatives rather than plain
listed equity/index options specifically.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional market makers routinely run large short option books, but
they manage the unlimited-loss exposure this lesson describes through
continuous **delta hedging** (Module 22, previewed) — dynamically
trading the underlying to offset directional risk — rather than by
accepting the naked payoff diagram shown above; a retail trader selling
options without such hedging carries the full, undiluted risk this
lesson's diagrams depict.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders are drawn to selling options because premium income
arrives immediately and the trade is profitable more often than not
(the underlying frequently stays within a range) — but this ignores that
the rare adverse move can erase many periods' worth of premium income at
once; `[21.12]` returns to this asymmetry from the buyer's side, while
Module 25 (Risk Management) addresses position sizing discipline needed
before selling options at all.

## Professional Insights
Professional option sellers size positions and hold margin/collateral
specifically calibrated to survive multi-standard-deviation adverse
moves, not just the "expected" range — treating the theoretically
unlimited (or large) loss tail as a real possibility to be capitalized
for, not a statistical curiosity to be dismissed.

## Common Mistakes
- Believing a short put's loss is unlimited — it is large but capped,
  since the underlying cannot fall below zero.
- Selling options for "steady income" without accounting for the size of
  the tail-risk loss relative to the premium collected.
- Forgetting that the short position's breakeven is identical to the
  corresponding long position's breakeven — only the direction of profit
  and loss beyond that point differs.

## Misconceptions
- **"Selling options is a reliable income strategy with manageable
  risk."** The strategy trades frequent small gains for infrequent large
  losses — "manageable" depends entirely on position sizing and hedging
  discipline, not on the strategy itself.
- **"A short call and short put have identical loss profiles since both
  are 'the seller's side.'"** A short call's maximum loss is unlimited;
  a short put's maximum loss is large but mathematically capped.
- **"If I sold an option and it expires worthless, I got lucky."** More
  often it reflects the underlying staying within its statistically
  expected range — though the seller must still survive every period
  where it doesn't.

## Key Takeaways
- Short call payoff = Premium Received − max(0, Underlying − Strike).
- Short put payoff = Premium Received − max(0, Strike − Underlying).
- Both: Maximum profit = Premium Received, fixed and known in advance.
- Breakeven is identical to the corresponding long position:
  Call = Strike + Premium; Put = Strike − Premium.
- Maximum loss: Call = theoretically unlimited. Put = large but capped
  (underlying floor is zero).

## Summary
Short call and short put payoff diagrams are the exact mirror image of
`[21.05]`'s long call and long put diagrams: the seller's maximum gain
is capped at the premium collected, while their maximum loss is
unlimited (call) or large-but-capped (put) — the structural consequence
of the seller's obligation, versus the buyer's right, established back
in `[21.02]`. With both sides of a single option now understood, the
next lesson turns to basic multi-leg strategies — covered calls and
protective puts — that combine an option position with a position in the
underlying itself to reshape this risk profile.

## Quiz
1. What is the maximum profit on a short put position?
   a) Unlimited  b) The strike price  c) The premium received  d) Zero

2. What is the maximum possible loss on a short call?
   a) The premium received  b) Zero  c) Theoretically unlimited  d) Capped at the strike price

3. Why is a short put's maximum loss capped, while a short call's is not?
   a) Puts always cost less  b) The underlying can fall only to zero but can rise indefinitely  c) Sellers of puts are protected by SEBI  d) It isn't — both are unlimited

4. A short call's breakeven price, compared to a long call at the same strike/premium, is:
   a) Higher  b) Lower  c) Identical  d) Undefined

5. True or False: Short option sellers profit only when the underlying moves sharply.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **c)** — The premium received, regardless of how favorably the underlying moves.
2. **c)** — Theoretically unlimited, since the underlying can rise indefinitely.
3. **b)** — The underlying's price floor is zero, but it has no ceiling.
4. **c)** — Identical: Strike + Premium in both cases.
5. **b) False** — Sellers profit when the underlying stays within a range (at/beyond the strike in their favor), not from sharp moves.
