## Core Concepts

### Definition
An **iron condor** combines a bear call spread (short lower-strike call,
long higher-strike call) and a bull put spread (short higher-strike put,
long lower-strike put), all four legs on the same underlying and expiry
— a net-credit strategy that profits if the underlying stays *between*
the two short strikes at expiry. An **iron butterfly** is a special case
using the *same* strike for both short legs (short call and short put at
the same ATM strike) with wings (long call above, long put below) — a
net-credit strategy that profits most if the underlying finishes exactly
at that middle strike, similar in shape to a short straddle (`[21.09]`)
but with defined, capped risk from the wing purchases.

### Intuition
Where a short strangle (`[21.09]`, mirror image) profits when the
underlying stays in a range but carries large/unlimited loss if it
doesn't, an iron condor buys further-OTM protection on both sides to cap
that risk — sacrificing some of the collected premium for a defined
maximum loss. The iron butterfly does the same thing to a short
straddle, trading some collected premium for capped risk.

### Mental Model
```
IRON CONDOR = Bear Call Spread (short K2, long K3) + Bull Put Spread (short K1, long K0)
  where K0 < K1 < K2 < K3, all same expiry
  Net Credit Received = Total premium collected - Total premium paid
  Max Profit = Net Credit Received (underlying finishes between K1 and K2)
  Max Loss   = (K3 - K2) - Net Credit  [equivalently (K1 - K0) - Net Credit; wings equal width]
  Breakevens = K1 - Net Credit (lower), K2 + Net Credit (upper)

IRON BUTTERFLY = Short Call + Short Put (same strike K) + Long Call (K+w) + Long Put (K-w)
  Net Credit Received = Total premium collected - Total premium paid
  Max Profit = Net Credit Received (underlying finishes exactly at K)
  Max Loss   = w - Net Credit
  Breakevens = K - Net Credit (lower), K + Net Credit (upper)
```

### Visual Explanation
Both diagrams form a flat-topped tent or plateau shape: maximum profit
across a middle zone (a range for the condor, a single point peak for
the butterfly), sloping down on both sides to a flat maximum-loss floor
once the underlying passes the outer (wing) strikes.

```

 Breakevens = inner (or center) strike(s) +/- Net Credit]
```

## Real World Analogy
An iron condor is like a landlord who collects rent (the net credit)
while betting a property's market value stays within a comfortable
band; they've also purchased cheap "catastrophe" insurance on both the
extreme-upside and extreme-downside scenarios (the long wings), capping
what they could lose if the market moves further than expected in
either direction — unlike a landlord with no insurance at all (a naked
short strangle, `[21.09]`).

## Indian Market Example
A trader expects Nifty to stay range-bound and constructs an iron
condor: sell 24,200 call, buy 24,400 call, sell 23,800 put, buy 23,600
put, collecting a net credit of ₹90 (illustrative figures). If Nifty
expires anywhere between 23,800 and 24,200, both spreads expire
worthless and the trader keeps the full ₹90. If Nifty expires at 24,500,
the call spread loses (200 wing width − 90 credit) = ₹110, the maximum
possible loss. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader expects a stock to stay near $200 through a quiet earnings
period and constructs an iron butterfly: sell the 200 call and 200 put,
buy the 210 call and 190 put, collecting a net credit of $6 (illustrative
figures). If the stock expires exactly at $200, both short legs expire
worthless and the trader keeps the full $6 — the maximum possible profit.
If the stock expires at $215, the loss is capped at (10 wing width − 6
credit) = $4. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
Extended, low-volatility consolidation periods — such as parts of 2017,
often cited as an unusually calm year for major US and Indian indices —
are the environment iron condors and iron butterflies are specifically
designed to exploit; conversely, sharp regime shifts like the volatility
spike accompanying the March 2020 COVID crash (`[21.07]`'s historical
example) are precisely when these range-bound strategies suffer their
maximum, though defined, losses.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional volatility-selling desks run iron condor and iron
butterfly books at scale specifically because implied volatility tends,
on average, to overstate realized volatility (a persistent phenomenon
Module 23 develops as the "volatility risk premium") — collecting that
average edge across many positions while using the defined-risk wing
structure to survive any individual adverse outlier.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders are increasingly drawn to iron condors as "steady income"
strategies, particularly in Indian weekly index options (`[03.08]`) —
but should recognize that a string of small wins can be erased by one
maximum-loss event if position sizing (Module 26) doesn't account for
the loss's full size relative to the credit collected.

## Professional Insights
Professional condor/butterfly traders actively manage positions before
expiry — adjusting or closing a threatened side rather than mechanically
holding to expiry — since the defined maximum loss describes the worst
case *at expiry*, not necessarily the best exit if the underlying moves
against the position early.

## Common Mistakes
- Setting wing widths too narrow relative to the credit collected,
  producing a poor risk-reward ratio (small credit vs. a much larger
  potential loss).
- Holding through a known high-volatility event (earnings, budget, Fed
  meeting) that the strategy wasn't designed to withstand.
- Forgetting that all four legs share the same expiry and underlying —
  and that closing them requires unwinding all four together for the
  position to behave as analyzed.

## Misconceptions
- **"Iron condors are low-risk since they profit most of the time."**
  Frequency of small wins says nothing about the size of the occasional
  large, though capped, loss — the two must be weighed together.
- **"An iron butterfly is just a wider iron condor."** They differ
  structurally: a butterfly's short legs share one strike (peak profit
  at a point); a condor's short legs are at different strikes (peak
  profit across a range).
- **"Selling premium via these structures is 'free money' in quiet
  markets."** The premium compensates for genuine tail risk — it isn't
  free, it's compensation for insurance the seller is providing.

## Key Takeaways
- Iron condor = Bear call spread + Bull put spread — profits in a
  range, wider profit zone, lower peak.
- Iron butterfly = Short straddle + protective long wings — profits
  near a single point, narrower profit zone, higher peak.
- Both: Max Profit = Net Credit Received. Max Loss = Wing Width − Net
  Credit.
- Both trade a defined, capped maximum loss for a smaller net credit
  than the equivalent naked short strangle/straddle (`[21.09]`).

## Summary
Iron condors and iron butterflies cap the large/unlimited risk of a
naked short strangle or straddle (`[21.09]`) by adding protective long
"wing" options, in exchange for a smaller net credit — one of the most
widely used range-bound, premium-collecting strategies in Indian weekly
index options and beyond. Having now covered the two- and four-leg
combination strategies that reshape risk in every direction, the next
lesson turns to reading a live NSE option chain — the tool through which
all of these strategies are actually constructed in practice.

## Revision Notes
- Iron condor: short call spread + short put spread, four different
  strikes. Profits in a RANGE between the two short strikes.
- Iron butterfly: short straddle (same strike) + long wings. Profits
  most AT a single strike.
- Both: Max Profit = Net Credit. Max Loss = Wing Width − Net Credit.
- Both cap the large/unlimited risk of the equivalent naked short
  strangle/straddle (`[21.09]`), at the cost of a smaller net credit.

## Quiz
1. An iron condor is built from:
   a) Two long calls  b) A bear call spread + a bull put spread  c) A straddle only  d) Four long options

2. What distinguishes an iron butterfly from an iron condor?
   a) It has no wings  b) Its short legs share the same strike  c) It requires five legs  d) It is always unprofitable

3. What is the maximum profit on either structure?
   a) Unlimited  b) The wing width  c) The net credit received  d) Zero

4. What is the maximum loss formula for an iron condor?
   a) Net credit received  b) Wing width minus net credit received  c) Unlimited  d) Zero

5. True or False: An iron condor profits most when the underlying makes a large move in one direction.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — A bear call spread + a bull put spread.
2. **b)** — Its short call and short put share the same strike, unlike a condor's different short strikes.
3. **c)** — The net credit received, the maximum possible profit.
4. **b)** — Wing width minus net credit received.
5. **b) False** — It profits most when the underlying stays range-bound, between the short strikes.
