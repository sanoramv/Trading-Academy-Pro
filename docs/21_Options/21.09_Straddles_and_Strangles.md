# [21.09] Straddles and Strangles

> Module: Options · Lesson 9 of 12 · Estimated time: 35 minutes

## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **long straddle** buys a call and a put at the *same* strike (usually
ATM, `[21.03]`) and same expiry — a bet that the underlying moves
significantly, in *either* direction, before expiry. A **long strangle**
buys an OTM call and an OTM put at *different* strikes (call strike
above, put strike below the current price) — the same directional-
agnostic bet, but cheaper to enter since OTM options cost less, at the
price of needing a larger move to reach breakeven. Both differ from
`[21.05]`'s single-leg long positions and `[21.08]`'s spreads, which bet
on a specific direction; straddles and strangles bet purely on
*magnitude* of movement, regardless of direction.

### Intuition
Sometimes a trader is confident a big move is coming — around earnings,
a budget announcement, or a central bank decision — but genuinely
uncertain which direction it will go. Buying both a call and a put means
profiting whichever way the underlying breaks, as long as the move is
large enough to cover the combined cost of both premiums.

### Mental Model
```
LONG STRADDLE = Long Call (Strike K) + Long Put (Strike K), same expiry
  Total Premium Paid = Call Premium + Put Premium
  Max Loss   = Total Premium Paid (if underlying = K exactly at expiry)
  Max Profit = Unlimited (upside) / Large (downside)
  Breakevens = K + Total Premium (upper), K - Total Premium (lower)

LONG STRANGLE = Long Call (Strike K_high) + Long Put (Strike K_low), K_high > K_low
  Total Premium Paid = Call Premium + Put Premium  [usually LESS than a straddle's]
  Max Loss   = Total Premium Paid (if underlying between K_low and K_high at expiry)
  Max Profit = Unlimited (upside) / Large (downside)
  Breakevens = K_high + Total Premium (upper), K_low - Total Premium (lower)
```

### Visual Explanation
Both diagrams form a "V" (straddle) or a flat-bottomed "V" / bathtub
shape (strangle): a loss zone in the middle around the strike(s), and
two rising arms extending outward in both directions. The strangle's
loss zone is wider (spanning between the two strikes) but the arms start
from a lower total cost.

### Flowchart
```
[Expect a LARGE move but are UNCERTAIN of direction, e.g. ahead of earnings/budget/Fed]
        |
        v
[Want tighter breakevens, willing to pay more?] --YES--> [Buy ATM call + ATM put, same strike -> LONG STRADDLE]
        | NO (want lower cost, willing to accept wider breakevens)
        v
[Buy OTM call + OTM put, different strikes -> LONG STRANGLE]
        |
        v
[Max Loss = Total Premium Paid (both); Breakevens = Strike(s) +/- Total Premium]
        |
        v
[If instead SELLING both legs -> SHORT STRADDLE/STRANGLE:
 mirror-image payoff, capped profit = premium collected, large/unlimited loss]
```

## Real World Analogy
A long straddle is like buying insurance against *both* a flood and a
drought on the same farmland in the same season — you pay two premiums
instead of one, but you're covered whichever extreme occurs, and you
only "lose" (forfeit both premiums) if the season turns out perfectly
average. A long strangle is the cheaper version of that same insurance,
covering only more extreme floods and droughts, leaving moderate
variation uncovered.

## Indian Market Example
Ahead of a Union Budget announcement (`[03.10]`), a trader buys a Nifty
24,000 straddle: the 24,000 call for ₹150 and the 24,000 put for ₹140,
total premium ₹290 (illustrative figures). If Nifty reacts sharply to
budget announcements and expires at 24,500, the call payoff = 500 − 150
= +350 and the put expires worthless (−140), net = +₹210. If Nifty
barely moves and expires at 24,010, both legs are worth little and the
trader loses close to the full ₹290. **Hypothetical Example** for
illustration only.

## US Market Example
Ahead of a company's quarterly earnings release, a trader buys a
strangle on the stock: a $210 call for $4 and a $190 put for $3.50,
total premium $7.50 (illustrative figures), with the stock at $200. If
earnings trigger a large move to $225, the call payoff = 15 − 4 = +$11
per share and the put expires worthless, net = +$7.50. If the stock
barely moves to $202, both legs expire worthless and the trader loses
the full $7.50. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
The March 2020 COVID crash produced exceptionally large, rapid moves in
both Nifty and major US indices within days — a period during which
straddles/strangles purchased even shortly before the sharpest declines
would have profited substantially from the *magnitude* of the move alone,
regardless of the specific direction a trader might have separately
predicted, illustrating this lesson's core "bet on magnitude, not
direction" logic in an extreme, real episode.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional volatility desks trade straddles and strangles as pure
expressions of a view on implied volatility itself (Module 23) rather
than on the underlying's direction — buying when they believe the
market is underpricing future movement, selling when they believe it is
overpricing it, independent of any directional forecast.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders are drawn to straddles/strangles ahead of known event
dates, but frequently underestimate how much implied volatility (and
therefore premium) is already elevated heading into the event — a large
move may still be insufficient to profit if it was already "priced in,"
a dynamic Module 23's volatility skew and smile lesson develops further.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders distinguish between buying volatility (straddle/
strangle) for a specific catalyst versus doing so as a standing
portfolio hedge — the same structure serves both purposes, but position
sizing and holding period differ substantially between the two uses.

## Common Mistakes
- Buying a straddle/strangle right before a widely anticipated event
  without checking whether implied volatility (and thus premium) is
  already unusually elevated.
- Underestimating how much combined premium (two legs) must be
  overcome — a common source of the "the move happened but I still
  lost money" experience.
- Confusing a long straddle/strangle (defined, capped loss) with a
  short straddle/strangle (large or unlimited loss) — they are opposite
  risk profiles built from the same strikes.

## Misconceptions
- **"A straddle guarantees profit if I'm right that a big move is
  coming."** It requires the move to exceed the combined premium paid,
  not merely to occur.
- **"Strangles are strictly better than straddles since they cost
  less."** Lower cost comes with wider breakevens — a smaller move that
  would have profited a straddle may fail to reach a strangle's
  breakeven.
- **"Selling a straddle/strangle is a safe way to collect premium."**
  The seller's loss is large or unlimited if the underlying moves
  sharply — the mirror image of this lesson's long position risk.

## Key Takeaways
- Long straddle = Long ATM Call + Long ATM Put, same strike — bets on
  large movement, either direction.
- Long strangle = Long OTM Call + Long OTM Put, different strikes —
  same bet, lower cost, wider breakevens.
- Both: Max Loss = Total Premium Paid. Max Profit = unlimited (upside) /
  large (downside).
- Short straddle/strangle mirrors the long version: capped profit
  (premium collected), large/unlimited loss.

## Summary
Straddles and strangles are the first strategies in this module built
purely on magnitude of movement rather than direction, combining a long
call and a long put (same strike for a straddle, different strikes for a
cheaper strangle) to profit from a large move either way, with loss
capped at the combined premium paid. The next lesson extends this
non-directional logic further into four-leg structures — iron condors
and iron butterflies — which instead profit when the underlying stays
*within* a range.

## Revision Notes
- Long straddle: Long Call + Long Put, SAME strike. Bets on large move,
  either direction. Max Loss = Total Premium.
- Long strangle: Long OTM Call + Long OTM Put, DIFFERENT strikes.
  Cheaper than straddle, wider breakevens.
- Both breakevens: Strike(s) ± Total Premium Paid.
- Short straddle/strangle = mirror image: capped profit, large/unlimited
  loss — sensitive to unexpectedly large moves.

## Quiz
1. A long straddle consists of:
   a) Long call + short put  b) Long call + long put, same strike  c) Two long calls  d) Long call + long put, different strikes

2. What distinguishes a strangle from a straddle?
   a) Strangles use different strikes for the call and put  b) Strangles use the same strike  c) Strangles require no premium  d) There is no difference

3. What is the maximum loss on a long strangle?
   a) Unlimited  b) The total premium paid for both legs  c) Zero  d) The strike width

4. Straddles and strangles primarily bet on:
   a) Direction only  b) Magnitude of movement, regardless of direction  c) Dividend payments  d) Interest rates

5. True or False: A short straddle has the same risk profile as a long straddle.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Long call + long put, same strike.
2. **a)** — Strangles use different (OTM) strikes for the call and put legs.
3. **b)** — The total premium paid for both legs.
4. **b)** — Magnitude of movement, regardless of direction.
5. **b) False** — A short straddle is the mirror image: capped profit, large/unlimited loss.
