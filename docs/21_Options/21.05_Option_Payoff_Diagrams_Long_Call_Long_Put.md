## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **payoff diagram** plots an option position's profit or loss (Y-axis)
against the underlying's price at expiry (X-axis). A **long call**
(buying a call) profits when the underlying rises above the strike by
more than the premium paid; loss is capped at the premium paid,
regardless of how far the underlying falls. A **long put** (buying a
put) profits when the underlying falls below the strike by more than
the premium paid; loss is likewise capped at the premium paid,
regardless of how far the underlying rises. Both are built entirely from
concepts already covered: the payoff at expiry equals intrinsic value
(`[21.04]`) minus the premium originally paid, since time value is zero
at expiry.

### Intuition
A payoff diagram answers one question precisely: "if the underlying
ends up at price X on expiry day, what is my profit or loss?" Buying an
option means paying a fixed, known cost upfront (the premium) in
exchange for the *right* — not obligation — to benefit from a favorable
move (`[21.02]`). Since the buyer can simply let an unfavorable option
expire worthless, the maximum they can ever lose is what they paid.
That structural fact — a fixed, known maximum loss paired with a
potentially large gain — is what makes long option payoff diagrams look
so different from buying a stock outright, where loss is proportional
to the price decline.

### Mental Model
```
LONG CALL payoff at expiry (per share/unit):
  Payoff = max(0, Underlying - Strike) - Premium Paid

LONG PUT payoff at expiry (per share/unit):
  Payoff = max(0, Strike - Underlying) - Premium Paid

Both positions:
  Maximum Loss   = Premium Paid (fixed, known in advance)
  Breakeven      = Call: Strike + Premium
                    Put:  Strike - Premium
  Maximum Profit = Call: Theoretically unlimited (underlying can rise indefinitely)
                    Put:  Capped but large (underlying can only fall to zero)
```

### Visual Explanation
Both diagrams share the same shape logic: a flat, capped-loss segment
on the unfavorable side, a breakeven point where the line crosses zero,
and a rising line on the favorable side. The call's rising line points
right (profits as the underlying rises); the put's rising line points
left (profits as the underlying falls) — mirror images of each other
around the strike price.

## Real World Analogy
A long call resembles paying a small, fixed deposit to reserve the
right to buy a house at today's price six months from now: if the
house's market value rises well above the reserved price, the buyer
exercises the reservation and profits from the difference; if the
house's value falls or stays flat, the buyer simply forfeits the
deposit and walks away, losing only that fixed, known amount — never
more. A long put is the mirror image: a fixed deposit reserving the
right to *sell* an asset at today's price later, valuable if the
asset's value falls.

## Indian Market Example
A trader buys one Nifty 24,000 call expiring in a week for a premium of
₹150 (illustrative figure; lot size ignored for simplicity). If Nifty
expires at 24,300, intrinsic value = max(0, 24,300 − 24,000) = 300;
payoff = 300 − 150 = +₹150 profit per unit. If Nifty expires at 23,900
(below strike), intrinsic value = 0; payoff = 0 − 150 = −₹150, the
maximum possible loss, regardless of how far below 24,000 Nifty falls —
even to 23,000. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader buys one AAPL 190 put for a premium of $4 (illustrative
figure). If AAPL expires at $180, intrinsic value = max(0, 190 − 180) =
$10; payoff = $10 − $4 = +$6 profit per share. If AAPL expires at $195
(above strike), intrinsic value = 0; payoff = 0 − 4 = −$4, the maximum
possible loss, regardless of how high above $190 AAPL rises.
**Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
The asymmetric payoff structure long options provide became especially
visible during the March 2020 COVID crash, when Nifty fell roughly 38%
from its January 2020 high to its March 2020 low in a matter of weeks —
traders holding long put positions ahead of the decline saw payoffs
grow disproportionately to the move, while their maximum possible loss
had been fixed and known well in advance, a structural feature this
lesson's payoff math makes precise.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional desks rarely hold naked long option positions as a
primary strategy; they more commonly use long calls/puts as one leg
within a larger multi-leg structure (spreads, `[21.08]`; straddles/
strangles, `[21.09]`) or as portfolio hedges, since a standalone long
option's cost (the premium, largely time value per `[21.04]`) erodes
steadily if the anticipated move doesn't materialize in time.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders are drawn to long calls and puts because the payoff
diagram's "capped loss, large/unlimited profit" shape looks like a
favorable bet — but this analysis ignores probability: options are
priced (via time value) so that the *expected* payoff accounts for how
likely the underlying is to move far enough, fast enough, to overcome
the premium paid and reach breakeven — the central theme of `[21.12]`'s
common option buyer mistakes discussion.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders read a payoff diagram together with two additional
questions this lesson doesn't yet cover: how much *time* remains for the
underlying to reach breakeven (time value decay, Module 22's theta), and
how *volatile* the underlying needs to be to make that move likely
(Module 23) — a payoff diagram alone describes outcomes at expiry, not
the probability of reaching them.

## Common Mistakes
- Believing a long call/put's maximum loss is unlimited — it is always
  capped at the premium paid.
- Forgetting to add the premium into the breakeven calculation (using
  the strike price itself as the breakeven, which is incorrect).
- Treating the payoff diagram (an expiry-only picture) as if it shows
  profit/loss at every point in time before expiry — before expiry, the
  position's value also includes remaining time value (`[21.04]`).

## Misconceptions
- **"Buying options is always low-risk since loss is capped."** The
  loss is capped in absolute rupee/dollar terms, but it is frequently
  100% of the amount risked — a much larger *percentage* loss than many
  other position types.
- **"A long call and long put are just opposite bets on direction with
  identical risk profiles."** They share the same *loss-capped,
  profit-open* structure, but a long put's maximum profit is capped
  (underlying can only fall to zero) while a long call's is not
  (underlying can rise indefinitely).
- **"The payoff diagram shows what happens at any moment before
  expiry."** It strictly describes value *at expiry only*.

## Key Takeaways
- Long call payoff = max(0, Underlying − Strike) − Premium Paid.
- Long put payoff = max(0, Strike − Underlying) − Premium Paid.
- Both: Maximum loss = Premium Paid, fixed and known in advance.
- Call breakeven = Strike + Premium; Put breakeven = Strike − Premium.
- Call maximum profit is theoretically unlimited; put maximum profit is
  large but capped (underlying floor is zero).

## Summary
Long call and long put payoff diagrams translate the intrinsic value
formulas from `[21.04]` into a visual, expiry-day profit/loss picture:
both share a capped, known maximum loss (the premium paid) against a
large or unlimited potential profit, with breakeven offset from the
strike by the premium amount. This sets up the next lesson's mirror-image
treatment: what the payoff diagram looks like from the *seller's* side —
short call and short put — where the risk/reward asymmetry flips
entirely.

## Revision Notes
- Long call payoff = max(0, Underlying − Strike) − Premium.
- Long put payoff = max(0, Strike − Underlying) − Premium.
- Max loss (both) = Premium paid. Fixed, known upfront.
- Breakeven: Call = Strike + Premium. Put = Strike − Premium.
- Max profit: Call = unlimited. Put = capped (underlying floor = 0).
- Payoff diagrams describe expiry-day outcomes only — not value at any
  earlier point in time.

## Quiz
1. What is the maximum loss on a long call position?
   a) Unlimited  b) The strike price  c) The premium paid  d) Zero

2. What is the breakeven price for a long put with strike 500 and premium 20?
   a) 520  b) 480  c) 500  d) 20

3. Why is a long call's maximum profit theoretically unlimited?
   a) Premiums always rise  b) The underlying's price can rise indefinitely  c) Strikes adjust automatically  d) It isn't — it's always capped

4. A payoff diagram shows profit/loss:
   a) At any point before expiry  b) Only at expiry  c) Only if the option is ITM  d) Only for the seller

5. True or False: A long put's maximum profit is unlimited.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **c)** — The premium paid, regardless of how unfavorably the underlying moves.
2. **b) 480** — Strike (500) − Premium (20) = 480.
3. **b)** — The underlying's price has no theoretical upper bound.
4. **b)** — Payoff diagrams describe expiry-day outcomes only.
5. **b) False** — Capped, since the underlying can only fall to zero.
