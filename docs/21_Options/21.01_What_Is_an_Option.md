# [21.01] What Is an Option — Calls and Puts Explained

## Core Concepts

### Definition
An **option contract** grants its buyer the **right, but not the
obligation**, to buy or sell an underlying asset at a predetermined
price (the **strike price**, `[21.03]`) on or before a specific expiry
date, in exchange for an upfront payment called the **premium**. A
**call option** grants the right to *buy* the underlying at the strike
price; a **put option** grants the right to *sell* the underlying at the
strike price. This "right, not obligation" structure is options'
single most important distinguishing feature from futures (`[20.01]`),
where both parties are equally *obligated* to transact — an option buyer
can simply let the option expire worthless if exercising it isn't
favorable, losing only the premium paid.

### Intuition
This asymmetry — the buyer has a choice, the seller does not — is the
foundation of every subsequent options concept this module develops:
because the buyer's downside is capped at the premium paid (they simply
won't exercise an unfavorable option) while their upside can be
substantial, the seller (who receives the premium) takes on the mirror-
image risk profile, obligated to fulfill the contract if the buyer
chooses to exercise, covered fully in `[21.02]`.

### Mental Model
```
CALL OPTION                        PUT OPTION

Right to BUY the underlying        Right to SELL the underlying
at the strike price                at the strike price

Buyer profits if underlying        Buyer profits if underlying
price RISES above strike           price FALLS below strike
(plus premium paid)                (minus premium paid)

Buyer's max loss = PREMIUM PAID (both call and put) --
this is TRUE REGARDLESS of direction, since the buyer
simply won't exercise an unfavorable option

KEY DISTINCTION FROM FUTURES ([20.01]):
  Futures: BOTH parties OBLIGATED to transact
  Options: BUYER has a RIGHT (optional); SELLER has an
           OBLIGATION (if buyer chooses to exercise)
```

### Visual Explanation
Unlike a futures position, where losses accumulate proportionally and
without a hard floor (for short positions, `[20.09]`), an option buyer's
maximum possible loss is always precisely known and capped in advance —
exactly the premium paid — regardless of how unfavorably the underlying
moves, a structurally different risk characteristic worth internalizing
from this very first lesson.

## Real World Analogy
An option is like paying a small, non-refundable deposit to reserve the
right to buy a specific house at a fixed price within the next three
months, without being obligated to actually complete the purchase — if
the house's market value rises well above your locked-in price, you
exercise your right and buy at the favorable, lower price; if the
house's value falls, you simply let your reservation lapse, losing only
the deposit, not the full difference in value.

## Indian Market Example
Indian retail traders extensively use Nifty 50 and Bank Nifty index
options — the NSE's options segment sees some of the highest trading
volumes globally, with retail traders frequently buying call options when
bullish or put options when bearish, drawn specifically by the capped,
known-in-advance maximum loss (the premium) compared to futures'
uncapped risk profile (`[20.09]`).

## US Market Example
US options traders similarly use calls and puts on both individual
stocks and broad index products (SPX, SPY options) — US options markets
additionally offer weekly expiries on many underlyings, providing more
granular expiry timing choices than were historically available in
earlier decades of options trading.

## Historical Example
Modern standardized, exchange-traded options trading began with the
Chicago Board Options Exchange's founding in 1973, alongside the
concurrent development of the Black-Scholes option pricing model
(`[22.01]`, previewed) — a landmark combination of market infrastructure
and financial theory that transformed options from a niche, informally-
traded instrument into the massive, liquid market they represent today.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional options desks view calls and puts as fundamental building
blocks for constructing considerably more sophisticated, customized risk
profiles — combining multiple options (spreads, `[21.08]`; straddles/
strangles, `[21.09]`) to express nuanced views on direction, volatility,
and time that a single call or put alone cannot capture.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders new to options should specifically internalize the
"right, not obligation" distinction as the single most important
starting concept — many trading mistakes described later in this module
(particularly `[21.12]`'s theta decay trap) stem from misunderstanding
this fundamental asymmetry between buyer and seller positions.

## Professional Insights
Professional options traders emphasize that while an option buyer's
maximum loss is capped and known in advance, this doesn't mean options
buying is inherently "safer" than other instruments — the probability of
losing the entire premium is often considerably higher than intuition
suggests, a nuance this module will develop fully through payoff
diagrams (`[21.05]`-`[21.06]`) and Module 22's Greeks.

## Common Mistakes
- Confusing options with futures, missing the fundamental "right vs.
  obligation" distinction.
- Assuming a capped maximum loss means options buying is automatically
  low-risk, without appreciating the high probability of losing the
  full premium.
- Not understanding that calls and puts are mirror-image opposites,
  profiting from opposite directional moves.

## Misconceptions
- **"Options and futures work the same way, just with different
  names."** Options grant the buyer a right without obligation; futures
  obligate both parties equally — a fundamental structural difference.
- **"A capped maximum loss means options buying is inherently low-
  risk."** While the loss amount is capped, the probability of losing
  the entire premium can be substantial, requiring careful understanding
  developed throughout this module.
- **"Calls and puts are essentially the same instrument with a different
  label."** They are mirror-image opposites — calls profit from upward
  moves, puts profit from downward moves.

## Key Takeaways
- An option grants the buyer a right, not an obligation, to transact at
  a predetermined strike price before expiry, in exchange for a premium.
- A call option is the right to buy; a put option is the right to sell.
- The option buyer's maximum loss is capped at the premium paid,
  regardless of how unfavorably the underlying moves.
- This "right vs. obligation" asymmetry is the foundation for every
  subsequent options concept in this module.

## Summary
An option contract — granting its buyer the right, but not the
obligation, to transact at a predetermined strike price, in exchange for
a premium — represents a fundamentally different risk structure from
futures' symmetric obligation (`[20.01]`), with calls and puts serving as
mirror-image directional tools. This lesson establishes options'
foundational vocabulary and asymmetric structure, setting up the next
lesson's detailed treatment of the option seller's mirror-image, less
intuitive risk profile.

## Quiz
1. What is an option contract?
   a) An obligation for both parties  b) A right, not an obligation, to transact at a predetermined strike price  c) A type of stock  d) A dividend policy

2. What is a call option?
   a) The right to sell  b) The right to buy the underlying at the strike price  c) An obligation to buy  d) A margin call

3. What is a put option?
   a) The right to buy  b) The right to sell the underlying at the strike price  c) The same as a call  d) A type of bond

4. What is an option buyer's maximum possible loss?
   a) Unlimited  b) The premium paid, capped  c) Always zero  d) The full notional value

5. True or False: Options and futures obligate both parties equally.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — A right, not an obligation, to transact at a predetermined strike price.
2. **b)** — The right to buy the underlying at the strike price.
3. **b)** — The right to sell the underlying at the strike price.
4. **b)** — The premium paid, capped.
5. **b) False** — Futures obligate both parties; options give the buyer a right without obligation.
