# [21.07] Basic Option Strategies — Covered Call, Protective Put

> Module: Options · Lesson 7 of 12 · Estimated time: 35 minutes

## Learning Objectives
- Construct a covered call (long stock + short call) and explain how it reshapes payoff versus holding stock alone.
- Construct a protective put (long stock + long put) and explain how it functions as portfolio insurance.
- Calculate breakeven, max profit, and max loss for both combined positions.
- Recognize why these two strategies are the natural first step from single-leg options (`[21.05]`, `[21.06]`) to multi-leg structures.

## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **covered call** combines owning 100 shares (or one lot) of the
underlying with selling a call against it — "covered" because the
seller already owns the shares needed to deliver if assigned, removing
the unlimited-loss exposure a *naked* short call (`[21.06]`) carries. A
**protective put** combines owning the underlying with buying a put —
using the put as insurance that caps downside loss below the strike, in
exchange for paying the put's premium.

### Intuition
Both strategies take a stock position the investor already wants to
hold and attach an option to change its risk profile in a specific
direction. Covered call: "I'll give up some upside above a certain price
in exchange for income today." Protective put: "I'll pay a small,
known cost today in exchange for a floor under how much I can lose."

### Mental Model
```
COVERED CALL = Long Stock + Short Call
  Payoff = (Stock P&L) + [Premium - max(0, Underlying - Strike)]
  Max Profit = (Strike - Stock Purchase Price) + Premium  [capped]
  Max Loss   = Stock Purchase Price - Premium              [large, stock can fall to 0]
  Breakeven  = Stock Purchase Price - Premium

PROTECTIVE PUT = Long Stock + Long Put
  Payoff = (Stock P&L) + [max(0, Strike - Underlying) - Premium]
  Max Loss   = (Stock Purchase Price - Strike) + Premium   [capped]
  Max Profit = Unlimited (stock can rise indefinitely) - Premium
  Breakeven  = Stock Purchase Price + Premium
```

### Visual Explanation
A covered call's combined payoff line rises with the stock up to the
strike, then flattens — the short call's cap kicks in exactly where
`[21.06]`'s short call payoff would flatten on its own. A protective
put's combined payoff line falls with the stock down to the strike, then
flattens — the long put's floor kicks in exactly where `[21.05]`'s long
put payoff would begin gaining.

## Real World Analogy
A covered call is like renting out a spare room you already own: you
collect steady income (the premium), but if property values in the area
suddenly spike, you've already agreed to a ceiling on what that room
earns you (the strike). A protective put is like paying for insurance
on a car you own: a small recurring cost that guarantees you never lose
more than a fixed amount even if the car is totaled.

## Indian Market Example
An investor holds 1 lot of Reliance Industries shares purchased at
₹2,800 and sells a 1-month 2,900 call for a premium of ₹40 (illustrative
figures). If Reliance stays at or below 2,900 at expiry, the investor
keeps the ₹40 premium in full; if Reliance rises to 3,000, the investor's
gain is capped at (2,900 − 2,800) + 40 = ₹140 per share, forgoing the
extra ₹60 they'd have earned holding the stock alone. **Hypothetical
Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
An investor holds 100 shares of MSFT purchased at $400 and buys a
1-month 380 put for a premium of $5 (illustrative figures). If MSFT
falls to $340, the loss is capped at (400 − 380) + 5 = $25 per share —
far less than the $60 per share loss the investor would have faced
holding the stock alone. **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
During the March 2020 COVID crash, investors holding protective puts
purchased in the preceding weeks saw their portfolio losses capped well
below the roughly 38% Nifty decline (January 2020 high to March 2020
low) and the comparable ~34% S&P 500 decline over the same window,
illustrating the "insurance" function this lesson describes in a real,
sharp drawdown.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional portfolio managers run covered call programs (often called
"buy-write" strategies) systematically across large equity holdings to
generate consistent premium income, and use protective puts (or put
spreads, to reduce the insurance cost) as tail-risk hedges ahead of known
event risk such as elections or central bank meetings (`[06.01]`,
`[03.10]`).

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors often adopt covered calls on stocks they already hold
long-term, viewing the premium as "extra yield" — but should recognize
they've capped their upside for that period; retail protective put
usage is less common due to the recurring premium cost, though it
becomes attractive specifically around known event risk.

## Professional Insights
Professional covered-call writers often select strikes based on a
target delta (Module 22, previewed) rather than an arbitrary price
level, balancing premium income against the probability of the call
being assigned; protective put buyers similarly weigh strike distance
against cost — a closer strike costs more but caps loss earlier.

## Common Mistakes
- Selling a covered call at a strike below the original purchase price,
  guaranteeing a loss if assigned even after collecting the premium.
- Treating a protective put's premium as a sunk cost regret if it
  expires worthless — insurance that isn't "used" still did its job by
  removing tail risk during the period held.
- Forgetting that a covered call still carries large downside risk below
  breakeven — it caps *upside*, not *downside*.

## Misconceptions
- **"A covered call has no risk since I'm collecting premium."** The
  underlying stock can still fall sharply; the premium only offsets a
  small portion of that loss.
- **"A protective put guarantees profit."** It only guarantees a floor
  on loss, not that the position is profitable overall — the premium
  paid reduces net returns in flat or rising markets too.
- **"Covered calls and protective puts are opposite strategies."** They
  address different concerns (income vs. insurance) and can even be
  combined into a single position (a "collar," beyond this lesson's
  scope).

## Key Takeaways
- Covered call = Long Stock + Short Call — caps upside, collects
  premium, still exposed to full downside minus the premium collected.
- Protective put = Long Stock + Long Put — caps downside at a known
  floor, in exchange for paying the put's premium.
- Both combined payoffs are built by simply adding the stock's linear
  payoff to the option leg's payoff from `[21.05]`/`[21.06]`.
- Covered call breakeven = Purchase Price − Premium; Protective put
  breakeven = Purchase Price + Premium.

## Summary
Covered calls and protective puts are the simplest multi-leg option
strategies, each combining stock ownership with a single option leg to
deliberately reshape the position's risk: income generation with a
capped upside (covered call) or downside insurance at a known cost
(protective put). Both are built directly from the single-leg payoff
formulas in `[21.05]` and `[21.06]`, simply added to the underlying
stock's linear payoff. The next lesson extends this combining logic to
pure options-only spreads — no stock required — starting with bull call
and bear put spreads.

## Revision Notes
- Covered call = Long Stock + Short Call. Caps upside at Strike +
  Premium above purchase price; still exposed to downside minus premium.
- Protective put = Long Stock + Long Put. Floors downside at Strike −
  Purchase Price − Premium; costs the premium in flat/up markets.
- Combined payoff = Stock P&L + Option leg payoff (`[21.05]`/`[21.06]`).
- Covered call breakeven = Purchase Price − Premium.
- Protective put breakeven = Purchase Price + Premium.

## Quiz
1. A covered call consists of:
   a) Short stock + long call  b) Long stock + short call  c) Long stock + long put  d) Short stock + short put

2. What does a protective put primarily provide?
   a) Extra income  b) Unlimited profit  c) A floor on potential loss  d) Guaranteed profit

3. If a covered call's strike is below the stock's purchase price, what happens if assigned?
   a) Guaranteed profit  b) A loss occurs even after the premium  c) No effect  d) The position doubles

4. What reduces a protective put owner's return in a flat or rising market?
   a) The stock dividend  b) The premium paid for the put  c) Brokerage fees only  d) Nothing

5. True or False: A covered call removes all downside risk.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Long stock + short call.
2. **c)** — A floor on potential loss, at the cost of the premium paid.
3. **b)** — A loss can occur even after collecting the premium, if the strike is below the purchase price.
4. **b)** — The premium paid reduces net returns whenever the put isn't needed.
5. **b) False** — It caps upside but leaves substantial downside exposure, reduced only by the premium collected.
