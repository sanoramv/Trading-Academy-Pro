## Core Concepts

### Definition
The **Harshad Mehta scam** (uncovered in April 1992) was a major
securities fraud in which stockbroker Harshad Mehta exploited
weaknesses in the Indian banking system's inter-bank securities
settlement process — using fraudulently obtained bank receipts and
funds meant for government securities transactions to instead fund
massive, leveraged purchases of stocks — driving a sharp, artificial
rally in Indian equity prices (particularly certain favored stocks)
before the scheme's exposure triggered a severe market crash and a
banking-sector crisis. The scandal directly prompted the establishment
of SEBI's expanded statutory powers (`[03.02]`) and NSE's 1992
founding with its modern, transparent electronic trading infrastructure
(`[01.04]`, `[03.01]`), making it arguably the single most consequential
event in shaping modern Indian market regulation.

### Intuition
Mehta exploited a specific settlement mechanism vulnerability — banks
used "Ready Forward" (RF) deals (a form of repo transaction) for
short-term government securities trading, and Mehta, through complicit
bank officials, obtained fake or unauthorized Bank Receipts (BRs) that
allowed him to divert bank funds intended for these government
securities transactions into the stock market instead, driving up
prices with essentially borrowed, misappropriated capital before the
fraud was discovered and the artificial price support suddenly collapsed.

```
  2. Mehta, WITH COMPLICIT BANK OFFICIALS, obtained FAKE/UNAUTHORIZED

     government securities held on a bank's behalf

  4. DIVERTED funds used to make MASSIVE, LEVERAGED purchases of
     STOCKS -- artificially driving UP prices of favored stocks
     (a "bull run" built on misappropriated bank capital)

  5. April 1992: the scheme is EXPOSED (banks discover missing
     funds/securities) -- artificial price support COLLAPSES --
     SHARP market crash and banking-sector crisis follows

  6. REGULATORY AFTERMATH: SEBI's powers SIGNIFICANTLY expanded
     (statutory authority, [03.02]); NSE FOUNDED (1992) with
     modern electronic, transparent settlement infrastructure
     SPECIFICALLY to prevent this TYPE of settlement-system abuse
```

## Real World Analogy
Mehta's scheme resembles someone discovering a flaw in a bank's internal
funds-transfer verification process, using forged authorization slips
to redirect large sums into an entirely different, undisclosed
investment — the scheme appears to work as long as the forged documents
go unnoticed, but once discovered, the sudden reversal (recalling the
misdirected funds) causes the artificially-inflated investment's value
to collapse just as suddenly as it rose.

## Indian Market Example
This lesson's entire content is itself the Indian market example — the
Harshad Mehta scam remains the foundational Indian case study directly
referenced in `[03.01]`'s history lesson and `[03.02]`'s SEBI powers
discussion, illustrating the specific "crisis-to-reform pattern"
`[03.01]` identified as a recurring theme throughout Indian market history.

## US Market Example
While the Harshad Mehta scam is specific to India's banking settlement
system, comparable settlement/clearing infrastructure vulnerabilities
have prompted analogous US regulatory reforms historically — the US
adoption of a strengthened Depository Trust Company (DTC) infrastructure
following various historical settlement failures reflects a similar,
if distinct, "settlement vulnerability exposed, then reformed" pattern
across different markets and eras.

## Historical Example
The Harshad Mehta scam's exposure in April 1992 is widely regarded, per
`[03.01]`'s historical framing, as the single most direct catalyst for
NSE's founding later that same year — NSE's modern, screen-based,
electronic trading and settlement system was specifically designed to
eliminate the type of paper-based settlement vulnerability (Bank
Receipts, physical documentation) that Mehta's scheme had exploited,
making this scandal arguably the most consequential single event in the
development of India's modern market infrastructure.

## Institutional Perspective
SEBI's transformation from a body with limited, largely advisory powers
into a statutory regulator with substantial enforcement authority
(`[03.02]`) traces directly to the regulatory gaps this scandal exposed
— the SEBI Act's 1992 amendments granting expanded powers were enacted
specifically in the scandal's immediate aftermath.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors who had participated in the artificially-fueled 1991-
92 rally, believing it reflected genuine market strength, experienced
significant losses once the scheme's exposure triggered the crash — a
concrete historical illustration of `[28.02]`'s fear/greed cycle and
the risks of participating in a rally without understanding its actual, underlying drivers.

## Professional Insights
Financial historians and market analysts consistently point to the
Harshad Mehta scam as the pivotal event separating India's "old,"
paper-based, opaque market infrastructure from the modern, electronic,
transparent system NSE introduced — a single scandal that catalyzed
more foundational market structure reform than perhaps any other event
in Indian market history.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating the Harshad Mehta scam as merely an interesting historical
  anecdote rather than the direct cause of NSE's founding and SEBI's
  modern regulatory powers.
- Underestimating how directly settlement/clearing infrastructure
  (`[01.09]`) connects to preventing this specific type of fraud.
- Assuming such settlement-based fraud is no longer possible given
  modern infrastructure, rather than understanding it as one specific,
  historically resolved vulnerability among evolving market risks.

## Misconceptions
- **"The Harshad Mehta scam was simply insider trading."** It was a
  more specific and structurally different form of fraud, exploiting
  bank settlement infrastructure vulnerabilities to divert funds into
  the stock market, distinct from insider trading's information-based mechanism.
- **"NSE's founding was unrelated to this scandal."** `[03.01]`
  establishes NSE's 1992 founding as directly, substantially motivated
  by the settlement transparency failures this scandal exposed.
- **"This event only matters for Indian market history, with no
  broader lesson."** The scandal illustrates a universal pattern —
  crisis exposing a genuine structural vulnerability, followed by
  meaningful reform — applicable broadly across markets and eras.

## Key Takeaways
- Harshad Mehta exploited bank settlement infrastructure (fraudulent
  Bank Receipts) to divert funds into a massive, leveraged, artificial stock rally.
- The scandal's April 1992 exposure triggered a sharp market crash and
  banking-sector crisis.
- Direct regulatory aftermath: SEBI's statutory powers significantly
  expanded, and NSE founded (1992) with modern electronic settlement infrastructure.
- Widely regarded as the single most consequential event shaping modern
  Indian market regulation and infrastructure.

## Summary
The Harshad Mehta scam exploited a specific vulnerability in India's
bank settlement infrastructure to fund an artificial, leveraged stock
market rally, and its April 1992 exposure — triggering a sharp crash —
directly catalyzed SEBI's expanded statutory powers and NSE's founding
with modern, transparent electronic settlement, making it arguably the
single most consequential event in shaping the modern Indian market
infrastructure this entire academy has taught throughout Module 01-03.
The next lesson examines the Ketan Parekh scam (2001) — a later,
different-mechanism scandal illustrating that market fraud continued
evolving even after this scandal's reforms.
