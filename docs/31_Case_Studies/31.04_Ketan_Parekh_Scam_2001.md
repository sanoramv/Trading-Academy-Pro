# [31.04] Ketan Parekh Scam (2001)

> Module: Case Studies · Lesson 4 of 10 · Estimated time: 30 minutes

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the Ketan Parekh scam's price manipulation mechanism ("pump and dump" via circular trading).
- Distinguish this scam's mechanism from Harshad Mehta's settlement-fraud approach (`[31.03]`).
- Trace the specific regulatory reforms this scandal prompted, particularly around circuit filters and surveillance.
- Understand market manipulation patterns as a recurring risk requiring ongoing regulatory vigilance.

## Prerequisites
- `[31.03]` Harshad Mehta Scam (1992) and Indian Market Reform
- `[03.09]` Indian Market Regulations — Insider Trading, Circuit Filters, Surveillance

## Core Concepts

### Definition
The **Ketan Parekh scam** (exposed in 2001) involved stockbroker Ketan
Parekh orchestrating a price manipulation scheme targeting a specific
group of stocks nicknamed the "K-10" — using circular trading (coordinated
buying/selling among a network of connected entities to create
artificial trading volume and price momentum), leveraged funding
(including funds allegedly borrowed from cooperative banks, echoing
`[31.03]`'s banking-system involvement in a different form), and
concentrated positions in relatively illiquid, mid-cap stocks to drive
dramatic, unsustainable price increases before the scheme collapsed
amid a broader market downturn and coinciding dot-com bubble unwind
(`[31.09]` previewed). The scandal directly prompted SEBI reforms
strengthening circuit filters and market surveillance systems
(`[03.09]`)  specifically designed to detect and limit this type of
concentrated, artificial price manipulation.

### Intuition
Where Mehta exploited bank settlement *infrastructure* to fund an
artificial rally, Parekh exploited the *market mechanism itself* —
concentrating trading in relatively illiquid stocks where a smaller
amount of coordinated capital could disproportionately move prices,
using circular trading among connected entities to manufacture the
appearance of genuine market demand and momentum, attracting
unsuspecting retail investors into what was ultimately an artificially inflated, unsustainable price structure.

### Mental Model
```
KETAN PAREKH SCAM MECHANISM (simplified):

  1. TARGET SELECTION: chose relatively ILLIQUID, mid-cap stocks
     (the "K-10") -- SMALLER capital could disproportionately
     move prices in thinly-traded names

  2. CIRCULAR TRADING: coordinated buying/selling among a NETWORK
     of connected entities/brokers -- created ARTIFICIAL volume
     and the APPEARANCE of genuine market demand/momentum

  3. LEVERAGED FUNDING: used borrowed funds (including from
     cooperative banks) to finance CONCENTRATED, large positions
     in these target stocks

  4. PRICE MANIPULATION: this combination drove DRAMATIC,
     UNSUSTAINABLE price increases in the K-10 stocks --
     attracting retail investors chasing the apparent momentum
     ([28.05]'s FOMO pattern, applied historically)

  5. 2001: scheme UNRAVELS amid broader market downturn (coinciding
     with the global dot-com bubble unwind, [31.09]) -- K-10 stock
     prices COLLAPSE, exposing the manipulation

  6. REGULATORY AFTERMATH: SEBI strengthens circuit filters and
     market SURVEILLANCE systems, [03.09], specifically to detect
     concentrated manipulation patterns like this one
```

## Real World Analogy
The Ketan Parekh scheme resembles a small group of coordinated bidders
at an auction for a relatively obscure item, repeatedly bidding against
each other to drive the apparent "market price" dramatically higher,
attracting genuine outside bidders who assume the rising price reflects
real, independent demand — only for the coordinated bidders to quietly
sell their own holdings into that artificially inflated demand before
the manipulation becomes apparent and the price collapses.

## Indian Market Example
This lesson's entire content is itself the Indian market example —
directly connected to `[03.09]`'s regulatory framework discussion,
where circuit filters and surveillance systems strengthened
specifically in response to this scandal remain a core part of
Indian market regulation today.

## US Market Example
While specific to Indian market structure, the circular trading
technique Parekh employed resembles "pump and dump" manipulation
schemes documented and prosecuted in US markets historically —
particularly in relatively illiquid, smaller-capitalization stocks
where concentrated coordinated trading can more easily create
artificial price momentum, reflecting a manipulation pattern that
recurs across markets rather than being unique to India.

## Historical Example
The Ketan Parekh scandal's unraveling coincided with the broader global
dot-com bubble's unwind in 2000-2001 (`[31.09]` previewed) — the
simultaneous broader market downturn is widely credited with exposing
the scheme, since the coordinated buying that had sustained the K-10
stocks' artificial prices could no longer be maintained once broader
market liquidity and sentiment deteriorated substantially.

## Institutional Perspective
SEBI's post-2001 surveillance system enhancements included improved
real-time monitoring for unusual trading patterns and concentration —
directly building on, and extending, the reforms `[31.03]`'s Harshad
Mehta scandal had already prompted a decade earlier, illustrating that
Indian market regulation has evolved through a sequence of scandal-
driven reforms rather than a single, complete overhaul.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors who chased the K-10 stocks' dramatic rally, believing
the price momentum reflected genuine market demand, experienced
significant losses once the manipulation was exposed and prices
collapsed — a concrete historical illustration of `[28.05]`'s FOMO
pattern and the specific danger of chasing price momentum in
relatively illiquid stocks without understanding what's actually driving the move.

## Professional Insights
Market analysts studying the Ketan Parekh scandal often highlight the
warning signs available even at the time — extreme price divergence
from broader market movement, concentration in illiquid names, and
unusually persistent buying pressure — as patterns that `[13.03]`'s
supply/demand zone analysis and volume-based confirmation (`[14.01]`)
could, in principle, help a disciplined trader recognize as atypical.

## Common Mistakes
- Confusing the Ketan Parekh scam's circular-trading mechanism with
  Harshad Mehta's settlement-fraud mechanism (`[31.03]`) — they are distinct approaches.
- Assuming price momentum in an individual stock always reflects
  genuine, independent market demand rather than potentially coordinated manipulation.
- Underestimating how illiquid stocks are specifically more vulnerable
  to concentrated manipulation than highly liquid, broadly-held names.

## Misconceptions
- **"The Ketan Parekh and Harshad Mehta scams were essentially the
  same type of fraud."** They employed distinctly different
  mechanisms — settlement-infrastructure fraud (Mehta) versus
  circular-trading price manipulation (Parekh).
- **"This scandal had no lasting regulatory impact."** It directly
  prompted specific strengthening of circuit filters and surveillance
  systems (`[03.09]`) still in use today.
- **"Manipulation schemes like this can't happen with modern
  surveillance."** While detection has improved significantly,
  understanding historical manipulation patterns remains relevant for
  recognizing analogous warning signs in any era.

## Key Takeaways
- Ketan Parekh's scheme used circular trading among connected entities
  to manipulate prices of relatively illiquid, mid-cap "K-10" stocks.
- The scheme unraveled in 2001, coinciding with the broader global
  dot-com bubble unwind exposing the artificial price support.
- Direct regulatory reform: strengthened circuit filters and market
  surveillance systems, still core to Indian market regulation today.
- Distinct mechanism from Harshad Mehta's settlement-infrastructure
  fraud — a market-manipulation approach rather than a banking-system exploitation.

## Summary
The Ketan Parekh scam manipulated prices of relatively illiquid,
mid-cap "K-10" stocks through circular trading among connected
entities, attracting unsuspecting retail investors chasing artificial
momentum before the scheme unraveled alongside the broader 2000-2001
dot-com bubble unwind, directly prompting SEBI's strengthened circuit
filter and surveillance reforms (`[03.09]`) that remain core to Indian
market regulation today. The next lesson turns to the 2004 Indian
election result crash — a very different kind of market shock, driven
by political outcome uncertainty rather than fraud or systemic financial failure.

3. **b)** — Strengthened circuit filters and market surveillance.
4. **b)** — Parekh used market manipulation; Mehta exploited bank settlement infrastructure.
5. **a) True** — Smaller amounts of coordinated capital can more easily move prices in thinly-traded stocks.
