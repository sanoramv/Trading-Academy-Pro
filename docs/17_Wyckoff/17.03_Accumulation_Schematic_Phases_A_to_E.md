## Core Concepts

### Definition
The **Wyckoff Accumulation Schematic** describes the typical, idealized
sequence of events occurring as the Composite Man (`[17.06]`, previewed)
builds a large position within a trading range before eventually marking
price up. The schematic is divided into **five phases (A through E)**:
**Phase A** (stopping the prior downtrend — Preliminary Support, Selling
Climax, Automatic Rally, Secondary Test); **Phase B** (building the
cause — extended range-bound consolidation); **Phase C** (the test — a
Spring or shakeout probing for remaining supply); **Phase D** (evidence
of strength — Sign of Strength rally, Last Point of Support); and
**Phase E** (the markup — price leaves the range in a sustained uptrend).

### Intuition
Each phase reflects a specific step in the Composite Man's deliberate,
patient accumulation process: Phase A stops the prior decline and
establishes the range boundaries; Phase B patiently builds the "cause"
(`[17.02]`'s Law of Cause and Effect); Phase C tests whether meaningful
supply remains by deliberately probing below support (the Spring); Phase
D confirms accumulation is essentially complete through decisive,
high-effort upward moves; and Phase E is simply the resulting "effect" —
the markup phase — playing out.

### Mental Model
```
WYCKOFF ACCUMULATION SCHEMATIC (5 phases)

PHASE A: STOPPING THE DOWNTREND
  PS (Preliminary Support) -> SC (Selling Climax) ->
  AR (Automatic Rally) -> ST (Secondary Test)

PHASE B: BUILDING THE CAUSE
  Extended range-bound consolidation, multiple tests of
  both range boundaries, building "cause" per [17.02]

PHASE C: THE TEST
  Spring (deliberate probe BELOW support, shaking out
  remaining weak holders) -- OR a less dramatic "Test"

PHASE D: EVIDENCE OF STRENGTH
  SOS (Sign of Strength -- strong rally through range
  resistance) -> LPS (Last Point of Support -- final
  pullback finding support at a HIGHER level)

PHASE E: THE MARKUP
  Price LEAVES the range decisively, sustained uptrend
  begins (the "effect" of Phase B's "cause")
```

### Visual Explanation
The Spring in Phase C is often the schematic's most misunderstood event
— it appears, in isolation, to be a bearish breakdown below support, but
within the full schematic context it represents a deliberate shakeout
designed to trigger stop-losses and attract short-sellers, providing
exactly the additional supply the Composite Man needs to absorb before
the eventual markup.

### Fully Worked Example
**Hypothetical Example.** Before the homework asks you to label a real
chart, walk through one complete, fully-labeled sequence first — a
hypothetical stock, "Company X," after a prolonged decline from Rs 500:

| Date (illustrative) | Price action | Phase | Event |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nov 1 | Decline slows near Rs 320; some buying appears but doesn't hold | A | **PS** — Preliminary Support |
| Nov 3 | Panic selloff plunges to Rs 280 on 5x average volume, closes well off the low | A | **SC** — Selling Climax |
| Nov 4-8 | Sharp bounce to Rs 340 as selling pressure exhausts | A | **AR** — Automatic Rally |
| Nov 10-20 | Retest down toward Rs 290-300, on much lighter volume than the SC; holds above the Nov 3 low | A | **ST** — Secondary Test (confirms Phase A) |
| Dec-Jan | Range-bound between Rs 290 (support) and Rs 340 (resistance) for ~2 months, repeated tests of both boundaries | B | Building the "cause" (`[17.02]`) |
| Feb 5 | Price briefly breaks below Rs 290 support, down to Rs 275 intraday, then sharply reverses and closes back above Rs 290 the same day | C | **Spring** — shakeout, not a breakdown |
| Feb 20 | Strong rally on high volume decisively clears the Rs 340 resistance, reaching Rs 380 | D | **SOS** — Sign of Strength |
| Feb 25-28 | Pullback holds at Rs 350 — well above the old Rs 340 resistance, now acting as support | D | **LPS** — Last Point of Support |
| Mar onward | Sustained uptrend continues beyond Rs 380 | E | Markup |

Notice what makes the Spring (Feb 5) identifiable *as a Spring* rather
than a genuine breakdown: it happened *after* Phase A and B were already
established (so there was a real range to shake out of), on a single-day
piercing move, with an immediate same-day close back above the broken
level — exactly the "deliberate probe, not a breakdown" signature the
Visual Explanation above describes. When you attempt the homework's real
chart, look for this same combination (prior established range + brief
piercing move + fast reclaim), not just "price went below support."

## Real World Analogy
The Accumulation Schematic is like a patient real estate investor quietly
buying up distressed properties in a declining neighborhood: first
absorbing the initial panic-selling wave (Phase A), then patiently
continuing to acquire properties at similar prices over an extended
period while the neighborhood's reputation remains poor (Phase B),
deliberately allowing one final scare to shake out any remaining
reluctant sellers at bargain prices (Phase C's Spring), then beginning to
visibly improve and market the properties once acquisition is complete
(Phase D's SOS), before the neighborhood's reputation and property values
finally rise substantially (Phase E's markup).

## Indian Market Example
Indian Wyckoff practitioners analyzing a Nifty 50 constituent stock after
a prolonged decline specifically watch for this five-phase sequence —
identifying a Selling Climax on unusually high volume (`[14.05]`),
followed by extended range-bound consolidation, a Spring probing below
range support, and eventually a Sign of Strength rally confirming
accumulation is likely complete before considering a long entry near the
Last Point of Support.

## US Market Example
US Wyckoff analysts studying historical US equity bear-market bottoms
frequently identify textbook Accumulation Schematics playing out over
several months — the extended Phase B consolidation period in these
historical examples often spans considerably longer than casual observers
initially expect, illustrating the genuine patience required for large
operators to complete substantial accumulation.

## Historical Example
Wyckoff's original correspondence course materials from the 1930s
(`[17.01]`) first formalized this specific five-phase schematic structure,
and it remains essentially unchanged in modern Wyckoff-based education —
a striking example of a nearly century-old analytical framework
persisting with minimal modification into contemporary trading practice.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional Wyckoff-trained analysts specifically use Phase B's
duration and range width, combined with the Law of Cause and Effect
(`[17.02]`), to estimate a rough proportional target for Phase E's
eventual markup — treating the accumulation range's "cause" as a
quantifiable input for post-breakout price projection.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders new to this schematic should practice identifying each
phase sequentially on historical charts, resisting the temptation to
jump directly to "Spring-spotting" (Phase C) without first confirming
Phase A and Phase B context — misidentifying a Spring without proper
phase context is a common, costly error this module's later lessons will
address further.

## Professional Insights
Professional Wyckoff practitioners emphasize that real-world accumulation
structures rarely match this idealized schematic exactly — some phases
may be compressed, extended, or occur in a slightly different sequence —
requiring the same kind of developed judgment `[17.01]` described, rather
than mechanical, rigid template-matching.

## Common Mistakes
- Attempting to identify a Spring (Phase C) without first confirming
  Phase A and Phase B context.
- Expecting every real-world accumulation structure to match the
  idealized schematic exactly, without allowing for natural variation.
- Entering a position immediately upon spotting what appears to be a
  Selling Climax, without waiting for the full schematic's later
  confirming phases.

## Misconceptions
- **"Every accumulation structure follows this exact five-phase sequence
  precisely."** Real-world structures often show compressed, extended, or
  slightly reordered phases, requiring judgment rather than rigid
  template-matching.
- **"A Selling Climax alone confirms accumulation is occurring."** Phase
  A's Selling Climax merely stops the prior downtrend; genuine
  confirmation requires observing the full subsequent schematic,
  particularly Phase D's Sign of Strength.
- **"The Spring (Phase C) is simply a bearish breakdown, unrelated to
  accumulation."** Within full schematic context, the Spring is a
  deliberate shakeout event that typically precedes, not signals against,
  eventual markup — covered in full in `[17.05]`.

## Key Takeaways
- The Accumulation Schematic has five phases: A (stopping the downtrend),
  B (building the cause), C (the test/Spring), D (evidence of strength),
  E (the markup).
- Key Phase A/D events: Preliminary Support, Selling Climax, Automatic
  Rally, Secondary Test, Sign of Strength, Last Point of Support.
- The Spring in Phase C is a deliberate shakeout, not simply a bearish
  breakdown — full treatment in `[17.05]`.
- Real-world structures require judgment; they rarely match the idealized
  schematic exactly.

## Summary
The Wyckoff Accumulation Schematic's five phases — stopping the
downtrend, building the cause, testing for remaining supply, confirming
strength, and finally marking up — describe the Composite Man's typical,
patient accumulation process, translating `[17.02]`'s Three Laws into a
concrete, observable structural sequence. This sets up the next lesson's
mirror-image treatment: the Distribution Schematic, describing the
equivalent process occurring at market tops.
