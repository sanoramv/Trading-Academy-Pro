## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **Spring** is a deliberate (or apparent) price move below an
established trading range's support, occurring during Phase C of the
Accumulation Schematic (`[17.03]`), which then quickly reverses back
above support — designed to trigger stop-losses and attract short
sellers, providing the Composite Man additional supply to absorb. An
**Upthrust** (or **Upthrust After Distribution/UTAD**) is the mirror
event during Phase C of the Distribution Schematic (`[17.04]`) — a
deliberate move above resistance that quickly reverses back below,
trapping late buyers. A **Test** is a more general Wyckoff term for any
subsequent, lower-volume probe of a previously significant level (a prior
Spring low, a prior Selling Climax low, etc.) that confirms the level is
holding, without necessarily breaching it as dramatically as the original
Spring or Upthrust.

### Intuition
These events exploit the exact same psychological trap `[13.04]`
described for generic fakeouts, but Wyckoff's framework provides the
crucial *contextual* explanation for *why* they occur specifically at
this point (Phase C, following Phase A/B) rather than randomly —
untrained traders see only "price broke support, then reversed" and
react emotionally; Wyckoff-trained traders recognize the specific phase
context and anticipate the event's likely occurrence and function.

### Mental Model
```
SPRING vs UPTHRUST vs TEST

SPRING (Accumulation, Phase C):
  Price probes BELOW range support -> quickly reverses back
  ABOVE support -> triggers stop-losses/short entries ->
  provides Composite Man additional SUPPLY to absorb

UPTHRUST / UTAD (Distribution, Phase C):
  Price probes ABOVE range resistance -> quickly reverses
  back BELOW resistance -> triggers stop-losses/long entries
  -> provides Composite Man opportunity to SELL into demand

TEST (general term, can occur in either context):
  A SUBSEQUENT, typically LOWER-VOLUME probe of a previously
  significant level, confirming it holds -- may or may not
  breach the level as dramatically as the original Spring/
  Upthrust
```

### Visual Explanation
The single most reliable distinguishing signature separating a genuine
Spring from a real breakdown is the *speed and volume character* of the
reversal — a genuine Spring typically shows a swift, often lower-volume
reversal back above support within a short time, while a real breakdown
shows sustained follow-through with continued volume in the breakdown
direction, mirroring `[13.04]`'s general fakeout-detection principle
applied specifically within Wyckoff's Phase C context.

## Real World Analogy
A Spring is like a store deliberately running a dramatic, very brief
"everything must go" clearance sale that appears to signal genuine
distress, prompting nervous competitors to also panic-discount — but the
store quickly returns to normal pricing once it has absorbed exactly the
bargain-hunting foot traffic it wanted, revealing the apparent distress
was temporary and deliberate rather than a sign of genuine, sustained
trouble.

## Indian Market Example
Indian Wyckoff-trained Bank Nifty traders specifically watch for Springs
occurring precisely within an already-identified Phase C context (after
confirming Phase A and Phase B, per `[17.03]`) — a probe below support
occurring without this established phase context is treated with far
more caution, since it lacks the specific structural setup that makes
genuine Springs identifiable with reasonable confidence.

## US Market Example
US Wyckoff practitioners studying historical Spring examples specifically
note that the subsequent Test (a lower-volume, less dramatic re-probe of
the Spring's low) often provides the highest-confidence entry
opportunity — rather than acting immediately on the Spring itself, many
practitioners wait for this confirming Test before establishing a
position.

## Historical Example
Wyckoff's original 1930s course material (`[17.01]`) specifically
emphasized Springs and Tests as advanced, higher-skill concepts requiring
considerable practiced judgment — a characterization that remains
accurate today, as Spring/Upthrust misidentification continues to be
cited as among the most common errors newer Wyckoff students make.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional Wyckoff-trained analysts specifically track volume
characteristics during the Spring/Upthrust event itself as a key
confirmation input — a Spring occurring on unusually LOW volume (limited
genuine selling pressure) is considered a stronger, higher-confidence
signal than one occurring on unusually high volume, which might instead
indicate genuine, sustained selling pressure rather than a deliberate
shakeout.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders should specifically practice waiting for the subsequent
Test before acting on a suspected Spring or Upthrust, rather than
attempting to enter immediately at the Spring/Upthrust's exact low or
high — this more patient approach sacrifices some potential profit for
meaningfully improved confirmation and reduced risk of misidentification.

## Professional Insights
Professional Wyckoff analysts caution that not every apparent Spring or
Upthrust succeeds — a probe that appears to be a Spring can occasionally
develop into a genuine, sustained breakdown instead, particularly during
unusually strong prevailing trend or macro conditions (`[06.09]`'s
geopolitical shock discussion) — appropriate risk management (a defined
stop, previewed from Module 21) remains essential even when a Spring
appears well-confirmed.

## Common Mistakes
- Acting immediately on an apparent Spring or Upthrust without first
  confirming proper Phase A/B context.
- Ignoring volume characteristics during the Spring/Upthrust probe
  itself, missing a valuable confirmation signal.
- Failing to wait for a subsequent Test before establishing a position,
  sacrificing meaningful confirmation for marginally better entry price.
- Assuming every apparent Spring/Upthrust succeeds, neglecting appropriate
  risk management.

## Misconceptions
- **"A Spring always occurs on high volume."** Genuine Springs often
  occur on relatively LOWER volume during the probe itself; high volume
  during a support breach more often suggests genuine, sustained selling
  pressure rather than a deliberate shakeout.
- **"Every probe below support is a Spring."** Only probes occurring
  within proper Phase C context (following confirmed Phase A/B) should be
  treated as likely Springs — random probes lacking this context require
  far more caution.
- **"Springs and Upthrusts always succeed."** Some apparent Springs
  develop into genuine, sustained breakdowns instead, particularly during
  strong prevailing trend or macro shock conditions — risk management
  remains essential regardless.

## Key Takeaways
- A Spring probes below support then swiftly reverses, providing supply
  for the Composite Man to absorb (Accumulation Phase C).
- An Upthrust/UTAD probes above resistance then swiftly reverses, trapping
  late buyers (Distribution Phase C).
- A Test is a subsequent, typically lower-volume probe confirming a
  previously significant level holds.
- Volume characteristics during the probe, proper phase context, and
  waiting for a confirming Test all improve identification confidence.
