# [06.09] Geopolitics and Markets (Wars, Sanctions, Trade Deals)

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish sanctions, trade deals, and armed conflict as distinct market drivers.
- Learn why geopolitical market reactions are often sharp but frequently short-lived.
- Apply this understanding to interpreting geopolitical headlines responsibly.

## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Geopolitical risk** refers to the potential for political, military, or
diplomatic events between or within nations to affect economic and market
conditions. Key mechanisms include: **armed conflict** (disrupting trade
routes, commodity supply — particularly energy — and regional economic
activity), **sanctions** (restricting trade, financial transactions, or
capital flows involving specific countries/entities), and **trade
deals/disputes** (tariffs, trade agreements, or trade wars affecting the
cost and flow of goods between countries). Markets generally price
geopolitical risk through the same channels covered throughout this
module: commodity prices (`[06.08]`), currency moves (`[06.04]`), and
risk-on/risk-off sentiment shifts (`[06.06]`).

### Intuition
Geopolitical events matter to markets because they can suddenly and
significantly disrupt the assumptions underlying existing prices — a war
affecting a major oil-producing region changes global energy supply
expectations overnight (`[06.08]`); sanctions can abruptly cut off a
country's access to global capital markets or specific trade
relationships; a major trade deal or tariff announcement can immediately
change the expected cost structure for entire industries. Markets, being
forward-looking (`[03.10]`, `[04.08]`), tend to react sharply and quickly
to genuinely surprising geopolitical developments.

## Real World Analogy
A geopolitical market shock is like a sudden loud noise in a quiet room —
everyone's immediate reaction (jumping, alertness, elevated heart rate) is
sharp and near-universal, but most people quickly return to their previous
state once they realize the noise wasn't an actual ongoing threat. Only if
the noise turns out to signal a genuine, lasting change to the room's
conditions (an actual fire, not a false alarm) does the initial reaction
translate into a durably different, lasting behavior change — precisely
the distinction this lesson's framework draws between short-term shock
reactions and lasting structural repricing.

## Indian Market Example
Major geopolitical events affecting global oil supply (`[06.08]`) have
historically triggered notable, if often temporary, volatility in Indian
markets given India's heavy oil import dependence (`[06.05]`) — a sudden
supply disruption from a major oil-producing region typically causes an
immediate spike in oil prices, rupee pressure, and Indian equity market
volatility, with the market's subsequent path depending heavily on whether
the disruption proves temporary or leads to a lasting change in global oil
supply dynamics.

## US Market Example
The **US-China trade tensions and tariff disputes** beginning in 2018
illustrate a trade-deal-driven geopolitical market driver distinct from
armed conflict or sanctions — tariff announcements and negotiations
produced repeated episodes of sharp market volatility across both US and
global markets (including India, via the correlation mechanisms in
`[04.06]`) as market participants continuously repriced expectations about
global trade costs and supply chain structures, an ongoing, multi-year
geopolitical theme rather than a single discrete event.

## Historical Example
**Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022** triggered an immediate,
sharp spike in global energy and agricultural commodity prices (given
Russia and Ukraine's significant roles in global oil, natural gas, and
grain markets), a broad risk-off shift across global equity markets
(`[06.06]`), and significant, sustained Western sanctions on Russia —
illustrating multiple geopolitical market channels (commodity supply
shock, sanctions, broad risk sentiment) operating simultaneously from a
single major event, with lasting structural effects on global energy
markets that persisted well beyond the initial shock reaction.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional geopolitical risk analysts (an increasingly formalized
specialization within institutional research) attempt to distinguish
genuinely novel, market-moving geopolitical developments from recurring,
already largely-priced-in tensions (e.g., certain long-standing regional
tensions that markets have grown accustomed to and no longer react
sharply to) — a skill requiring both geopolitical subject knowledge and
market pricing awareness combined.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders often react emotionally and immediately to alarming
geopolitical headlines without waiting to assess whether the underlying
event represents a genuine, lasting structural shift or a shorter-term
shock likely to partially reverse — building the habit of asking "is this
event likely to cause lasting change to fundamentals, or primarily
short-term fear" (this lesson's core flowchart question) supports more
measured, less reactive decision-making.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders specifically avoid making large, leveraged directional
bets purely on unfolding geopolitical headlines, given the historically
high frequency of sharp initial reactions that partially or fully reverse
— instead favoring reduced position sizing and wider risk buffers
(Module 25-26) during periods of elevated geopolitical uncertainty, rather
than confidently predicting the specific direction and magnitude of the
market's ultimate response.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating every geopolitical headline as equally significant, without
  distinguishing potentially lasting structural shocks from shorter-term,
  likely-to-reverse fear reactions.
- Making large, leveraged directional trades based purely on initial
  geopolitical shock reactions, without allowing time for the market's
  more considered reassessment.
- Ignoring the specific transmission channel (commodity, trade flow, risk
  sentiment) through which a given geopolitical event is most likely to
  affect markets.

## Misconceptions
- **"All geopolitical shocks produce permanent, lasting market
  effects."** As this lesson's framework emphasizes, many geopolitical
  shocks produce sharp initial reactions that substantially or fully
  reverse once the market reassesses actual lasting economic
  significance — only events causing genuine structural change to
  fundamentals tend to produce durably different price levels.
- **"Geopolitical risk is impossible to analyze systematically and is
  purely random news-driven noise."** While individual event timing is
  inherently unpredictable, the transmission channels (commodity supply,
  trade flows, risk sentiment) and the general pattern of sharp-reaction-
  then-reassessment are systematically understandable, even if specific
  event prediction isn't possible.
- **"Geopolitical events only matter for currency and commodity traders,
  not equity investors."** As the 2022 Russia-Ukraine and 2018+ US-China
  trade tension examples illustrate, geopolitical events routinely affect
  equity markets broadly, not just currency and commodity markets
  specifically.

## Key Takeaways
- Geopolitical risk affects markets through commodity supply, trade flow,
  and risk sentiment channels.
- Initial market reactions to geopolitical shocks are often sharp but
  frequently partially or fully reverse unless lasting structural change
  to fundamentals has occurred.
- The 2022 Russia-Ukraine invasion and 2018+ US-China trade tensions
  illustrate different geopolitical market drivers (armed conflict/
  sanctions vs. trade disputes).
- Professional traders favor reduced sizing and wider risk buffers during
  geopolitical uncertainty rather than confident directional predictions.

## Quiz
1. What are the three main channels through which geopolitical events
   affect markets?
   a) Weather, sports, entertainment
   b) Commodity supply, trade flows, and risk sentiment
   c) Only currency markets
   d) Only bond markets

2. Do geopolitical shocks always produce permanent, lasting market
   effects?
   a) Yes, always  b) No — many sharp reactions partially or fully reverse unless lasting structural change occurs  c) Only in India  d) Only during recessions

3. What 2022 event illustrates multiple geopolitical market channels
   operating simultaneously?
   a) The Zomato IPO  b) Russia's invasion of Ukraine  c) The 1992 Harshad Mehta scam  d) India's T+1 settlement transition

4. How do professional traders typically respond during periods of
   elevated geopolitical uncertainty?
   a) Making large, confident leveraged directional bets
   b) Reducing position sizing and widening risk buffers
   c) Ignoring geopolitical events entirely
   d) Exiting all markets permanently

5. True or False: Geopolitical events only affect currency and commodity
   markets, never equities.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Commodity supply, trade flows, and risk sentiment are the three main channels.
2. **b)** — Many sharp reactions reverse unless lasting structural change to fundamentals occurs.
3. **b)** — Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine triggered commodity, sanctions, and risk-off effects simultaneously.
4. **b)** — Professionals favor reduced sizing and wider risk buffers rather than confident directional bets.
5. **b) False** — Geopolitical events routinely affect equity markets broadly, not just currency/commodity markets.
