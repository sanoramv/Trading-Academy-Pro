## Core Concepts

### Definition
An **exchange rate regime** is the system a country uses to determine its
currency's value relative to others. A **fixed (pegged) exchange rate**
ties a currency's value to another currency (or basket) at a set rate,
maintained through active central bank intervention. A **floating exchange
rate** lets market demand/supply (`[05.02]`) determine the currency's
value with minimal government intervention. A **managed float** (India's
regime) sits between these — the currency floats based on market forces
but the central bank actively intervenes periodically to smooth excessive
volatility. **Devaluation** specifically refers to a deliberate, official
reduction in a *fixed/pegged* currency's value by the government/central
bank; **depreciation** refers to a currency losing value through *market
forces* under a floating or managed float regime.

### Intuition
The choice of exchange rate regime involves a fundamental trade-off: fixed
rates offer stability and predictability (valuable for trade and
investment planning) but require the central bank to hold significant
foreign currency reserves and sacrifice independent monetary policy control
(since defending a peg often requires interest rate decisions driven by
currency stability needs rather than domestic economic conditions).
Floating rates preserve monetary policy independence but introduce currency
volatility that businesses and investors must manage.

## Real World Analogy
A fixed exchange rate is like a company setting a fixed exchange rate for
an internal company currency used across its global offices, maintained
by the head office actively buying/selling to keep it stable — reassuring
and predictable for internal planning, but requiring constant head-office
resources and reducing each office's ability to set its own independent
local policies. A floating exchange rate is like letting each office's
"currency" find its own value based on local supply and demand — more
locally responsive, but introducing volatility that global coordination
must then manage separately. India's managed float is like a head office
that mostly lets local offices set their own value but occasionally steps
in with guidance during unusually chaotic periods.

## Indian Market Example
India operates a **managed float** regime — the rupee's value against the
US dollar and other currencies is primarily determined by market forces
(trade flows, capital flows, per `[03.07]`), but the **RBI periodically
intervenes** in the FX market (buying or selling dollars from its reserves)
specifically to smooth excessive short-term volatility, without targeting
a fixed rate level — a deliberate middle-ground policy choice reflecting
India's specific economic development priorities and its desire to
maintain meaningful, though not unlimited, monetary policy independence.

## US Market Example
The **US dollar operates under a floating exchange rate regime** with
minimal direct government intervention in currency markets under normal
conditions — its value is determined predominantly by market forces,
reflecting relative interest rates (`[04.04]`), growth expectations, and
global risk sentiment, consistent with the US's position as the issuer of
the world's primary reserve currency and its preference for maximizing
domestic monetary policy independence.

## Historical Example
**China's exchange rate regime evolution** — from a tightly managed peg to
the US dollar for many years, toward a more (though still not fully)
market-driven managed float in subsequent reforms — illustrates how
countries can and do transition along this regime spectrum over time as
their economic priorities and development stage evolve, a useful
comparative reference point alongside India's own managed float experience
covered in this lesson.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional FX trading desks closely track a country's exchange rate
regime type as a fundamental input to their trading and risk models —
trading a pegged currency involves fundamentally different dynamics
(watching for signs of peg sustainability/stress, since pegs can and
occasionally do break under sufficient market pressure) than trading a
freely floating currency (where continuous market-driven price discovery,
similar to the equity order book mechanics from `[01.04]`, is the primary
dynamic).

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors and traders in India benefit from understanding that the
RBI's periodic FX interventions are a normal, expected feature of India's
managed float regime — not evidence of currency manipulation or a hidden
fixed target — helping avoid misinterpretation of RBI's routine currency
market operations covered in financial news.

## Professional Insights
Professional macro traders specifically study historical instances of
**currency peg breaks** (a fixed regime failing under sustained market
pressure that exceeds the central bank's reserves/willingness to defend
it) as important risk case studies, since these events have historically
produced some of the sharpest, most disruptive currency and broader market
moves in financial history — understanding the specific mechanics and
warning signs of peg stress is a specialized but valuable macro trading
skill.

## Common Mistakes
- Confusing devaluation (deliberate action under a fixed regime) with
  depreciation (market-driven decline under a floating/managed regime) —
  these terms are not interchangeable.
- Assuming India's rupee operates under a purely free float, missing the
  RBI's active, if selective, intervention role.
- Assuming a fixed exchange rate regime is always safer/better than a
  floating one, without recognizing the genuine trade-offs (reserve
  requirements, sacrificed monetary policy independence) each regime type
  carries.

## Misconceptions
- **"India's rupee has a fixed target value the RBI defends at all
  costs."** India operates a managed float, not a fixed peg — the RBI
  smooths volatility but does not target or defend a specific fixed rate
  level.
- **"Devaluation and depreciation mean the same thing and are used
  interchangeably by professionals."** Devaluation specifically refers to
  a deliberate policy action under a fixed/pegged regime; depreciation
  refers to market-driven currency decline under floating/managed
  regimes — professionals use these terms precisely, not interchangeably.
- **"A floating exchange rate means the government has zero influence over
  currency value."** Even under floating regimes, government/central bank
  actions (interest rate policy, verbal intervention, occasionally direct
  market operations) can meaningfully influence currency value, even
  without a formal fixed target.

## Key Takeaways
- Exchange rate regimes range from fixed/pegged to floating, with managed
  float (India's regime) as a middle ground.
- Devaluation (deliberate, under fixed regimes) and depreciation
  (market-driven, under floating/managed regimes) are distinct, non-
  interchangeable terms.
- Each regime type involves a trade-off between currency stability and
  monetary policy independence.
- The RBI's periodic FX interventions are a normal feature of India's
  managed float, not evidence of a hidden fixed target.

## Summary
Exchange rate regimes — ranging from fixed/pegged systems requiring active
defense, to fully floating systems driven by market forces, to India's
managed float middle ground — represent a fundamental policy trade-off
between currency stability and monetary policy independence, with
devaluation and depreciation as distinct, regime-specific terms describing
currency value declines. Understanding India's specific managed float
regime and the RBI's role within it sets up the next lesson's focus on the
balance of payments — the broader accounting framework that captures why
currencies move the way they do in the first place.

## Revision Notes
- Fixed/pegged: government-set rate, actively defended, sacrifices
  monetary policy independence. Floating: market-driven, preserves policy
  independence, more volatile. Managed float (India): hybrid.
- Devaluation = deliberate action under a fixed regime. Depreciation =
  market-driven decline under floating/managed regimes.
- RBI intervenes periodically to smooth volatility, not to defend a fixed
  target.
- China: historical example of regime evolution from tighter peg toward
  more managed float.

## Quiz
1. What exchange rate regime does India operate under?
   a) A pure fixed peg  b) A managed float  c) A pure free float with zero intervention  d) No regime at all

2. What is devaluation?
   a) Market-driven currency decline under a floating regime
   b) A deliberate policy action reducing a currency's value under a fixed regime
   c) A type of interest rate hike
   d) A stock market crash

3. What trade-off does a fixed exchange rate regime present?
   a) No trade-offs exist
   b) Currency stability at the cost of requiring reserves and sacrificing monetary policy independence
   c) Guaranteed economic growth
   d) Elimination of all inflation

4. Does the RBI target and defend a specific fixed rupee value?
   a) Yes, always  b) No — it smooths volatility under a managed float without a fixed target  c) Only on weekends  d) Only during elections

5. True or False: Devaluation and depreciation are interchangeable terms
   used identically by professionals.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — India operates a managed float exchange rate regime.
2. **b)** — Devaluation is a deliberate policy action under a fixed exchange rate regime.
3. **b)** — Fixed regimes offer stability but require reserves and sacrifice monetary policy independence.
4. **b)** — The RBI smooths volatility under a managed float, without defending a fixed target.
5. **b) False** — The terms are distinct: devaluation (fixed regime, deliberate) vs. depreciation (floating/managed, market-driven).
