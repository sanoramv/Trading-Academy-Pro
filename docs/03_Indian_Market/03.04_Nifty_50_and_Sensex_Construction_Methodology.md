## Core Concepts

### Definition
Both **Nifty 50** and **Sensex** use **free-float market capitalization
weighting** — meaning each constituent company's influence on the index is
proportional to its **free-float market cap** (share price × number of
publicly tradeable shares, excluding promoter/government holdings not
available for public trading), not simply its share price or total market
cap including locked-in shares. Nifty 50 selects the 50 largest, most
liquid NSE-listed companies meeting specific eligibility criteria; Sensex
selects 30 similarly large, liquid BSE-listed companies. Both are
periodically **rebalanced** (typically semi-annually) to reflect changing
company sizes and eligibility.

### Intuition
Free-float weighting exists because a company's total market
capitalization can be misleading if a huge portion of its shares are held
by promoters/government and never actually trade — those locked shares
don't reflect real, tradeable market activity. By weighting only the
free-float (publicly tradeable) portion, the index more accurately reflects
what's actually available for investors to buy and sell, and prevents a
company with a huge total market cap but very few tradeable shares from
dominating the index disproportionately relative to its actual trading
liquidity.

### Mental Model
```
Free-float market cap = Share Price x (Total Shares - Promoter/Locked Shares)

Index weight of Company X = Company X's free-float market cap
                              -----------------------------------
                              Sum of all constituents' free-float market caps
```
A company with a large free-float weight moves the index more than a
company with a small free-float weight, for the same percentage price
change.

### Visual Explanation
Larger free-float market cap companies (like Reliance Industries, HDFC
Bank, ICICI Bank, TCS, Infosys — historically among Nifty 50's largest
weights) have outsized influence on daily index moves compared to smaller
free-float weight constituents.

### ASCII Diagram
```
Hypothetical simplified example (not real weights):

  Company A: Free-float market cap = 10,00,000 crore --> Weight: ~10%
  Company B: Free-float market cap =  5,00,000 crore --> Weight: ~5%
  Company C: Free-float market cap =  2,00,000 crore --> Weight: ~2%
  ... (47 more constituents for Nifty 50, or 27 more for Sensex)
  ---------------------------------------------------------------
  Total free-float market cap of all 50 (or 30) constituents = 100%

  If Company A rises 2% and Company C rises 2%, Company A's move
  contributes roughly 5x more to the INDEX's overall percentage
  change than Company C's identical percentage move, because of A's
  larger free-float weight.
```

### Flowchart
```
[NSE/BSE index committee reviews eligibility criteria periodically]
        |
        v
[Screen candidate companies: minimum free-float market cap, minimum
 trading liquidity/turnover, listing history requirements]
        |
        v
[Select top 50 (Nifty) or top 30 (Sensex) eligible companies by
 free-float market cap]
        |
        v
[Assign index weights proportional to each company's free-float
 market cap]
        |
        v
[Rebalance periodically (semi-annually) — companies that no longer
 meet criteria are removed and replaced]
```

## Real World Analogy
Free-float weighting is like measuring a company's influence in a business
association not by how many total shares exist on paper, but by how many
shares are actually "in circulation" and tradeable among the general
membership — a founder holding 70% of a company's shares in a permanent,
non-trading family trust doesn't give that company outsized influence on an
index built to reflect real, tradeable market activity, so only the
publicly available 30% counts toward its index weight.

## Indian Market Example
Historically, companies like **Reliance Industries, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank,
Infosys, and TCS** have consistently ranked among the highest-weighted
constituents in Nifty 50, meaning their price moves have an outsized effect
on the index's daily performance compared to smaller-weighted constituents
— a fact many Indian traders specifically track, since a sharp move in just
one or two of these mega-weight stocks can meaningfully move the entire
Nifty 50 index even if most other constituents are flat.

## US Market Example
The **S&P 500**, while methodologically similar in using float-adjusted
market cap weighting, offers a useful comparison point: the index has
become increasingly concentrated in its largest constituents (notably
several major US technology companies) over recent years, illustrating the
same core dynamic — large free-float weight means outsized index influence
— playing out at a scale that has drawn considerable market commentary
about concentration risk within capitalization-weighted indices generally,
a concept equally relevant to Nifty 50 and Sensex.

## Historical Example
**Nifty 50's periodic rebalancing** has, over the decades, added
technology and newer-economy companies while removing companies that
declined in relative size or liquidity — for example, various companies
prominent in Nifty 50 during the 1990s and early 2000s are no longer
constituents today, replaced by companies that grew to meet the
eligibility criteria more recently. This ongoing evolution reflects how
Nifty 50 is a living, periodically-updated snapshot of India's largest,
most liquid companies — not a fixed, unchanging list.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutions running **index funds and ETFs** that track Nifty 50 or
Sensex must precisely replicate each rebalancing event, buying newly added
constituents and selling removed ones in the correct free-float-weighted
proportions — creating predictable, closely-watched trading flows around
announced rebalancing dates that some institutional and sophisticated
traders specifically position around (an "index rebalancing" trading
strategy, an advanced topic connecting to Module 24).

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors buying an index fund or ETF tracking Nifty 50 or Sensex
are, often without realizing the mechanical details, buying exposure
precisely weighted by free-float market cap — meaning their fund's
performance is disproportionately driven by the largest few constituents,
not equally spread across all 50 (or 30) companies, an important nuance for
understanding true diversification (developed further in Module 27,
Portfolio Management).

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts distinguish free-float weighting from
**equal-weighting** (where every constituent has identical influence
regardless of size) and **price-weighting** (like the US Dow Jones
Industrial Average, where higher-priced stocks have more influence
regardless of company size) — three fundamentally different index
construction philosophies producing meaningfully different performance
characteristics from the same underlying group of stocks, worth
understanding before assuming "an index" is a single, uniform concept.

## Common Mistakes
- Assuming all 50 Nifty constituents (or 30 Sensex constituents)
  contribute equally to the index's daily move.
- Not understanding that promoter/government-held shares are excluded from
  the weighting calculation, even though they're part of the company's
  total market cap.
- Assuming index composition is permanent, rather than periodically
  reviewed and rebalanced.

## Misconceptions
- **"A stock's total market cap determines its index weight."** It's
  specifically the *free-float* market cap (excluding locked-in
  promoter/government shares) that determines weight, not total market
  cap.
- **"Nifty 50 and Sensex will always contain the same 50/30 companies."**
  Constituents change over time through periodic rebalancing as company
  sizes, liquidity, and eligibility evolve.
- **"All index construction methodologies (free-float, equal-weight,
  price-weight) produce similar results from the same stocks."** These
  methodologies can produce meaningfully different performance profiles
  from an identical underlying group of companies, since they distribute
  influence very differently.

## Key Takeaways
- Nifty 50 and Sensex both use free-float market capitalization weighting.
- Free-float excludes locked-in promoter/government shares from the
  weighting calculation.
- Index weight determines how much a constituent's price move affects the
  overall index.
- Both indices are periodically rebalanced, with constituents changing over
  time.

## Summary
Nifty 50 and Sensex are both constructed using free-float market
capitalization weighting, meaning each company's influence on the index is
proportional to its publicly tradeable share value, not its total (often
larger) market cap including locked-in promoter or government holdings.
Understanding this methodology explains why a handful of mega-cap
constituents can move the entire index while smaller constituents barely
register, and sets up the next lesson's practical focus on Indian market
timings, circuits, and trading sessions.

## Revision Notes
- Free-float market cap = share price × publicly tradeable shares
  (excludes locked-in promoter/government holdings).
- Index weight ∝ free-float market cap — larger free-float weight = larger
  influence on index moves.
- Both Nifty 50 and Sensex use free-float weighting, rebalanced periodically
  (semi-annually).
- Distinct from equal-weighting and price-weighting (e.g., Dow Jones)
  methodologies used elsewhere.

## Quiz
1. What weighting methodology do both Nifty 50 and Sensex use?
   a) Equal-weighting  b) Price-weighting  c) Free-float market capitalization weighting  d) Volume-weighting

2. What is excluded from a company's free-float market cap calculation?
   a) All shares  b) Locked-in promoter/government shares  c) Publicly traded shares  d) Nothing is excluded

3. How often are Nifty 50 and Sensex typically rebalanced?
   a) Daily  b) Semi-annually (periodically)  c) Once every 10 years  d) Never

4. What index construction method does the Dow Jones Industrial Average use,
   in contrast to Nifty 50/Sensex?
   a) Free-float weighting  b) Price-weighting  c) Equal-weighting  d) Volume-weighting

5. True or False: All 50 constituents of Nifty 50 have exactly equal
   influence on the index's daily percentage move.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **c)** — Both indices use free-float market capitalization weighting.
2. **b)** — Locked-in promoter/government shares are excluded from the free-float calculation.
3. **b)** — Both indices undergo periodic (typically semi-annual) rebalancing.
4. **b)** — The Dow Jones uses price-weighting, a distinct methodology from free-float weighting.
5. **b) False** — Influence is proportional to free-float market cap weight, not equal across constituents.
