## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Insider trading** is trading a security while in possession of
**Unpublished Price-Sensitive Information (UPSI)** — material information
not yet made public that could reasonably affect the security's price
(e.g., an unannounced merger, an unreleased earnings result). It is
prohibited under **SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations,
2015**. **Front-running** is a broker or intermediary trading ahead of a
known, imminent large client order to profit from the anticipated price
impact. **Circular trading** is a manipulative scheme where a group of
connected parties trade among themselves to create artificial volume/price
movement, misleading other market participants. **Market surveillance**
refers to the systems exchanges and SEBI use to detect these and other
prohibited practices, including automated pattern-detection algorithms
monitoring unusual trading activity.

### Intuition
All three prohibited practices — insider trading, front-running, and
circular trading — share a common thread: they exploit an information or
positional advantage that ordinary market participants don't have access
to, undermining the fairness and trust that `[01.01]` and `[03.02]`
identified as essential to markets functioning at all. Regulations exist
specifically to preserve a reasonably level playing field, not to
eliminate all advantages (skilled analysis and faster legitimate
information access remain legal and are a normal part of competitive
markets).

### Mental Model
```
LEGAL EDGE:          Better public information analysis, faster
                      LEGITIMATE research, better risk management,
                      genuinely superior skill/process.

ILLEGAL EDGE:         Trading on UPSI (insider trading), trading
                      ahead of known client orders (front-running),
                      creating artificial price/volume signals
                      (circular trading/manipulation).
```
The dividing line is whether the advantage comes from **skill and
legitimate access to public/analyzable information** versus **privileged,
non-public information or manipulative, deceptive conduct**.

### Visual Explanation
Surveillance systems continuously scan for statistically unusual patterns
— sudden volume spikes ahead of announcements, coordinated trading among
connected accounts, and price/volume anomalies inconsistent with public
information flow.

## Real World Analogy
Insider trading is like knowing the exact winning lottery numbers before
they're publicly drawn and buying a ticket with that knowledge — it's not
that lottery participation itself is wrong, it's that using privileged,
non-public knowledge to guarantee an advantage over everyone else
participating fairly destroys the legitimacy of the entire game.
Front-running is like a waiter secretly rushing to buy the last portion of
a popular dish for themselves the moment they overhear a large table about
to order it, then reselling it to that table at a markup — exploiting
advance knowledge of someone else's imminent action for personal gain.

## Indian Market Example
SEBI has brought numerous **insider trading enforcement actions** over the
years against corporate insiders, connected persons, and sometimes
external parties who traded ahead of significant corporate announcements
(mergers, earnings surprises, regulatory approvals) using UPSI — cases
that are publicly documented in SEBI's enforcement orders and serve as an
ongoing, visible demonstration of the surveillance and enforcement
mechanisms described in this lesson actually operating in practice.

## US Market Example
The US SEC similarly enforces insider trading laws vigorously, with
historically prominent cases (such as those involving hedge fund managers
convicted of trading on non-public corporate information obtained through
improper channels) receiving significant media attention — illustrating
that insider trading enforcement, while implemented through different
specific legal mechanisms, is a shared regulatory priority across major
global markets, not a uniquely Indian concern.

## Historical Example
The **1992 Harshad Mehta scam** and **2001 Ketan Parekh scam**
(`[03.01]`, `[03.02]`) both involved elements of market manipulation
resembling circular trading and coordinated price manipulation schemes,
directly motivating the stronger surveillance infrastructure and
regulatory powers SEBI has progressively built since — a clear historical
throughline connecting these lesson's specific regulatory concepts to the
crisis-driven reform pattern established across this entire module.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutions maintain extensive internal **compliance and information
barrier ("Chinese wall")** systems specifically to prevent employees with
access to material non-public information (e.g., investment bankers
working on a pending merger) from improperly sharing it with the firm's
trading desks — a direct, practical institutional response to insider
trading regulations, and a significant area of financial industry
compliance infrastructure and cost.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders are far more likely to encounter insider trading risk
indirectly — for example, receiving a "hot tip" from someone claiming
insider knowledge about an upcoming corporate announcement. Acting on such
a tip, even as a retail trader with no direct corporate connection, can
still constitute a insider trading violation if the retail trader knew or
should have known the information was UPSI obtained improperly — a
genuinely important legal risk many retail participants underestimate.

## Professional Insights
Professional compliance officers emphasize that surveillance systems have
become increasingly sophisticated, using statistical and machine-learning-
based anomaly detection across enormous volumes of trade data — meaning
that manipulative schemes which might have gone undetected decades ago are
significantly more likely to be flagged and investigated in today's
market environment, a meaningful deterrent effect beyond just the
after-the-fact enforcement actions that make headlines.

## Common Mistakes
- Acting on a "hot tip" or "insider information" from a friend/contact,
  without realizing this can constitute a genuine legal violation, not
  just an ethical gray area.
- Assuming surveillance only catches large, obvious schemes — modern
  systems can and do detect smaller-scale, less obvious coordinated
  activity too.
- Confusing legitimate research edges (better analysis, faster legal
  information access) with illegal edges (UPSI, front-running) — the
  distinction matters legally, not just ethically.

## Misconceptions
- **"Insider trading only applies to company executives."** It applies to
  anyone trading on UPSI, including "connected persons" and even, in some
  circumstances, more distant recipients of improperly shared information
  who knew or should have known its status.
- **"If everyone is doing it (a stock 'tip' is widely shared), it must be
  legal."** Wide circulation of a tip doesn't make trading on genuine UPSI
  legal — the legal standard concerns whether the information is
  material, non-public, and improperly obtained/shared, not how many
  people ultimately received it.
- **"Regulatory surveillance mainly exists to punish, not prevent."**
  Surveillance systems serve a significant deterrent and real-time
  detection function, not solely after-the-fact punishment — many
  potential violations are interrupted or discouraged by the known
  presence of active monitoring.

## Key Takeaways
- Insider trading (UPSI-based), front-running, and circular trading are
  distinct but related prohibited practices undermining market fairness.
- The legal dividing line is skill/legitimate public information versus
  privileged non-public information or manipulative conduct.
- Modern surveillance uses sophisticated, automated anomaly detection
  across enormous trade data volumes.
- Retail traders can face genuine legal risk from acting on "hot tips"
  involving UPSI, not just an abstract ethical concern.

## Summary
India's market regulatory framework prohibits insider trading, front-
running, and circular trading — practices that exploit privileged
information or positional advantages to undermine the fair, transparent
functioning of markets that this entire academy has emphasized since
`[01.01]`. SEBI and the exchanges maintain increasingly sophisticated
surveillance systems to detect these practices, a direct legacy of the
crisis-driven reforms traced throughout this module. The final lesson of
this module turns to how two major recurring events — the Union Budget and
RBI policy announcements — concretely move Indian markets.

## Answers
1. **b)** — Insider trading involves trading while possessing UPSI.
2. **b)** — Front-running exploits advance knowledge of a client's imminent large order.
3. **b)** — The legal line is legitimate skill/public information vs. privileged/manipulative conduct.
4. **b)** — Retail traders can face genuine legal risk from knowingly acting on improperly obtained UPSI.
5. **b) False** — Modern surveillance relies heavily on automated, statistical anomaly detection.
