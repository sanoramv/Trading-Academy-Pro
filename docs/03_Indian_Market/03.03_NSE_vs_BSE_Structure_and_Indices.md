## Core Concepts

### Definition
**NSE (National Stock Exchange)**, founded 1992, is India's largest stock
exchange by trading volume and the exchange where the majority of Indian
equity and derivatives trading activity occurs. Its flagship index is the
**Nifty 50**. **BSE (Bombay Stock Exchange)**, founded 1875, is Asia's
oldest stock exchange and India's other major exchange. Its flagship index
is the **Sensex (BSE SENSEX, "Sensitive Index")**. Both are SEBI-regulated,
and most major Indian companies are **dual-listed** — tradeable on both
exchanges simultaneously.

### Intuition
Having two major exchanges might seem redundant, but it reflects both
history (BSE's century-plus head start versus NSE's technology-driven
disruption) and healthy competitive dynamics that have historically pushed
both exchanges toward better technology, tighter spreads, and lower costs
for participants — similar in spirit to how NYSE and NASDAQ coexist in the
US market.

### Mental Model
```
NSE: younger (1992), tech-first origin, LARGER trading volume,
     flagship index = Nifty 50 (50 large-cap stocks)

BSE: older (1875), legacy origin, SMALLER trading volume today,
     flagship index = Sensex (30 large-cap stocks)

BOTH: SEBI-regulated, most large stocks dual-listed, prices stay
      very close to each other due to arbitrage
```

### Visual Explanation
Both exchanges' major indices track a curated basket of large,
liquid Indian companies, but differ in exact composition size and specific
selection methodology.

## Real World Analogy
NSE and BSE coexisting is like two major highways connecting the same two
cities — one built more recently with modern engineering (NSE) attracting
most of today's traffic due to speed and convenience, and one older,
historic route (BSE) that's still fully functional, well-maintained, and
occasionally preferred for specific reasons, with both ultimately leading
to the same destination (owning the same underlying company's shares).

## Indian Market Example
**Reliance Industries** is dual-listed on both NSE (ticker: RELIANCE) and
BSE (scrip code: 500325). At any given moment during trading hours, its
price on both exchanges typically differs by only a few paise, if at all,
because of continuous arbitrage activity between the two venues — a
practical, everyday illustration of how dual-listing and price convergence
actually work.

## US Market Example
The **NYSE and NASDAQ** relationship offers a rough parallel, though
structured differently — many US companies list exclusively on one or the
other (e.g., most tech companies list on NASDAQ, many traditional/
industrial companies list on NYSE) rather than the more universal
dual-listing pattern seen with large Indian companies on NSE/BSE, an
important structural difference for students comparing the two markets.

## Historical Example
When NSE launched in 1992 with fully electronic trading, it captured
trading volume from BSE relatively quickly due to its transparency and
speed advantages, pressuring BSE to modernize its own systems — a
historical example of competitive pressure between exchanges directly
driving infrastructure improvement, benefiting all market participants
regardless of which exchange they used, a pattern discussed briefly in
`[01.04]` and `[03.01]`.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutions monitor both NSE and BSE order books when executing large
trades, sometimes splitting orders across both exchanges to access
combined liquidity and minimize market impact (`[01.08]`) — a practice
that requires sophisticated smart order routing technology, an area
covered further in Module 29 (Algorithmic Trading).

## Retail Perspective
Most Indian retail traders default to NSE simply because their broker's app
defaults there and because NSE typically has deeper liquidity for most
stocks — but it's worth knowing BSE remains a fully valid, equally
regulated venue, and for certain smaller or specific stocks, BSE liquidity
can occasionally be comparable or even preferable.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts and index providers pay close attention to the
specific **methodology differences** between Nifty 50 and Sensex (covered
in full detail in `[03.04]`) — for example, differing numbers of
constituent stocks (50 vs. 30) and slightly different rebalancing rules —
which can cause the two indices' percentage returns to diverge slightly
over any given period, even though they track a broadly similar set of
large-cap Indian companies.

## Common Mistakes
- Assuming NSE and BSE trade completely different sets of companies, rather
  than understanding most large caps are dual-listed.
- Not realizing Sensex tracks 30 stocks while Nifty 50 tracks 50, leading to
  confusion when the two indices show slightly different percentage moves
  on the same day.
- Ignoring BSE entirely as "irrelevant" simply because NSE has higher
  overall volume — BSE remains a legitimate, SEBI-regulated exchange.

## Misconceptions
- **"Sensex and Nifty always move by the exact same percentage."** They
  are correlated but not identical — differing constituent stocks and
  weighting methodology mean their exact percentage moves can differ
  slightly on any given day, even while directionally aligned almost all
  the time.
- **"BSE is outdated/irrelevant compared to NSE."** BSE has modernized
  significantly since 1992 and remains Asia's oldest and a fully
  legitimate, SEBI-regulated exchange with its own valuable role,
  particularly for specific listings and smaller companies.
- **"A stock's price should be identical on both exchanges at every
  instant."** Minor, typically very small and short-lived price
  differences do occur due to slightly different order books, quickly
  closed by arbitrage activity.

## Key Takeaways
- NSE (1992, tech-first, higher volume) and BSE (1875, Asia's oldest) are
  India's two major SEBI-regulated exchanges.
- Nifty 50 (NSE, 50 stocks) and Sensex (BSE, 30 stocks) are the flagship
  indices, correlated but not identical in construction.
- Most large Indian companies are dual-listed on both exchanges.
- Arbitrage keeps prices on both exchanges very close together.

## Summary
NSE and BSE are India's two major stock exchanges — NSE younger and larger
by volume, BSE older and historically significant as Asia's oldest exchange
— both SEBI-regulated, with most large companies dual-listed across both.
Their flagship indices, Nifty 50 and Sensex, are correlated but
methodologically distinct, a distinction explored in full detail in the
next lesson.

## Quiz
1. What is NSE's flagship index?
   a) Sensex  b) Nifty 50  c) BSE 100  d) Nifty Bank

2. How many stocks does the Sensex track?
   a) 50  b) 100  c) 30  d) 500

3. Why do NSE and BSE prices for the same stock stay close together?
   a) They are legally required to be identical
   b) Continuous arbitrage activity closes any meaningful price gap
   c) They use a shared single order book
   d) Prices are set by the government

4. What is a key structural difference between how Indian large-caps and US
   large-caps typically list on exchanges?
   a) No difference exists
   b) Most large Indian companies are dual-listed on both NSE and BSE, while many US companies list exclusively on one exchange
   c) US companies are never listed on more than one exchange, ever
   d) Indian companies cannot list on more than one exchange

5. True or False: BSE is no longer SEBI-regulated since NSE became larger.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Nifty 50 is NSE's flagship index.
2. **c)** — Sensex tracks 30 large-cap stocks.
3. **b)** — Arbitrage activity keeps prices on both exchanges closely aligned.
4. **b)** — Large Indian companies are typically dual-listed, unlike the more common single-exchange listing pattern for many US companies.
5. **b) False** — BSE remains fully SEBI-regulated regardless of relative trading volume.
