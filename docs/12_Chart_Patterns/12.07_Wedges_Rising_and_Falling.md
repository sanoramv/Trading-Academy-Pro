# [12.07] Wedges — Rising and Falling

> Module: Chart Patterns · Lesson 7 of 10 · Estimated time: 30 minutes

## Learning Objectives
- Identify Rising and Falling Wedges precisely.
- Understand why wedges are counter-intuitive: same-direction slope, opposite implication.
- Distinguish wedges from triangles and channels.
- Learn why wedges can function as either reversal or continuation patterns depending on context.

## Prerequisites
- `[12.04]` Ascending, Descending, and Symmetrical Triangles
- `[10.05]` Trendlines and Channels

## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **Rising Wedge** is formed by two converging trendlines, both sloping
**upward**, with the lower trendline rising more steeply than the upper —
despite its upward slope, a Rising Wedge is generally considered a
**bearish** pattern. A **Falling Wedge** is the mirror image: two
converging trendlines both sloping **downward**, generally considered a
**bullish** pattern despite its downward slope. This counter-intuitive
relationship (upward-sloping pattern = bearish; downward-sloping pattern
= bullish) is this pattern's defining, most important characteristic to
internalize.

### Intuition
A Rising Wedge's upward slope reflects **diminishing momentum** even as
price technically continues higher — each successive high is higher, but
by a progressively smaller margin (a narrowing, converging pattern),
suggesting buying conviction is weakening even while price nominally
still rises, similar in spirit to `[10.10]`'s bearish regular divergence
concept (price making higher highs while underlying strength weakens).
The Falling Wedge shows the mirror-image dynamic on the downside.

### Mental Model

```
\
 \  /\

       \/  \  /\
            \/  \  /
                 \/
  (BOTH trendlines slope DOWN, but the pattern NARROWS --
   each lower low is a SMALLER decline than the last --
   weakening downward momentum despite the downward direction)
```

### Visual Explanation
The key distinguishing feature from a simple channel (`[10.05]`) or
symmetrical triangle (`[12.04]`) is that BOTH wedge trendlines slope in
the SAME direction (both up, or both down) while still converging — a
channel has parallel, non-converging lines; a symmetrical triangle has
trendlines sloping in OPPOSITE directions.

## Real World Analogy
A Rising Wedge is like a runner who's still technically gaining ground
with each stride but whose stride length is visibly, progressively
shrinking — an observer watching only the runner's overall forward
position might see continued "progress," but the shrinking stride length
itself signals fading energy and an increasingly likely imminent
slowdown or stop, despite the still-forward direction of travel.

## Indian Market Example
Rising Wedge patterns have historically formed near several notable
Nifty 50 and individual Indian stock tops, where price continued making
marginally higher highs with progressively narrowing momentum before an
eventual reversal — Indian technicians specifically watch for this
narrowing, diminishing-momentum signature as a warning sign distinct from
a healthy, expanding uptrend.

## US Market Example
Falling Wedges have formed at several notable US stock and index
bottoms, where price continued making marginally lower lows with
progressively narrowing downward momentum before an eventual bullish
reversal — US technicians specifically distinguish this pattern from a
simple, healthy downtrend continuation by checking for the characteristic
narrowing, converging trendline structure.

## Historical Example
Wedge patterns, alongside Triangles and other patterns covered in this
module, appear throughout the same foundational 20th century Western
technical analysis literature, with their specifically counter-intuitive
slope-versus-implication relationship (documented consistently across
decades of subsequent technical analysis writing) making them a
frequently-cited example of why understanding a pattern's underlying
psychological logic matters more than superficial visual pattern-matching
based on slope direction alone.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional technicians specifically distinguish wedges functioning as
**reversal patterns** (forming at the end of an established trend,
signaling a change in direction) from wedges functioning as
**continuation patterns** (forming as a brief counter-trend pullback
within a larger, still-intact opposite-direction trend) — both are
documented, legitimate interpretations depending entirely on the broader
structural context (`[02.01]`-`[02.02]`), directly connecting to
`[11.08]`'s contextual checklist.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders very commonly confuse a Rising Wedge's bullish-appearing
upward slope with an actual bullish implication — internalizing the
counter-intuitive slope-versus-implication relationship this lesson
emphasizes is one of the most important, frequently-tested distinctions
in practical chart pattern application.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders specifically note that wedges, like Triangles
(`[12.04]`), typically show contracting volume through their formation —
a Rising Wedge with genuinely EXPANDING volume through its formation
(rather than the expected contraction) is considered a less reliable, less
"textbook" example of the pattern, worth interpreting with additional
caution.

## Common Mistakes
- Assuming a Rising Wedge's upward slope implies a bullish outcome,
  missing the pattern's defining counter-intuitive bearish implication.
- Confusing a Wedge (same-direction converging trendlines) with a
  Symmetrical Triangle (opposite-direction converging trendlines).
- Not checking whether a wedge is functioning as a reversal or
  continuation pattern based on its broader structural context.

## Misconceptions
- **"A Rising Wedge, given its upward slope, must be a bullish
  pattern."** This is precisely the counter-intuitive trap this lesson
  addresses directly — Rising Wedges are generally considered bearish
  despite their upward slope, reflecting diminishing underlying momentum.
- **"Wedges and Symmetrical Triangles are the same pattern."** Wedges have
  both trendlines sloping in the SAME direction; Symmetrical Triangles
  have trendlines sloping in OPPOSITE directions — a meaningful structural
  distinction.
- **"A wedge can only function as a reversal pattern, never a
  continuation pattern."** Both interpretations are documented and
  legitimate, depending entirely on the broader structural context in
  which the wedge forms.

## Key Takeaways
- Rising Wedge (bearish) and Falling Wedge (bullish) are defined by two
  same-direction, converging trendlines — a counter-intuitive slope-
  versus-implication relationship that must be internalized carefully.
- Wedges differ from Symmetrical Triangles (opposite-direction slopes)
  and Channels (parallel, non-converging lines).
- Wedges can function as either reversal or continuation patterns
  depending on the broader structural context.
- Volume typically contracts through a wedge's formation, similar to
  Triangles.

## Summary
Rising and Falling Wedges — defined by two same-direction, converging
trendlines with a deliberately counter-intuitive slope-versus-implication
relationship (upward slope = bearish; downward slope = bullish) —
represent one of this module's most conceptually important patterns
precisely because of how easily their slope direction can be misread as
implying the opposite outcome. This sets up the next lesson's coverage of
Rounding Top and Bottom — patterns sharing some visual similarity with
`[12.06]`'s Cup and Handle but without the handle component and applicable
to both bullish and bearish reversal contexts.

## Quiz
1. Is a Rising Wedge generally bullish or bearish?
   a) Bullish, matching its upward slope
   b) Bearish, despite its upward slope
   c) Neutral, with no implication
   d) It depends only on volume, never slope

2. What distinguishes a Wedge from a Symmetrical Triangle?
   a) Nothing, they are identical
   b) Wedge trendlines slope in the SAME direction; Symmetrical Triangle trendlines slope in OPPOSITE directions
   c) Wedges only occur in currency markets
   d) Symmetrical Triangles require three trendlines

3. What does a Rising Wedge's narrowing structure reflect?
   a) Expanding, strengthening momentum
   b) Diminishing momentum despite the upward price direction
   c) Nothing significant
   d) A guaranteed continued rally

4. Can wedges function as continuation patterns as well as reversal
   patterns?
   a) No, only reversal
   b) Yes — both interpretations are documented, depending on context
   c) No, only continuation
   d) Wedges never resolve in any direction

5. True or False: A Falling Wedge is generally considered a bearish
   pattern, matching its downward slope.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — A Rising Wedge is generally bearish despite its upward slope.
2. **b)** — Wedges have same-direction slopes; Symmetrical Triangles have opposite-direction slopes.
3. **b)** — The narrowing structure reflects diminishing momentum despite the price direction.
4. **b)** — Wedges can function as either reversal or continuation patterns depending on context.
5. **b) False** — A Falling Wedge is generally bullish, despite its downward slope — the counter-intuitive relationship applies to both wedge types.
