# [12.03] Triple Top and Triple Bottom

> Module: Chart Patterns · Lesson 3 of 10 · Estimated time: 30 minutes

## Learning Objectives
- Identify Triple Top and Triple Bottom patterns precisely.
- Understand why three tests generally signal stronger resistance/support than two.
- Compare Triple Top to Head and Shoulders (three peaks, different configuration).
- Recognize the trade-off between pattern rarity and reliability.

## Prerequisites
- `[12.02]` Double Top and Double Bottom
- `[02.04]` Support and Resistance — First Principles

## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **Triple Top** is `[12.02]`'s Double Top with one more confirming
test — three roughly comparable peaks at the same resistance level
instead of two, confirmed the identical way (a break below the pattern's
intervening swing lows). A **Triple Bottom** is the mirror-image bullish
version. The mechanics you already know from `[12.02]` — same resistance
level, same confirmation trigger — do not change here. **What genuinely
changes, and is the actual point of this lesson, is a trade-off: does one
additional test make the signal more trustworthy, or just rarer?** The
rest of this lesson answers that question; the Mental Model below then
covers the one other thing that's genuinely new — not confusing a Triple
Top with the visually similar but structurally distinct Head and
Shoulders (`[12.01]`).

### Intuition
More confirming tests sounds like it should mean a more reliable signal —
each successful test without a breakout is one more data point suggesting
the level reflects genuine, persistent seller conviction (`[02.04]`).
But that intuition has a hidden cost: a pattern that requires *three*
qualifying tests instead of two is mechanically rarer in real market
data — most resistance levels get tested twice and either break or fail
to reform a third clean test at all. So a confirmed Triple Top isn't
obviously "better evidence" than a confirmed Double Top; it's evidence
from a smaller, less-studied sample. This rarity-vs-reliability trade-off
— not the pattern's shape — is what a trader actually needs to weigh
before treating a Triple Top as a stronger signal.

### Mental Model
```
TRIPLE TOP vs. HEAD AND SHOULDERS: KEY DISTINCTION

TRIPLE TOP (three ROUGHLY EQUAL peaks):
   /\      /\      /\
  /  \    /  \    /  \
 /    \  /    \  /    \
        \/      \/
     (comparable heights, unlike H&S)

HEAD AND SHOULDERS (middle peak DISTINCTLY higher):
        /\
       /  \
  /\  /    \  /\
 /  \/      \/  \
   (middle peak clearly EXCEEDS the outer two --
    THIS is what distinguishes H&S from a Triple Top)
```

### Visual Explanation
Both patterns require confirmation via a break of the intervening swing
lows/highs (analogous to `[12.01]`'s neckline and `[12.02]`'s intervening
point) — the specific peak configuration (equal vs. one-distinctly-
higher) is what distinguishes the pattern name, not the confirmation
mechanism itself, which is structurally identical across both.

## Real World Analogy
A Triple Top is like a negotiating party making three separate attempts
to push past the same firm boundary, each time being turned back at
essentially the same point — three consistent rejections at the identical
boundary provide even stronger evidence of that boundary's genuine
firmness than two rejections would, though the fact that three attempts
even occurred (rather than the negotiation resolving after just one or
two) is itself a less common, more specific scenario.

## Indian Market Example
Triple Top patterns occasionally form on individual Indian stocks
during extended consolidation phases at significant historical
resistance levels — Indian technicians treat a confirmed Triple Top,
given its rarity relative to Double Tops, as a notably significant signal
when it does occur and confirm, though they equally note such patterns
appear considerably less frequently than the simpler Double Top pattern
across typical Indian market history.

## US Market Example
Triple Bottom patterns have occasionally been identified at significant
US index and individual stock lows historically — US technicians
specifically distinguish a genuine Triple Bottom (three roughly equal
troughs) from an Inverse Head and Shoulders (`[12.01]`, where the middle
trough would be distinctly lower/deeper than the outer two), since the
two patterns, despite superficial visual similarity, carry the same
general bullish implication through structurally distinct configurations.

## Historical Example
Triple Top and Triple Bottom patterns appear in the same early-to-mid
20th century Western technical analysis literature (Schabacker,
`[12.02]`) that documented Double Top/Bottom and Head and Shoulders,
reflecting a systematic historical effort to catalog and name the full
range of resistance/support-testing structural configurations observed
across decades of chart analysis, rather than these patterns being
independently "discovered" separately over time.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional technicians generally place somewhat more analytical weight
on a confirmed Triple Top/Bottom than a Double Top/Bottom, given the
additional confirming test — while simultaneously acknowledging, per
`[11.10]`'s backtesting caution extended to chart patterns, that fewer
historical instances of any specific rarer pattern exist to statistically
validate this intuitive "more tests = more reliable" assumption with
the same rigor as more common patterns.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders should specifically practice distinguishing a genuine
Triple Top (roughly equal peaks) from a Head and Shoulders (middle peak
distinctly higher) — since both patterns are structurally similar
three-peak formations with the same general bearish implication once
confirmed, but represent formally distinct named patterns based on this
specific configuration difference.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders note that as the number of required tests increases
(from Double to Triple, and rare further extensions), the pattern becomes
progressively rarer in real market data — meaning that while each
individual confirmed instance may carry somewhat stronger intuitive
significance, traders will encounter genuine, clearly-qualifying examples
considerably less often than simpler, more common patterns like Double
Tops or basic support/resistance retests (`[02.04]`).

## Common Mistakes
- Confusing a Triple Top (roughly equal peaks) with a Head and Shoulders
  (middle peak distinctly higher) due to superficial visual similarity.
- Overweighting a Triple Top's significance purely due to its rarity,
  without equally weighing the correspondingly thinner empirical evidence
  base for any specific rare pattern.
- Not requiring confirmation (intervening point break) before treating a
  Triple Top as a validated signal.

## Misconceptions
- **"Triple Top and Head and Shoulders are the same pattern with a
  different name."** They are structurally distinct — Triple Top has three
  roughly equal peaks, while Head and Shoulders has one middle peak
  distinctly exceeding the outer two.
- **"A Triple Top is always a stronger, more reliable signal than a
  Double Top simply because it involves more tests."** While intuitively
  appealing, this assumption should be weighed against the pattern's
  rarity and correspondingly thinner empirical validation base, per
  `[11.10]`'s backtesting caution.
- **"Triple Top patterns occur as commonly as Double Top patterns."**
  Requiring an additional confirming test makes Triple Tops inherently
  rarer in real market data than the simpler, more common Double Top
  pattern.

## Key Takeaways
- Triple Top (bearish) and Triple Bottom (bullish) show three roughly
  equal tests of a resistance/support level, confirmed by a break of the
  intervening swing points.
- The key distinction from Head and Shoulders is peak/trough configuration:
  roughly equal (Triple Top) versus one distinctly more extreme (Head and
  Shoulders).
- Triple Top/Bottom patterns are inherently rarer than Double Top/Bottom,
  given the additional required confirming test.
- Rarity should be weighed against a correspondingly thinner empirical
  validation base, not assumed to automatically mean higher reliability.

## Summary
Triple Top and Triple Bottom — three roughly equal tests of a resistance
or support level, confirmed by breaking the pattern's intervening swing
points — extend `[12.02]`'s Double Top logic one test further, while
requiring careful distinction from the structurally similar but
configurationally distinct Head and Shoulders pattern (`[12.01]`). This
completes the module's coverage of reversal patterns built on repeated
level-testing, setting up the next lesson's shift to continuation
patterns — triangles — which describe converging, rather than
level-testing, price behavior.

## Quiz
1. What is a Triple Top?
   a) A single candlestick pattern
   b) A bearish reversal pattern with three roughly equal peaks at resistance
   c) A type of moving average
   d) A currency devaluation

2. What distinguishes a Triple Top from a Head and Shoulders pattern?
   a) They are identical  b) Triple Top has three roughly equal peaks; Head and Shoulders has one distinctly higher middle peak  c) Triple Top only occurs in bear markets  d) Head and Shoulders requires four peaks

3. Is a Triple Top pattern more common or rarer than a Double Top?
   a) More common  b) Rarer, given the additional confirming test required  c) Exactly equally common  d) Triple Tops don't exist

4. Does a Triple Top's rarity automatically make it a more reliable
   signal than a Double Top?
   a) Yes, always  b) Not automatically — rarity should be weighed against thinner empirical validation  c) Rarity is irrelevant to reliability  d) Rarer patterns are always less reliable

5. True or False: A Triple Top and a Head and Shoulders pattern are the
   exact same formation with just a different name.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — A Triple Top has three roughly equal peaks at resistance, confirmed by a break of intervening lows.
2. **b)** — Triple Top has equal peaks; Head and Shoulders has one distinctly higher middle peak.
3. **b)** — Triple Tops are rarer, requiring an additional confirming test compared to Double Tops.
4. **b)** — Rarity should be weighed against a correspondingly thinner empirical validation base, not assumed to automatically mean higher reliability.
5. **b) False** — They are structurally distinct patterns based on peak configuration.
