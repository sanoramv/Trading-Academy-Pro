## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **Fair Value Gap (FVG)**, also called an **imbalance**, is a specific
three-candle price pattern where the first candle's range and the third
candle's range do not overlap — leaving a visible "gap" bounded by the
first candle's high (or low) and the third candle's low (or high), with
the middle candle representing the sharp, decisive move that created the
imbalance. SMC theory holds that this gap represents a zone where price
moved too quickly for genuine two-way trading (buyers and sellers
transacting at every price level) to occur, making it statistically more
likely that price will later return to "fill" or partially fill this
gap, similarly to a magnet.

### Intuition
An FVG represents a specific, three-candle-precise version of the more
general "inefficient move" concept — normal, healthy price movement
typically involves overlapping candle ranges representing genuine
two-way auction activity (`[15.01]`'s Auction Market Theory); an FVG's
non-overlapping structure signals an unusually one-sided, rapid move that
left a genuine pricing "gap" behind, distinct from classical overnight
gaps (`[12.09]`) which occur between sessions rather than within a
continuous intraday sequence.

### Visual Explanation
SMC traders mark the specific price range between Candle 1's relevant
extreme and Candle 3's relevant extreme as the FVG zone, watching for
price to later return and trade back into this zone — a partial or full
"fill" of the gap is treated as a natural, statistically expected
event, though not a guaranteed one.

## Real World Analogy
A Fair Value Gap is like a sudden, sharp price spike in a local market
caused by a temporary shortage announcement — prices jump rapidly without
genuine, gradual buyer-seller negotiation occurring at every
intermediate price point, and once the shortage news is absorbed and
normal supply resumes, prices often naturally drift back to "fill in"
some of that gap as genuine two-way trading resumes at the previously
skipped price levels.

## Indian Market Example
Indian SMC-influenced Nifty 50 intraday traders specifically mark FVGs
forming during sharp, news-driven or momentum-driven candle sequences,
then monitor whether price later returns to fill these zones during
subsequent, calmer trading — often combining FVG analysis with order
block (`[18.03]`) and liquidity pool (`[18.02]`) context for higher-
confidence setups.

## US Market Example
US SMC traders analyzing futures markets frequently note that FVGs
forming during the opening minutes of a session (when volatility and
momentum are typically highest) tend to be filled with notably higher
frequency than FVGs forming during calmer, lower-volatility periods —
an empirical pattern some practitioners report, though rigorous,
published statistical validation remains limited.

## Historical Example
The specific "Fair Value Gap" terminology and precise three-candle
identification methodology emerged within SMC's 2010s-2020s
popularization (`[18.01]`), representing a distinct, more granular
concept from classical overnight/weekend gap analysis (`[12.09]`), which
has been part of technical analysis literature for considerably longer.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional market microstructure researchers studying price
efficiency have examined related concepts around rapid, one-sided price
movement and subsequent mean-reversion tendencies — while not using SMC's
specific FVG terminology, some genuine academic literature on order
imbalances and short-term price reversion provides tangential, partial
support for the underlying intuition, though direct validation of SMC's
specific three-candle FVG definition remains limited.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders should specifically practice distinguishing FVGs (an
intraday, continuous-sequence concept) from classical overnight gaps
(`[12.09]`, which occur between distinct trading sessions) — these are
related but genuinely distinct concepts that are sometimes conflated by
less careful market commentary.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts caution that FVG "fill" statistics circulated
within SMC educational content are frequently based on informal,
unsystematic observation rather than rigorous backtesting across
representative sample periods — traders relying heavily on FVG-fill
probability claims should ideally conduct their own systematic
verification (Module 30's backtesting methodology, previewed) rather
than accepting commonly-cited fill-rate figures uncritically.

## Common Mistakes
- Conflating FVGs (intraday, three-candle concept) with classical
  overnight/weekend gaps (`[12.09]`), which are distinct phenomena.
- Trading FVG fills based on commonly-cited but unverified fill-rate
  statistics, without personal systematic verification.
- Treating every FVG as an equally significant target, without
  considering additional context (order blocks, liquidity pools, overall
  structure).

## Misconceptions
- **"FVGs are the same thing as classical overnight gaps."** FVGs are a
  distinct, intraday, three-candle concept; classical gaps (`[12.09]`)
  occur between separate trading sessions.
- **"FVGs always get filled eventually."** While a common tendency,
  filling is not guaranteed, and commonly-cited fill-rate statistics
  often lack rigorous, systematic backtesting support.
- **"Every FVG is an equally reliable trading signal."** Context
  (surrounding structure, order blocks, liquidity pools) meaningfully
  affects an FVG's practical significance.

## Key Takeaways
- An FVG is a three-candle pattern where Candle 1 and Candle 3 do not
  overlap, leaving a visible price gap.
- FVGs are distinct from classical overnight/weekend gaps (`[12.09]`).
- Price often, but not always, returns to fill FVG zones — treat this
  as a tendency, not a guarantee.
- Combine FVG analysis with order blocks (`[18.03]`) and liquidity pools
  (`[18.02]`) for higher-confidence context, and verify fill-rate claims
  through personal systematic backtesting where possible.

## Summary
Fair Value Gaps — three-candle price imbalances where genuine two-way
trading appears to have been skipped — provide SMC's framework for
identifying potential future price-target "magnet" zones, distinct from
classical overnight gap analysis (`[12.09]`) and best combined with order
block and liquidity pool context rather than traded in isolation. This
sets up the next lesson's treatment of Mitigation and Inducement — SMC
concepts describing how and why price returns to previously significant
zones.
