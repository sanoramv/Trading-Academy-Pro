# [11.06] Three-Candle Patterns — Morning Star, Evening Star

> Module: Candlesticks · Lesson 6 of 10 · Estimated time: 30 minutes

## Learning Objectives
- Identify Morning Star and Evening Star patterns precisely.
- Understand the three-stage psychological narrative these patterns tell.
- Learn the role of the "star" candle's gap and small body.
- Compare three-candle pattern reliability to single- and two-candle patterns.

## Prerequisites
- `[11.04]` Two-Candle Patterns — Engulfing, Harami
- `[11.02]` Single Candlestick Patterns — Doji, Hammer, Shooting Star

## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **Morning Star** is a three-candle bullish reversal pattern: (1) a
large bearish candle continuing the prior downtrend, (2) a small-bodied
"star" candle (often gapping down, ideally showing indecision similar to
a Doji, `[11.02]`) that closes near the prior bearish candle's low, and
(3) a large bullish candle that closes well into (ideally past the
midpoint of) the first candle's body — together telling a story of
decline, indecision/exhaustion, then decisive reversal. An **Evening
Star** is the mirror-image bearish version: a large bullish candle, a
small-bodied star candle near the highs, and a large bearish candle
closing well into the first candle's body.

### Intuition
Three-candle patterns tell a more complete psychological narrative than
single- or two-candle patterns can: the first candle establishes the
prevailing trend's continued strength, the middle "star" candle captures
the critical moment of hesitation/exhaustion (directly connecting to
`[11.02]`'s Doji indecision concept and `[02.02]`'s trend exhaustion
phase), and the third candle confirms the reversal decisively — a
complete beginning-middle-end story that inherently builds in its own
confirmation step, unlike patterns requiring separate subsequent
confirmation.

## Real World Analogy
A Morning Star pattern is like watching a heated argument play out over
three distinct phases: intense escalation (Candle 1, continued decline),
a sudden, tense pause where both sides seem uncertain how to proceed
(Candle 2, the star), and then a decisive, confident turn by the
previously losing side that clearly reclaims control (Candle 3,
confirmation) — a complete, self-contained three-act story rather than a
single ambiguous moment requiring outside context to interpret.

## Indian Market Example
Morning Star patterns occasionally form on Nifty 50 or Bank Nifty daily
charts at the conclusion of sharp, multi-day corrective declines — Indian
technical analysts specifically watch for this three-candle sequence
forming at previously-established support zones (`[02.04]`) as one of the
more convincing, self-confirming reversal signals in their broader
technical toolkit, given its inherently three-stage confirming structure.

## US Market Example
Evening Star patterns have historically formed near several notable US
index short-term tops, including instances during extended 2021 rally
phases before subsequent pullbacks — US technicians treat this pattern,
when combined with additional confluence (volume, resistance level,
`[10.11]`), as a meaningfully stronger signal than a single Shooting Star
(`[11.02]`) occurring in isolation, given the additional confirming
candle built into the three-candle structure.

## Historical Example
Morning Star and Evening Star patterns, like other multi-candle
formations covered in this module, originate from the historical Japanese
candlestick tradition (`[11.09]`) — their three-stage narrative structure
(continuation, hesitation, reversal confirmation) reflects a
sophisticated, historically-refined observational framework for reading
market psychology that predates, by roughly two centuries, the more
recent Western concept of requiring explicit "confirmation candles" for
single- or two-candle patterns (`[11.02]`, `[11.04]`) — the three-candle
patterns essentially build this confirmation requirement directly into
their own definition.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional technicians generally regard three-candle reversal patterns
as carrying somewhat higher inherent reliability than single- or two-
candle patterns, specifically because the pattern's own definition already
requires the kind of follow-through confirmation that must be separately
awaited for simpler patterns — though even Morning Star/Evening Star
patterns are still combined with structural and volume confluence
(`[10.11]`) in professional practice, not relied upon in complete
isolation.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders learning three-candle patterns should specifically
practice identifying all three required stages precisely (trend-
continuation candle, small indecisive star, confirming reversal candle)
rather than loosely pattern-matching on a general visual impression —
precise, criterion-based identification, consistent with this module's
recurring emphasis, produces more reliable pattern recognition than
approximate visual matching.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders note that the **star candle's gap** (though not
always strictly required by all pattern definitions, and sometimes absent
particularly in less liquid instruments or 24-hour markets) adds
meaningful additional conviction when present — a gapping star candle
represents a more dramatic, visible break in the prevailing trend's
momentum than a non-gapping small-bodied candle occurring in the same
position.

## Common Mistakes
- Identifying only two of the three required stages and mistakenly
  calling it a complete Morning Star or Evening Star.
- Not requiring the third candle to close sufficiently far into the first
  candle's body, accepting only a marginal, weak confirmation.
- Ignoring structural context and volume even for these inherently more
  self-confirming three-candle patterns.

## Misconceptions
- **"Three-candle patterns require no additional confirmation beyond the
  pattern itself."** While inherently more self-confirming than single-
  or two-candle patterns, professional practice still recommends
  additional structural and volume confluence (`[10.11]`), not complete
  standalone reliance.
- **"The star candle must always gap for the pattern to be valid."**
  While a gap adds conviction, some practical pattern definitions accept
  a small-bodied star candle without a strict gap requirement, particularly
  in markets or instruments where gaps are less common.
- **"Morning Star and Evening Star are the same as Hammer and Shooting
  Star, just with an extra candle."** They tell a distinctly more
  elaborated three-stage narrative and require different, specific
  qualifying criteria across all three candles, not simply an embellished
  version of the single-candle patterns.

## Key Takeaways
- Morning Star (bullish) and Evening Star (bearish) are three-candle
  reversal patterns telling a complete continuation-hesitation-
  confirmation narrative.
- The middle "star" candle captures the critical moment of trend
  exhaustion/indecision, connecting to `[11.02]`'s Doji concept.
- Three-candle patterns carry somewhat higher inherent reliability than
  single- or two-candle patterns, given their built-in confirmation
  structure.
- Structural context, volume, and additional confluence remain valuable
  even for these more self-confirming patterns.

## Summary
Morning Star and Evening Star — three-candle patterns telling a complete
continuation-hesitation-confirmation psychological narrative — represent
some of the more self-confirming, historically-reliable reversal signals
in the candlestick tradition, building directly on this module's single-
and two-candle pattern foundation while requiring precise identification
of all three qualifying stages. This sets up the next lesson's continued
three-candle pattern coverage: Three White Soldiers and Three Black
Crows — patterns describing sustained, consecutive directional
conviction rather than reversal.
