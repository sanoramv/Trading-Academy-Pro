## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **candlestick** visually represents four prices for a given period —
**Open, High, Low, Close (OHLC)**, first introduced in `[10.03]` — using
two components: the **body** (the rectangle between the open and close
price) and the **wicks/shadows** (thin lines extending from the body to
the period's high and low). A **bullish candle** (typically shown green
or white) has its close above its open; a **bearish candle** (typically
red or black) has its close below its open.

### Intuition
A candlestick is a compressed visual summary of the entire buyer-versus-
seller "battle" that occurred during that period — the body shows who
ultimately "won" (bulls if close > open, bears if close < open) and by how
much (body size = magnitude of that win), while the wicks show how far
price was pushed in the opposite direction before being rejected back,
revealing rejected price levels and failed attempts by the "losing" side.

```
     |   |  <- BODY (close                |   |  <- BODY (close

     +---+     this period)               +---+     this period)
       |  <- lower wick                     |  <- lower wick
       |     (often near close)             |     (low reached,
                                             |      then rejected
                                             |      upward)

BODY SIZE = magnitude of the "win" (large body = decisive

              firmly rejected back -- often meaningful,
              especially at key structural levels, [02.04])
```

The relationship between body size and wick length across a single candle
provides an immediate, compressed read of that period's directional
conviction and any significant price rejection.

## Real World Analogy
A candlestick is like a single round's summary scorecard in a boxing
match — the body shows which fighter (buyers or sellers) landed the
decisive blows and won that specific round clearly, while the wicks show
moments where the "losing" fighter briefly had the upper hand (pushed
their opponent back) before ultimately being overcome — a single round's
scorecard alone doesn't tell you who wins the whole match, but a skilled
analyst reading many consecutive rounds' scorecards together, in the
context of the overall fight structure, can identify meaningful shifts in
momentum.

## Indian Market Example
Indian intraday traders analyzing Nifty 50 or Bank Nifty candlestick
charts routinely read individual candles' body-to-wick ratios as an
immediate gauge of intraperiod conviction — a Bank Nifty 15-minute candle
with a very large body and minimal wicks signals strong, decisive
directional conviction during that specific 15-minute window, while a
candle with a small body and long wicks on both sides signals
indecision and a contested, two-way battle.

## US Market Example
US day traders analyzing 5-minute candles on major indices like the S&P
500 futures similarly use body/wick anatomy as an immediate visual read
of short-term conviction — particularly around scheduled news events
(`[04.08]`), where a candle's wick length can reveal how much of an
initial, sharp reaction was subsequently rejected within the same period.

## Historical Example
Candlestick charting's roots trace to **18th-century Japanese rice
trading** (developed fully in `[11.09]`'s dedicated history lesson),
where traders needed an efficient visual method to track and communicate
price battles across many trading sessions — the body-and-wick
construction covered in this lesson has remained essentially unchanged
for over two centuries, a remarkable testament to its enduring visual
efficiency at compressing OHLC information into an immediately
interpretable form.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional traders reading candlestick anatomy alongside volume
(`[10.08]`) specifically look for candles with unusually large bodies AND
unusually high volume together as signals of genuinely strong, well-
participated conviction — versus a large body on unusually low volume,
which may reflect a less broadly-supported, potentially less reliable
move.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders new to candlestick charts sometimes focus purely on candle
color (green/red) without examining body size and wick length in detail —
building the habit of reading the *full* anatomy (body size, wick length,
and direction) of each significant candle, not just its color, extracts
considerably more useful information from the same chart.

## Professional Insights
Professional price action traders emphasize that a candle's **wick,
especially a long wick at a significant structural level** (`[02.04]`),
often carries as much or more analytical significance as the body itself
— a long wick represents a specific price level where a meaningful,
concentrated rejection occurred, information directly relevant to
identifying potential support/resistance and liquidity zones (`[02.06]`).

## Common Mistakes
- Reading only candle color without examining body size and wick length
  in detail.
- Analyzing a single candle's anatomy in complete isolation from its
  structural location (a theme corrected fully in `[11.08]`).
- Assuming a large body always indicates a reliable, sustainable move
  without cross-checking volume (`[10.08]`).

## Misconceptions
- **"Candle color alone tells you everything you need to know."** Body
  size and wick length provide considerably more granular, useful
  information than color alone, as this lesson's examples demonstrate.
- **"A long wick means nothing significant happened."** Long wicks often
  mark genuinely significant price rejection at specific levels,
  particularly valuable when they occur at key structural zones
  (`[02.04]`, `[02.06]`).
- **"Candlestick anatomy is a modern Western invention."** As `[11.09]`
  will cover in full, candlestick charting has Japanese origins dating
  back roughly two and a half centuries, well predating most Western
  technical analysis history covered in Module 10.

## Key Takeaways
- A candlestick's body shows which side (bulls/bears) controlled the
  period and by how much; wicks show rejected price extremes.
- Large bodies signal strong conviction; small bodies signal contested
  indecision.
- Long wicks, especially at key structural levels, mark significant price
  rejection worth noting.
- A candle's significance depends heavily on its structural location, a
  theme developed fully in `[11.08]`.

## Summary
A candlestick's anatomy — body (showing which side won the period's
buyer-seller battle and by how much) and wicks (showing rejected price
extremes) — provides a compressed, visually efficient summary of
short-term price psychology, building directly on `[10.03]`'s chart type
introduction. This foundational anatomy understanding prepares you for
the specific, named single- and multi-candle patterns covered throughout
the rest of this module, starting with the most widely-recognized single-
candle patterns: Doji, Hammer, and Shooting Star.

## Revision Notes
- Candlestick = body (open-to-close range) + wicks/shadows (high/low
  extremes).
- Bullish candle: close > open. Bearish candle: close < open.
- Body size = conviction magnitude. Wick length = rejection magnitude.
- Long wicks at key structural levels (`[02.04]`) carry particular
  significance.
- Candlestick charting: Japanese origins, ~250+ years old (`[11.09]`).

## Quiz
1. What does a candlestick's body represent?
   a) The high and low of the period
   b) The range between open and close, showing which side controlled the period
   c) Trading volume
   d) The next period's expected price

2. What does a large candle body indicate?
   a) Indecision  b) Strong directional conviction  c) No trading occurred  d) A currency devaluation

3. What do long wicks often indicate, especially at key structural levels?
   a) Nothing significant  b) Significant, concentrated price rejection  c) A stock split  d) A dividend payment

4. Where did candlestick charting originate?
   a) Modern Wall Street  b) 18th-century Japanese rice trading  c) Ancient Rome  d) 21st-century algorithmic trading

5. True or False: Candle color alone provides all the information needed
   to interpret a candlestick fully.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — The body represents the open-to-close range, showing which side controlled the period.
2. **b)** — A large body indicates strong directional conviction by one side.
3. **b)** — Long wicks, especially at key levels, indicate significant price rejection.
4. **b)** — Candlestick charting originated in 18th-century Japanese rice trading.
5. **b) False** — Body size and wick length provide considerably more information than color alone.
