# [11.02] Single Candlestick Patterns — Doji, Hammer, Shooting Star

> Module: Candlesticks · Lesson 2 of 10 · Estimated time: 35 minutes

## Learning Objectives
- Identify Doji, Hammer, and Shooting Star patterns precisely.
- Understand each pattern's psychological story and required context.
- Distinguish Hammer from Hanging Man, and Shooting Star from Inverted Hammer.
- Apply `[11.01]`'s body/wick framework to specific named patterns.

## Prerequisites
- `[11.01]` Anatomy of a Candlestick
- `[02.04]` Support and Resistance — First Principles

## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **Doji** has a very small or nonexistent body (open ≈ close), with
wicks of varying length — signaling genuine indecision, a roughly even
battle between buyers and sellers. A **Hammer** has a small body near the
top of its range, with a long lower wick (at least twice the body length)
and little to no upper wick — appearing after a decline, signaling
potential bullish reversal (buyers forcefully rejected a deeper decline).
A **Shooting Star** has a small body near the bottom of its range, with a
long upper wick and little to no lower wick — appearing after an advance,
signaling potential bearish reversal (sellers forcefully rejected further
advance).

### Intuition
Each pattern directly applies `[11.01]`'s body/wick reading framework to a
specific, named, recognizable shape — but critically, **all three
patterns require appropriate structural context** (`[02.04]`) to carry
genuine significance: a Hammer only signals a potential bullish reversal
when it appears after a decline, ideally at a support zone; the identical
candle shape appearing randomly mid-trend, without this context, carries
far less analytical meaning.

### Mental Model
```
DOJI:                HAMMER:              SHOOTING STAR:
   |  <- wick             |  <- minimal        +-+  <- small body
   |                       upper wick          | |     near bottom
 --+--  <- tiny/no        +-+  <- small          |  <- LONG upper
   |     body              | |    body near       |     wick
   |                       |     top              |
                           |
                           |  <- LONG lower
                           |     wick (2x+
                           |     body length)

CONTEXT REQUIREMENT (critical for all three):
  Doji: most significant after an extended trend (signals
        POTENTIAL indecision/exhaustion)
  Hammer: most significant AFTER A DECLINE, ideally at
          support ([02.04])
  Shooting Star: most significant AFTER AN ADVANCE, ideally
                 at resistance ([02.04])
```

### Visual Explanation
The Hammer and Shooting Star are visually near-mirror-images of each
other (long wick on opposite ends) — their differing implications come
entirely from where they appear in the prevailing trend context, not
from any difference in their basic shape logic.

## Real World Analogy
A Hammer candle is like a crowd being pushed backward forcefully during a
public event, only for a strong barrier (support) to hold and push them
right back to nearly where they started — the "long lower wick" is the
visual record of how far back they were pushed before the barrier held. A
Shooting Star is the mirror image: a crowd pushing forward enthusiastically,
only to be firmly turned back by an equally strong barrier (resistance)
near the top.

## Indian Market Example
Nifty 50 and individual Indian stocks frequently display Hammer patterns
at previously-established support zones (`[02.04]`) during pullbacks
within broader uptrends — Indian technical analysts specifically watch
for Hammers forming at well-tested historical support levels, treating
the *combination* of pattern and location as considerably more meaningful
than either alone, consistent with `[11.01]`'s emphasis on structural
context.

## US Market Example
US traders analyzing daily charts of major indices commonly identify
Doji patterns forming near the peak of extended rallies — for example,
Doji candles appearing near several notable S&P 500 short-term tops
historically preceded pullback phases, though US technicians universally
emphasize that a Doji alone, without subsequent confirming price action
and structural context, is an insufficient standalone signal.

## Historical Example
The **Hammer** and **Hanging Man** patterns share an identical visual
shape (small body near the top, long lower wick) but carry opposite
psychological implications purely based on context — a Hammer appears
after a decline (bullish reversal potential), while a Hanging Man appears
after an advance (bearish reversal potential, since the long lower wick
after an uptrend suggests sellers are beginning to successfully push price
down even though buyers managed to close the period back near the highs)
— a classic, frequently-taught illustration of why shape alone, without
context, is analytically insufficient, directly reinforcing `[11.01]`'s
core theme.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional price action traders treat single-candle patterns as
**one input requiring multiple confirming factors** (per `[10.11]`'s
confluence framework) — a Hammer at a well-tested support zone, on
above-average volume (`[10.08]`), followed by a confirming bullish candle
the next period, represents a considerably stronger, more actionable
signal than a Hammer shape alone in isolation.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders very commonly learn candlestick pattern shapes from
introductory sources without adequately learning the critical structural
context requirement — leading to frequent misapplication (trading every
Hammer-shaped candle regardless of location) and disappointing results
that then unfairly discredit the pattern itself, when the actual failure
was ignoring context, not any flaw in the pattern concept.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders specifically emphasize **waiting for confirmation**
— rather than acting immediately upon spotting a Hammer or Shooting Star,
waiting for the subsequent candle(s) to confirm the implied reversal
(e.g., a bullish candle following a Hammer, closing above the Hammer's
high) meaningfully improves reliability, at the cost of a slightly less
favorable entry price — a worthwhile trade-off most professionals
consider standard practice.

## Common Mistakes
- Trading any Hammer or Shooting Star shape regardless of structural
  context (trend direction, support/resistance location).
- Confusing a Hammer with a Hanging Man (or Shooting Star with an
  Inverted Hammer) by ignoring the critical context distinguishing them.
- Acting immediately on a single-candle pattern without waiting for
  confirmation from subsequent price action.

## Misconceptions
- **"A Hammer candle shape is always bullish, regardless of where it
  appears."** As the Hanging Man comparison demonstrates, the identical
  shape carries opposite implications depending entirely on prevailing
  trend context.
- **"A Doji always signals an imminent, certain reversal."** A Doji
  signals indecision/potential exhaustion, which sometimes precedes
  reversal and sometimes precedes trend continuation after a brief pause
  — it requires confirmation, not certainty.
- **"Single candlestick patterns are sufficient standalone trading
  signals."** As the Institutional and Professional Insights sections
  emphasize, single-candle patterns require structural context, volume
  confirmation, and follow-through confirmation to be considered reliably
  actionable.

## Key Takeaways
- Doji (indecision), Hammer (potential bullish reversal after a decline),
  and Shooting Star (potential bearish reversal after an advance) each
  apply `[11.01]`'s body/wick framework to specific, named shapes.
- Structural context (trend direction, support/resistance location) is
  essential — the same shape can carry opposite implications depending on
  context (Hammer vs. Hanging Man).
- Confirmation from subsequent price action meaningfully improves
  reliability over acting on the pattern shape alone.
- These patterns are best combined with volume and structural confluence
  (`[10.08]`, `[10.11]`), not used as standalone signals.

## Summary
Doji, Hammer, and Shooting Star — the most widely-recognized single-
candle patterns — apply `[11.01]`'s body/wick reading framework to
specific shapes signaling indecision or potential reversal, with their
genuine significance depending critically on structural context (as the
Hammer-versus-Hanging-Man comparison starkly illustrates) and confirmation
from subsequent price action, never the shape alone. This sets up the
next lesson's coverage of two additional single-candle patterns: Marubozu
and Spinning Top.

## Revision Notes
- Doji: near-zero body, signals indecision. Hammer: small body near top,

  Star: small body near bottom, long upper wick, bearish reversal

- Hammer and Hanging Man share identical shape, opposite implications
  based purely on trend context — classic illustration of context's

  pattern shape alone.
- Combine with volume (`[10.08]`) and structural confluence (`[10.11]`).

## Quiz
1. What does a Doji signal?
   a) A certain, guaranteed reversal
   b) Genuine indecision between buyers and sellers
   c) A dividend payment
   d) A currency devaluation

2. When is a Hammer pattern most significant?
   a) In the middle of a trend, with no particular context
   b) After a decline, ideally at a support zone
   c) Only on Mondays
   d) Only during earnings season

3. What distinguishes a Hammer from a Hanging Man?
   a) Nothing, they are unrelated patterns
   b) They share identical shape, but context (after a decline vs. after an advance) determines the name and implication
   c) A Hanging Man has a completely different shape
   d) Hammers only occur in currency markets

4. Why should traders wait for confirmation before acting on a single-
   candle pattern?
   a) Confirmation is unnecessary
   b) A single candle alone has limited reliability; subsequent price action confirms the signal
   c) Confirmation guarantees a profitable trade
   d) Patterns are always 100% reliable without confirmation

5. True or False: A Hammer-shaped candle is always bullish regardless of
   where it appears in the trend.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — A Doji signals genuine indecision between buyers and sellers.
2. **b)** — A Hammer is most significant after a decline, ideally at a support zone.
3. **b)** — Hammer and Hanging Man share identical shape; context determines the name and implication.
4. **b)** — Confirmation from subsequent price action improves reliability over the shape alone.
5. **b) False** — The same shape (Hanging Man) is bearish when it appears after an advance instead.
