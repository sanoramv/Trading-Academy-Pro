## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **timeframe** defines the time period each individual candle/bar on a
chart represents — ranging from very short (1-minute, 5-minute) to very
long (daily, weekly, monthly). Timeframe selection should match a
trader's **holding period and trading style**: scalpers and day traders
typically use very short timeframes (1-15 minute charts), swing traders
use medium timeframes (1-hour to daily charts), and positional/long-term
investors use longer timeframes (weekly to monthly charts) — directly
extending `[02.08]`'s multi-timeframe analysis framework into practical
day-to-day charting setup.

### Intuition
Each candle on a chart aggregates all the trading activity within that
period into a single OHLC data point — a 1-minute chart shows extremely
granular detail but high noise, while a monthly chart shows broad,
smoothed trend context but hides all short-term detail. Neither is
"better" in isolation; the appropriate timeframe depends entirely on how
long you intend to hold a position and what decisions you're trying to
make, echoing `[02.08]`'s bias/setup/entry-timing framework.

### Mental Model
```
TIMEFRAME SELECTION BY TRADING STYLE:

Scalping (seconds-minutes holding):     1-min, 5-min charts
Day Trading (intraday only):             5-min, 15-min, 1-hour charts
Swing Trading (days-weeks holding):      1-hour, 4-hour, daily charts
Positional/Long-term Investing
(months-years holding):                  Daily, weekly, monthly charts

GENERAL PRINCIPLE: your PRIMARY chart timeframe should roughly
match your intended holding period; use HIGHER timeframes for
bias/context and LOWER timeframes for entry timing, per [02.08]
```

### Visual Explanation
The same instrument's chart can tell dramatically different structural
stories depending purely on timeframe selection — a genuinely important
practical consequence of `[02.08]`'s observation that different
timeframes can show different, equally valid structure simultaneously.

## Real World Analogy
Choosing a chart timeframe is like choosing the zoom level on a map for
different purposes — planning a cross-country road trip requires a
zoomed-out national map (long timeframe, broad trend context), while
navigating the final few blocks to your specific destination requires a
zoomed-in street-level map (short timeframe, precise entry timing). Using
only the national map to navigate your final turn, or only the street
map to plan your entire cross-country route, would both be genuinely
impractical mismatches between tool and purpose.

## Indian Market Example
An Indian intraday Nifty futures trader typically works primarily with
**5-minute or 15-minute charts** for entry/exit timing, while checking the
**daily or weekly chart** each morning for broader trend bias (per
`[02.08]`) — a practical, everyday application of matching timeframe to
specific analytical purpose within a single trading session.

## US Market Example
A US long-term investor building a position in a company over several
months typically relies primarily on **weekly or monthly charts** to
assess the broader trend and avoid being distracted by short-term
noise, only occasionally checking daily charts for specific entry timing
around a favorable pullback — illustrating how appropriate timeframe
selection scales with genuinely different intended holding periods.

## Historical Example
The proliferation of ultra-short timeframe charts (1-minute, even
tick-by-tick charts) became practically accessible to retail traders only
with the rise of electronic trading and real-time data feeds from the
1990s-2000s onward (`[01.04]`'s NSE 1992 electronic transition, and
similar global electronic trading transitions) — before this
technological shift, most technical analysis, including Dow's original
work (`[10.02]`), was necessarily conducted on longer timeframes given
data availability constraints of the era.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional execution algorithms (Module 29) often operate on extremely
granular, sub-minute or even millisecond timeframes for order execution
purposes, while the same institution's portfolio managers might be making
investment decisions based on weekly or monthly chart context — a single
institution routinely operates across the full timeframe spectrum
simultaneously, with different teams and purposes matched to appropriate
timeframes.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders sometimes select their chart timeframe somewhat randomly
or based purely on habit, without deliberately matching it to their
actual intended holding period — a mismatch (e.g., making swing-trading
decisions primarily from a 1-minute chart) is a common, avoidable source
of poor decision-making and excessive noise-driven trading.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders caution against **"timeframe shopping"** — the habit
of checking progressively shorter or longer timeframes until finding one
that happens to confirm a pre-existing bias, rather than objectively
starting from the appropriate higher timeframe first (per `[02.08]`'s
top-down MTFA process) — a subtle but genuinely important discipline
distinguishing rigorous multi-timeframe analysis from confirmation-biased
timeframe cherry-picking.

## Common Mistakes
- Selecting a chart timeframe that doesn't match the trader's actual
  intended holding period.
- "Timeframe shopping" until finding a chart that confirms a pre-existing
  bias, rather than starting objectively from the higher timeframe.
- Making long-term investment decisions based purely on very short-
  timeframe chart noise.

## Misconceptions
- **"A shorter timeframe always provides more accurate or useful
  information."** Shorter timeframes provide more granular detail but also
  more noise — appropriateness depends entirely on matching timeframe to
  trading style and purpose, not a universal "more detail is always
  better" rule.
- **"Timeframe choice doesn't really matter as long as you're looking at
  the same instrument."** As the same-instrument, different-timeframes
  ASCII diagram illustrates, timeframe choice can produce dramatically
  different structural pictures of identical underlying price history.
- **"Professional traders only use one single timeframe."** As the
  Institutional Perspective illustrates, professionals routinely work
  across multiple timeframes simultaneously for different specific
  purposes (bias, setup, execution).

## Key Takeaways
- Timeframe selection should match intended holding period: scalping
  (very short), day trading (short), swing trading (medium), positional
  investing (long).
- The same instrument can show dramatically different structural pictures
  depending purely on timeframe, directly extending `[02.08]`'s multi-
  timeframe insight.
- "Timeframe shopping" to confirm a pre-existing bias is a discipline
  failure to avoid, distinct from legitimate multi-timeframe analysis.
- Institutions and skilled traders routinely operate across multiple
  timeframes simultaneously for different specific purposes.

## Summary
Timeframe selection — matching chart granularity to intended holding
period, from scalping's 1-minute charts to positional investing's monthly
charts — directly extends `[02.08]`'s multi-timeframe analysis framework
into practical, everyday charting setup, with the discipline of avoiding
"timeframe shopping" separating rigorous analysis from confirmation-biased
cherry-picking. This completes the module's foundational charting

## Quiz
1. How should timeframe selection generally relate to trading style?
   a) It should be completely random
   b) It should match the trader's intended holding period
   c) Only professionals should choose a specific timeframe
   d) Timeframe never matters

2. What is "timeframe shopping"?
   a) A legitimate multi-timeframe analysis technique
   b) Cherry-picking a timeframe that confirms a pre-existing bias, a discipline failure
   c) Buying stocks across different time zones
   d) A type of order execution

3. Can the same instrument show meaningfully different structure across
   different timeframes?
   a) No, structure is always identical regardless of timeframe
   b) Yes — each timeframe is an equally valid description of that period's price action
   c) Only for currencies
   d) Only during market holidays

4. Do institutions typically use only a single timeframe for all
   purposes?
   a) Yes, always exactly one timeframe
   b) No — they operate across multiple timeframes for different purposes simultaneously
   c) Institutions never use charts
   d) Only retail traders use multiple timeframes

5. True or False: A shorter timeframe always provides objectively more
   useful information than a longer timeframe.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Timeframe selection should match the trader's intended holding period.
2. **b)** — Timeframe shopping is cherry-picking a timeframe to confirm bias, a discipline failure.
3. **b)** — Different timeframes can show genuinely different, equally valid structure.
4. **b)** — Institutions operate across multiple timeframes simultaneously for different purposes.
5. **b) False** — Shorter timeframes provide more detail but also more noise; appropriateness depends on trading style and purpose.
