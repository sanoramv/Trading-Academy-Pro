## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Momentum oscillators** measure the speed and strength of price
movement, typically bounded within a defined range, helping identify
potentially overextended conditions. The **Relative Strength Index
(RSI)** measures the magnitude of recent gains versus losses on a 0-100
scale, with readings above 70 conventionally considered "overbought" and
below 30 "oversold." The **Stochastic Oscillator** compares a closing
price to its recent trading range, also on a 0-100 scale, similarly
interpreted. **MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence)** measures
the relationship between two EMAs (`[10.06]`) of different periods,
functioning as a hybrid trend-following/momentum tool via its signal line
crossovers and histogram.

### Intuition
Oscillators exist to answer a question moving averages (`[10.06]`) and
trendlines (`[10.05]`) don't directly address: not just "what direction is
price moving" but "how fast and how far has it moved, and is this pace
becoming unsustainable?" The overbought/oversold framework rests on the
intuition that price rarely moves in one direction indefinitely without
some pause or reversal — though this intuition, as this lesson develops,
requires significant nuance in genuinely strong trending conditions.

### Mental Model
```
RSI = 100 - [100 / (1 + Average Gain / Average Loss)]
      (0-100 scale; >70 conventionally "overbought,"
       <30 conventionally "oversold")

STOCHASTIC = compares CLOSE to the recent HIGH-LOW RANGE
      (0-100 scale; similar overbought/oversold convention)

MACD = (Fast EMA - Slow EMA), plotted against its own
       "signal line" (an EMA of the MACD line itself)
      (crossovers of MACD above/below its signal line are
       widely-used momentum shift signals; the MACD
       histogram visualizes the gap between the two)
```

### Visual Explanation
The critical, most commonly misunderstood nuance: "overbought" does NOT
mean "must reverse" — in a genuinely strong trend, an oscillator can
remain in overbought/oversold territory for an extended period while
price continues trending strongly, a pattern that traps many
inexperienced traders fading (betting against) strong trends prematurely.

## Real World Analogy
Treating "overbought" as an automatic sell signal is like assuming a
sprinter running impressively fast must be about to collapse from
exhaustion at any moment — sometimes true for an ordinary runner near
their limit, but a genuinely elite, well-conditioned athlete (a strong
trend) can sustain an impressive pace far longer than a casual observer
might expect, and betting against them purely because they're "running
fast" (overbought) repeatedly loses to a runner who simply keeps running
well.

## Indian Market Example
During Nifty 50's strong 2020-2021 recovery uptrend, RSI frequently
reached and remained in overbought territory (above 70) for extended
periods while the index continued rising substantially — Indian traders
who mechanically shorted or exited positions purely based on "RSI
overbought" readings during this period would have repeatedly missed
significant further upside, a widely-discussed cautionary lesson among
Indian technical analysts about naive oscillator use in strong trends.

## US Market Example
US technology stocks during strong bull phases have similarly shown
extended periods of RSI and Stochastic readings in overbought territory
without any near-term reversal, while MACD's trend-following crossover
signals (rather than pure overbought/oversold readings) have historically
provided somewhat more reliable timing signals during these genuinely
strong trending conditions, illustrating MACD's hybrid trend-momentum
nature discussed in this lesson's Core Concepts.

## Historical Example
**J. Welles Wilder Jr.**, who developed RSI in 1978 (also developing
several other widely-used indicators, including the Parabolic SAR and
Average True Range), explicitly cautioned in his original writings that
overbought/oversold readings should be interpreted within the context of
overall trend direction, not applied mechanically in isolation — a
nuance Wilder himself emphasized from the indicator's earliest days that
many subsequent users have historically overlooked.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional systematic traders typically combine oscillator signals
with explicit trend filters (only acting on oversold signals within an
established uptrend, for instance, filtering out oversold signals during
downtrends where they're historically less reliable) — a rules-based
approach directly addressing this lesson's core caution about naive,
context-free oscillator use.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders very commonly treat "RSI above 70" or "RSI below 30" as a
simple, standalone trading signal without considering the broader trend
context — this lesson's core message, reinforced by Wilder's own original
caution, is that oscillator readings must always be interpreted within
market state and trend context, never as an isolated mechanical rule.

## Professional Insights
Professional technicians increasingly favor using oscillators for
**divergence analysis** (covered fully in `[10.10]`) rather than raw
overbought/oversold levels alone — divergence (where price makes a new
extreme but the oscillator doesn't confirm it) is considered a more
nuanced, often more reliable signal than simple threshold-crossing alone.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating overbought/oversold readings as automatic reversal signals
  without checking the broader trend context.
- Naively fading (betting against) a strong trend purely because an
  oscillator has reached an extreme reading.
- Using oscillators in complete isolation from market structure (Module
  02) and other confirming tools.

## Misconceptions
- **"Overbought means the price must fall soon."** As Wilder's own
  original caution and the strong-trend examples demonstrate, overbought
  readings can persist for extended periods during genuinely strong
  trends without any near-term reversal.
- **"All oscillators (RSI, Stochastic, MACD) measure exactly the same
  thing."** They use different underlying calculations (gain/loss
  magnitude for RSI, close-relative-to-range for Stochastic, EMA
  relationships for MACD) and can produce meaningfully different, though
  often correlated, readings.
- **"MACD is a pure momentum oscillator like RSI or Stochastic."** MACD is
  better understood as a hybrid trend-following/momentum tool, since it's
  fundamentally built from moving average relationships (`[10.06]`), not a
  purely bounded oscillator in the same sense as RSI or Stochastic.

## Key Takeaways
- RSI and Stochastic measure momentum on a 0-100 scale, with
  conventional overbought (>70) and oversold (<30) thresholds; MACD
  measures EMA relationships as a hybrid trend/momentum tool.
- Overbought/oversold readings can persist for extended periods during
  genuinely strong trends — a critical, historically overlooked nuance
  even Wilder himself cautioned about.
- Oscillator signals should always be interpreted within broader trend/
  market-state context (Module 02), never in isolation.
- Divergence analysis (`[10.10]`) is often considered more nuanced and
  reliable than raw threshold-crossing signals alone.

## Summary
Momentum oscillators — RSI, Stochastic, and MACD — measure the speed and
strength of price movement, with conventional overbought/oversold
thresholds providing genuinely useful but frequently misapplied signals,
since strong trending conditions can sustain extreme readings far longer
than naive threshold-based trading anticipates, a nuance J. Welles Wilder
himself cautioned about from RSI's earliest days. This sets up the next
lesson's focus on volume as a confirming tool — extending Dow's original
volume-confirmation tenet (`[10.02]`) into modern practice.

## Revision Notes
- RSI: 0-100 scale, gain/loss magnitude, >70 overbought / <30 oversold
  (conventional).
- Stochastic: 0-100 scale, close relative to recent range, similar
  convention.
- MACD: hybrid trend/momentum tool built from EMA relationships
  (`[10.06]`), uses signal line crossovers/histogram.
- Overbought/oversold can persist through extended strong trends — check
  trend context first (`[02.01]`-`[02.02]`).
- J. Welles Wilder (RSI creator, 1978) himself cautioned against naive,
  context-free interpretation.

## Quiz
1. What is RSI's conventional overbought threshold?
   a) Above 50  b) Above 70  c) Above 30  d) Above 100

2. Can overbought/oversold readings persist for extended periods during
   strong trends?
   a) No, never  b) Yes — this is a common, historically overlooked nuance  c) Only in India  d) Only for currencies

3. Who developed RSI, and in what year?
   a) Charles Dow, 1900  b) J. Welles Wilder Jr., 1978  c) Warren Buffett, 1965  d) Richard Wyckoff, 1930

4. Is MACD best understood as a pure momentum oscillator like RSI?
   a) Yes, identical to RSI
   b) No — it's a hybrid trend-following/momentum tool built from EMA relationships
   c) MACD measures currency exchange rates only
   d) MACD is unrelated to moving averages

5. True or False: An overbought RSI reading is an automatic, reliable
   signal that price must fall soon, regardless of trend context.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — RSI's conventional overbought threshold is above 70.
2. **b)** — Overbought/oversold readings can persist for extended periods during strong trends.
3. **b)** — J. Welles Wilder Jr. developed RSI in 1978.
4. **b)** — MACD is a hybrid trend-following/momentum tool, not a pure bounded oscillator like RSI.
5. **b) False** — Overbought readings can persist through strong trends; context matters significantly.
