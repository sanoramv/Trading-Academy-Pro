# [09.05] Valuing Growth vs Value Stocks

> Module: Valuation · Lesson 5 of 8 · Estimated time: 30 minutes

## Learning Objectives
- Distinguish growth and value investing philosophies.
- Understand why growth stocks are more sensitive to discount rate changes.
- Learn the historical performance debate between growth and value.
- Apply appropriate valuation emphasis based on a stock's growth/value classification.

## Prerequisites
- `[09.03]` Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Fundamentals
- `[05.04]` Interest Rates — What They Are and Why They Move Markets

## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Growth stocks** are companies expected to grow revenue and earnings
significantly faster than the broader market, typically trading at higher
valuation multiples (`[08.06]`, `[09.02]`) reflecting these elevated
growth expectations, and often reinvesting most or all cash flow rather
than paying dividends (`[09.04]`). **Value stocks** are companies trading
at lower valuation multiples relative to their current fundamentals,
often mature, slower-growing businesses, sometimes reflecting genuine
undervaluation and sometimes reflecting legitimate concerns about future
prospects (`[08.06]`'s valuation trap caution). Neither classification is
inherently "better" — they represent different risk/return profiles and
require different valuation emphasis.

### Intuition
Growth stocks derive most of their estimated value from cash flows far in
the future (heavy terminal value weighting, `[09.03]`), making them
especially sensitive to discount rate changes via the discounting
mechanism (`[05.04]`) — when interest rates rise, growth stock valuations
tend to compress more than value stock valuations, since more of their
value depends on distant future cash flows being discounted at a higher
rate. Value stocks, with more of their value derived from current,
near-term cash flows and assets, are comparatively less rate-sensitive.

## Real World Analogy
Growth stocks are like young fruit trees still years away from their
peak harvest — their current value depends heavily on optimistic
assumptions about their future productive years, making that valuation
quite sensitive to how you discount those distant future harvests. Value
stocks are like mature, already-fruiting trees with a predictable,
near-term harvest — their value depends much less on distant future
assumptions and much more on what they're already reliably producing
right now.

## Indian Market Example
Indian **new-age consumer internet companies** (e-commerce, food delivery,
fintech platforms that IPO'd in recent years) exemplify growth stock
characteristics — high revenue growth expectations, minimal current
profitability, heavy reliance on DCF-style valuation with substantial
terminal value assumptions — in contrast to established **Indian FMCG or
utility companies**, which more closely fit the value stock profile with
stable dividends and lower multiples.

## US Market Example
The extended period of **near-zero US interest rates** through much of
the 2010s and early 2020s coincided with a sustained period of growth
stock outperformance relative to value stocks — directly consistent with
this lesson's rate sensitivity framework, since low discount rates
particularly favor the heavily terminal-value-weighted growth stock
category — a pattern that notably reversed during the 2022 rate-hiking
cycle (`[05.04]`, `[06.02]`), when growth stocks underperformed value
stocks significantly.

## Historical Example
The **"growth versus value" performance debate** has been extensively
studied by academic finance researchers for decades, including
foundational work by Eugene Fama and Kenneth French identifying a
historical "value premium" (value stocks outperforming growth stocks over
very long horizons in some studied periods) — though this pattern has not
held consistently across all time periods (notably, growth significantly
outperformed value through much of the 2010s), illustrating that neither
style demonstrates permanent, guaranteed superiority.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional asset managers frequently offer distinct **"growth" and
"value" fund strategies** as separate investment products, reflecting
genuinely different client risk/return preferences and portfolio
construction philosophies — this isn't merely an academic distinction but
a structurally significant categorization shaping how large pools of
capital are actually allocated and marketed.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors sometimes hold strong, dogmatic preferences for either
growth or value investing without recognizing that appropriate valuation
*methodology* (DCF emphasis vs. relative/DDM emphasis) should adapt to the
specific stock's characteristics, rather than applying one universal
valuation approach regardless of a company's growth/value profile.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts note that the growth-value distinction is
increasingly viewed as a **spectrum rather than a strict binary** — many
companies exhibit characteristics of both, and rigid classification can
obscure more than it reveals; the underlying analytical question — how
much of this company's value depends on distant, uncertain future growth
versus current, more predictable cash generation — matters more than the
label itself.

## Common Mistakes
- Applying DDM to a growth stock with minimal dividends, or applying pure
  relative valuation to a genuinely differentiated growth company without
  DCF-based intrinsic analysis.
- Assuming growth or value investing is permanently, universally superior
  based on any single historical period's performance.
- Not adjusting rate-sensitivity expectations based on a stock's growth/
  value classification when assessing interest rate risk.

## Misconceptions
- **"Growth stocks are always better long-term investments than value
  stocks, or vice versa."** Historical performance has favored each style
  during different periods — neither demonstrates permanent, guaranteed
  superiority, as the Fama-French research and 2010s-vs-2022 pattern
  illustrate.
- **"The growth-value distinction is a strict, binary classification."**
  Professional analysts increasingly view it as a spectrum, with the
  underlying analytical question (dependence on future vs. current cash
  flows) mattering more than the label.
- **"Value stocks are always safer than growth stocks."** While
  comparatively less rate-sensitive, value stocks can carry their own
  risks (declining industries, `[08.09]`; valuation traps, `[09.06]`) —
  "value" doesn't automatically mean "safe."

## Key Takeaways
- Growth stocks derive value heavily from distant future cash flows
  (terminal value), making them more sensitive to interest rate changes.
- Value stocks derive value more from current/near-term cash flows and
  assets, making them comparatively less rate-sensitive.
- Neither style demonstrates permanent historical superiority — growth
  and value have each outperformed during different periods.
- The growth-value distinction is increasingly viewed as a spectrum,
  with rate sensitivity as the more analytically useful underlying
  question.

## Summary
The growth-versus-value distinction — reflecting how much of a stock's
value depends on distant, uncertain future growth (favoring DCF emphasis
and carrying higher interest rate sensitivity) versus current, more
predictable cash generation (favoring relative valuation/DDM emphasis and
lower rate sensitivity) — directly extends `[05.04]`'s discounting
mechanism into a practical, widely-used investment framework, with neither
style demonstrating permanent historical superiority. This sets up the
next lesson's focus on valuation traps — the specific dangers of
misapplying value-style analysis to companies whose low multiples reflect
genuine, not illusory, problems.

## Quiz
1. Why are growth stocks generally more sensitive to interest rate
   changes than value stocks?
   a) They are legally required to be more volatile
   b) More of their value depends on distant future cash flows, heavily affected by discount rate changes
   c) They never pay any taxes
   d) Interest rates don't affect any stocks

2. What happened to growth vs. value performance during 2022's rate-hiking
   cycle?
   a) Growth stocks strongly outperformed value stocks
   b) Value stocks outperformed growth stocks
   c) There was no difference at all
   d) Both fell to zero

3. What did Fama and French's research identify regarding value stocks?
   a) Value stocks always underperform in every period
   b) A historical "value premium" over some very long studied horizons, though not consistent across all periods
   c) Growth stocks never existed before 2000
   d) Value investing is illegal

4. Is the growth-value distinction considered a strict binary by
   professional analysts today?
   a) Yes, always strictly binary
   b) Increasingly viewed as a spectrum, not a strict binary
   c) Only value stocks exist
   d) Only growth stocks exist

5. True or False: Value stocks are always completely safe with no
   meaningful risks.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Growth stocks' value depends more on distant future cash flows, more sensitive to discount rate changes.
2. **b)** — Value stocks outperformed growth stocks during 2022's rate-hiking cycle, consistent with the rate sensitivity framework.
3. **b)** — Fama-French identified a historical value premium, though not consistent across all periods.
4. **b)** — The distinction is increasingly viewed as a spectrum by professional analysts.
5. **b) False** — Value stocks carry their own risks (declining industries, valuation traps), not automatic safety.
