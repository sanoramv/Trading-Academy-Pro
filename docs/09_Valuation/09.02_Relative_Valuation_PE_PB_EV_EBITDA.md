## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Relative valuation** estimates a company's value by comparing its
valuation multiples to similar companies (peers) or its own historical
range, on the premise that similar businesses should trade at broadly
similar multiples, absent a specific justified reason for divergence.
Beyond P/E (`[08.06]`), key multiples include: **Price-to-Book (P/B)** =
Share Price / Book Value per Share (`[07.03]`'s equity, per share) —
useful for asset-heavy businesses like banks; and **EV/EBITDA** =
Enterprise Value / EBITDA (`[07.08]`), where **Enterprise Value (EV)** =
Market Capitalization + Total Debt − Cash — often preferred over P/E for
cross-company comparison since it accounts for differing capital
structures (`[07.07]`) and is unaffected by different tax jurisdictions or
non-cash depreciation policies.

### Intuition
Each multiple normalizes price by a different fundamental metric, useful
in different contexts: P/E normalizes by earnings (useful for profitable,
stable companies); P/B normalizes by book value (useful for asset-heavy
businesses like banks and financial institutions, where book value closely
tracks tangible net worth); EV/EBITDA normalizes by pre-interest,
pre-tax, pre-depreciation cash-generating capacity (useful for comparing
companies with different debt levels and depreciation policies, since it
uses Enterprise Value — which includes debt — rather than just equity
market cap).

### Mental Model
```
P/E      = Share Price / EPS
           (best for profitable, stable-earnings companies;
            distorted by different capital structures/tax rates)

P/B      = Share Price / Book Value per Share
           (best for asset-heavy businesses, especially banks/
            financials where book value ≈ tangible net worth)

EV/EBITDA = Enterprise Value / EBITDA
           (best for CROSS-COMPANY comparison with different
            capital structures -- EV includes debt, EBITDA
            excludes interest/tax/depreciation differences)
```

### Visual Explanation
Enterprise Value captures the total cost to acquire a company's entire
business (equity plus assuming its debt, minus its cash cushion) — a more
complete measure than market capitalization alone for comparing companies
with different leverage levels.

## Real World Analogy
Relative valuation is like pricing a used car by comparing it to similar
recently-sold cars (same make, model, age, mileage) rather than
estimating its value from scratch — if comparable cars have consistently
sold for a certain price range, a car priced significantly above or below
that range demands an explanation (unusually low mileage justifying a
premium, or hidden damage justifying a discount) — exactly how relative
valuation flags stocks trading at a premium or discount to peers as
warranting further investigation.

## Indian Market Example
**Indian private sector banks** are frequently compared using **P/B
ratio** rather than P/E, since banking is a fundamentally asset-heavy,
balance-sheet-driven business where book value closely reflects tangible
net worth — analysts routinely compare banks like HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank,
and Kotak Mahindra Bank on P/B multiples, with premium P/B multiples
typically justified by superior ROE (`[08.03]`) and asset quality relative
to peers.

## US Market Example
**EV/EBITDA** is widely used in US private equity and M&A analysis
(`[07.08]`, connecting to leveraged buyouts from `[07.07]`) specifically
because it removes the distorting effect of different target companies'
existing capital structures — a company being evaluated for acquisition
will likely have its capital structure completely restructured post-
acquisition, making EV/EBITDA's capital-structure-neutral comparison more
relevant than P/E for this specific analytical purpose.

## Historical Example
During the **dot-com bubble** (`[08.06]`, `[09.01]`), many internet
companies had no earnings at all (making P/E uncalculable) and minimal
tangible book value (making P/B similarly unhelpful), leading some
analysts at the time to shift toward alternative, less rigorous metrics
(like price-to-revenue or price-to-website-visitors) specifically to
justify otherwise unsupportable valuations — a historical cautionary
example of how the *absence* of meaningful traditional multiples should
itself be treated as a warning sign, not an invitation to invent
increasingly disconnected alternative metrics.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional M&A and private equity analysts rely heavily on EV/EBITDA
"comparable company" and "precedent transaction" analyses as core
valuation methodologies for acquisition targets, building detailed peer
group comparisons and precedent deal multiples as standard components of
formal valuation work — one of the most established, high-stakes
professional applications of relative valuation.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors commonly default to P/E as their sole relative valuation
metric, missing that P/B may be more appropriate for asset-heavy sectors
(banks, real estate) and EV/EBITDA more appropriate for comparing
companies with meaningfully different debt levels — matching the right
multiple to the right context, as this lesson emphasizes, improves
analytical quality significantly.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts caution that **peer group selection** is itself a
critical, sometimes underappreciated judgment call — comparing a company
to an inappropriately chosen peer group (too broad, too narrow, or
including companies with fundamentally different growth/risk profiles)
can produce misleading relative valuation conclusions regardless of how
carefully the multiples themselves are calculated.

## Common Mistakes
- Using P/E as the default multiple for every company type, regardless of
  whether P/B or EV/EBITDA might be more contextually appropriate.
- Not adjusting for capital structure differences when comparing P/E
  across companies with meaningfully different debt levels.
- Selecting an inappropriate or overly narrow/broad peer group for
  comparison.

## Misconceptions
- **"P/E is always the best, most universal valuation multiple."**
  Different multiples suit different contexts — P/B for asset-heavy
  businesses, EV/EBITDA for cross-capital-structure comparison, as this
  lesson's framework establishes.
- **"A stock trading at a discount to peers is automatically
  undervalued."** As with `[08.06]`'s P/E caution, a discount can reflect
  genuinely justified concerns (weaker moat, governance issues, per
  `[08.07]`-`[08.08]`) rather than a straightforward buying opportunity.
- **"When traditional multiples don't apply (e.g., no earnings), any
  alternative metric is equally valid."** The dot-com bubble example shows
  that the *absence* of meaningful traditional multiples often itself
  signals genuine valuation risk, not license to adopt less rigorous
  alternative metrics uncritically.

## Key Takeaways
- P/E, P/B, and EV/EBITDA each normalize price by a different fundamental
  metric, suited to different analytical contexts.
- EV/EBITDA is often preferred for cross-company comparison since it
  accounts for differing capital structures via Enterprise Value.
- Appropriate peer group selection is a critical, judgment-intensive step
  in relative valuation.
- The dot-com bubble illustrates how the absence of meaningful traditional
  multiples can itself be a warning sign.

## Summary
Relative valuation — using P/E, P/B, and EV/EBITDA multiples compared
against appropriately-selected peers and historical ranges — provides a
practical, widely-used valuation approach, with each multiple suited to
different contexts (P/B for asset-heavy businesses, EV/EBITDA for cross-
capital-structure comparison), and the dot-com bubble offering a stark
warning against abandoning rigorous multiples when traditional ones don't
apply favorably. This sets up the next lesson's shift to intrinsic
valuation via the Discounted Cash Flow model — an approach independent of
peer comparison entirely.

## Quiz
1. How is Enterprise Value calculated?
   a) Share Price × Shares Outstanding only
   b) Market Capitalization + Total Debt − Cash
   c) Net Profit / Revenue
   d) Assets minus Liabilities

2. Why is EV/EBITDA often preferred over P/E for cross-company comparison?
   a) It's always a smaller number
   b) It accounts for differing capital structures via Enterprise Value
   c) It ignores profitability entirely
   d) It's easier to calculate

3. When is P/B ratio particularly useful?
   a) For asset-heavy businesses like banks
   b) Only for technology companies
   c) Never useful
   d) Only for currencies

4. What did the dot-com bubble illustrate about relative valuation
   multiples?
   a) All multiples are always meaningless
   b) The absence of meaningful traditional multiples was itself a warning sign
   c) P/E is always the best multiple regardless of context
   d) Book value is irrelevant to all companies

5. True or False: A stock trading at a discount to its peer group's
   average multiple is automatically undervalued and a good buy.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Enterprise Value = Market Capitalization + Total Debt − Cash.
2. **b)** — EV/EBITDA accounts for differing capital structures via Enterprise Value.
3. **a)** — P/B is particularly useful for asset-heavy businesses like banks.
4. **b)** — The absence of meaningful traditional multiples during the dot-com bubble was itself a warning sign.
5. **b) False** — A discount can reflect genuinely justified concerns, not automatic undervaluation.
