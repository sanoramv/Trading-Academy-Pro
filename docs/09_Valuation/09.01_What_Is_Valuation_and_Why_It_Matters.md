# [09.01] What Is Valuation and Why It Matters

- Understand why even a great company can be a poor investment at the wrong price.

## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Valuation** is the process of estimating an asset's **intrinsic
value** — what it is genuinely worth based on its underlying
fundamentals — as distinct from its current **market price**, which
reflects whatever buyers and sellers are currently willing to transact at
(recall `[01.01]`'s demand/supply-driven price discovery). The two
primary valuation approaches are: **relative valuation** (comparing a
company's valuation multiples, like P/E, to peers or historical norms,
`[08.06]`) and **intrinsic/absolute valuation** (estimating value directly
from a company's expected future cash flows, independent of what other
similar companies are trading at, primarily via the Discounted Cash Flow
model).

### Intuition
Price and value are not the same thing, even though they're related and
tend to converge over long time horizons (`[08.01]`). A stock's price can
be pushed above or below its intrinsic value by short-term sentiment,
liquidity conditions, or market-wide factors (Modules 02, 04, 06) — the
entire purpose of valuation is to form an independent view of genuine
worth, so you can assess whether the current price offers a favorable,
neutral, or unfavorable entry point relative to that worth.

### Mental Model
```
PRICE:        What the market is currently offering/demanding
               (driven by sentiment, liquidity, supply/demand,
                per Module 02)

VALUE:         What the underlying business is genuinely worth
               (driven by fundamentals: cash flows, growth,
                risk, per Module 07-08)

INVESTMENT
OPPORTUNITY = f(gap between PRICE and VALUE)

  Price < Value  --> Potentially undervalued (favorable entry)
  Price = Value  --> Fairly valued
  Price > Value  --> Potentially overvalued (unfavorable entry)
```

### Visual Explanation
Price and value can diverge significantly over the short-to-medium term,
but the core premise of fundamental, value-oriented investing (`[08.01]`)
is that they tend to converge over sufficiently long horizons.

## Real World Analogy
Valuation is like appraising a house's genuine market worth independent
of its current asking price — an appraiser examines the house's condition,
size, location, and comparable recent sales to form an independent
estimate of value, which may be higher, lower, or equal to whatever the
seller happens to be asking. A savvy buyer uses this independent valuation
to negotiate or decide whether the current asking price represents a fair,
favorable, or unfavorable deal — exactly the process fundamental investors
apply to stocks.

## Indian Market Example
Indian equity analysts routinely publish **target prices** for stocks like
Reliance Industries or Infosys, derived from a combination of relative
valuation (comparing P/E, EV/EBITDA multiples to historical ranges and
peers, `[09.02]`) and intrinsic valuation (DCF models, `[09.03]`) — these
target prices represent the analyst's independent estimate of intrinsic
value, which the current market price may sit above, below, or near at
any given time.

## US Market Example
**Warren Buffett's** decades-long investment approach (`[08.01]`,
`[08.08]`) centers explicitly on this price-versus-value distinction — his
famous quote, "Price is what you pay, value is what you get," directly
captures this lesson's core premise, and his investment success is
substantially attributed to disciplined patience in waiting for prices to
fall meaningfully below his independently-estimated intrinsic value
before investing.

## Historical Example
The **dot-com bubble** (referenced in `[01.02]`, `[08.06]`) represents a
historically extreme, sustained divergence between price and any
reasonable intrinsic value estimate — many internet companies with minimal
or no profits traded at valuations implying growth expectations that
proved entirely unrealistic, illustrating both how far price can diverge
from value during periods of extreme sentiment, and how painful the
eventual convergence (the 2000-2002 crash) can be for investors who
ignored this divergence.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional equity research analysts' core deliverable is essentially a
formal valuation — combining relative and intrinsic approaches to arrive
at a specific target price and buy/hold/sell recommendation, published and
updated as new information emerges, representing one of the most
established, resource-intensive professional applications of this
module's entire framework.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors often conflate "a good company" with "a good investment
right now," without performing any explicit valuation step — this
module's entire purpose is closing that gap, building the practical
toolkit (relative multiples, DCF, and their proper application) that turns
qualitative business quality assessment (Module 08) into an actual,
comparable value estimate.

## Professional Insights
Professional valuation analysts emphasize that valuation is inherently
**a range, not a single precise number** — reasonable analysts using
slightly different growth, discount rate, or risk assumptions can and do
arrive at meaningfully different intrinsic value estimates for the same
company, reinforcing `[08.01]`'s point that fundamental analysis is
probabilistic and judgment-based, not a precise science.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating a stock's current price as automatically reflecting its true
  value, skipping independent valuation entirely.
- Assuming valuation produces a single, precise "correct" number rather
  than a reasonable range under different assumptions.
- Buying a "good company" without any explicit consideration of whether
  the current price offers a favorable entry point.

## Misconceptions
- **"A stock's price always reflects its true value in an efficient
  market."** While markets are generally reasonably efficient over time,
  the dot-com bubble and numerous other historical episodes demonstrate
  meaningful, sometimes extended, divergences between price and any
  reasonable value estimate.
- **"Valuation is purely subjective with no rigorous methodology."** While
  it involves genuine judgment and produces a range rather than a single
  number, the specific frameworks developed in this module (relative
  multiples, DCF, dividend discount models) provide systematic, rigorous
  methodology, not pure guesswork.
- **"A cheap (low P/E) stock is automatically undervalued and
  attractive."** As `[08.06]` established, a low multiple can reflect
  genuinely weak prospects rather than undervaluation — this module's
  later lesson on valuation traps (`[09.06]`) develops this caution
  further.

## Key Takeaways
- Valuation estimates intrinsic value, distinct from current market price,
  using relative (multiples-based) or intrinsic (cash-flow-based)
  approaches.
- Price and value can diverge significantly, sometimes for extended
  periods, as the dot-com bubble starkly illustrates.
- Valuation produces a reasonable range, not a single precise number —
  inherent uncertainty requires appropriate humility.
- Institutional equity research is substantially built around exactly this
  price-versus-value framework.

## Summary
Valuation — estimating a business's genuine intrinsic value and comparing
it to current market price — is the essential final step connecting
Modules 07-08's statement literacy and business quality assessment to an
actual, actionable investment judgment, captured precisely in Warren
Buffett's "price is what you pay, value is what you get." The dot-com
bubble's extreme price-value divergence stands as a permanent historical
reminder of this distinction's importance. This sets up the next lesson's
detailed treatment of relative valuation — the P/E, P/B, and EV/EBITDA
multiples most commonly used in practice.

   b) Price is what you pay; value is what you genuinely get — they can differ
   c) Only price matters, never value
   d) Only value matters, never price

   divergence?
   a) The 1875 founding of BSE
   b) The dot-com bubble
   c) The 2023 T+1 settlement transition
   d) The 2021 Zomato IPO alone

5. True or False: Valuation produces a single, universally-agreed precise
   number for any given company.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Valuation estimates intrinsic value, distinct from current market price.
2. **b)** — Relative valuation and intrinsic valuation are the two primary approaches.
3. **b)** — The quote captures that price paid and genuine value received can differ.
4. **b)** — The dot-com bubble illustrates extreme, sustained price-value divergence.
5. **b) False** — Valuation produces a reasonable range under different assumptions, not a single precise number.
