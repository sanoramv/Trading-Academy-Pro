## Core Concepts

### Definition
The **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) model** estimates a company's intrinsic
value by projecting its future **Free Cash Flow** (`[07.04]`) over a
forecast period, then discounting those future cash flows back to
**present value** using the discounting mechanism from `[05.04]`, plus a
**Terminal Value** representing the business's value beyond the explicit
forecast period. The **discount rate** used is typically the **Weighted
Average Cost of Capital (WACC)** — a blended rate reflecting the cost of
both the company's debt and equity financing (`[07.07]`), weighted by
their relative proportions in the capital structure, with the cost of debt
applied on an **after-tax basis** (Cost of Debt × (1 − Tax Rate)) since
interest expense is tax-deductible — this generally makes debt financing
appear cheaper within the blended WACC than its stated interest rate alone
would suggest.

### Intuition
DCF directly applies `[05.04]`'s core insight — a rupee/dollar received in
the future is worth less than a rupee/dollar today — to value an entire
business as the sum of all its future cash-generating capacity, discounted
back to today's value. This is considered the most theoretically "pure"
valuation approach because it doesn't rely on comparison to other
companies (`[09.02]`'s relative valuation) — it estimates value directly
from the business's own expected cash generation.

### Mental Model
```
DCF FORMULA (simplified):

Intrinsic Value = Sum of [Free Cash Flow(year t) / (1 + WACC)^t]
                   for each forecast year t
                   + Terminal Value / (1 + WACC)^n

  FCF projections:    Estimated future Free Cash Flow
                       (typically 5-10 year explicit forecast)

  WACC:                Blended cost of capital (discount rate)
                        reflecting debt + equity financing costs

  Terminal Value:       Value of all cash flows BEYOND the
                        explicit forecast period (often the
                        LARGEST single component of total value)
```

### Visual Explanation
Terminal value frequently represents the majority of a DCF's total
estimated value, making the assumptions behind it (long-term growth rate,
terminal WACC) disproportionately influential on the final result.

### ASCII Diagram
```
   DCF VALUE COMPOSITION (illustrative, typical pattern)

   Explicit forecast period cash flows (Years 1-10):  ~30-40%
   of total value
        |##########|

   Terminal Value (all cash flows beyond Year 10):     ~60-70%
   of total value
        |####################|

   IMPLICATION: small changes in terminal growth rate or
   terminal WACC assumptions can swing the ENTIRE valuation
   dramatically, since terminal value dominates the total
```

### Flowchart
```
[Project Free Cash Flow for an explicit forecast period
 (typically 5-10 years), using revenue growth, margin, and
 CapEx assumptions]
        |
        v
[Estimate WACC: blended cost of debt and equity capital]
        |
        v
[Discount each year's projected FCF back to present value
 using WACC]
        |
        v
[Estimate Terminal Value: value of all cash flows beyond the
 explicit forecast period, using either a perpetuity growth
 rate or an exit multiple method]
        |
        v
[Discount Terminal Value back to present value; SUM all
 discounted cash flows + discounted Terminal Value = Intrinsic
 Value]
```

## Real World Analogy
A DCF valuation is like deciding how much to pay today for a rental
property based purely on your estimate of all the future rental income
it will generate over its lifetime, discounted for the fact that rent
received 20 years from now is worth much less to you today than rent
received next year — you're not comparing the property's price to similar
properties (relative valuation); you're calculating value directly from
its own expected future cash generation, adjusted for the time value of
money.

## Indian Market Example
Indian equity research analysts building DCF models for companies like
**HDFC Bank** or **TCS** must carefully estimate India-specific inputs —
an appropriate WACC reflecting India's risk-free rate environment
(influenced by RBI policy, `[06.02]`) and equity risk premium, alongside
realistic long-term growth assumptions reflecting India's broader
macroeconomic growth trajectory (`[05.05]`, `[06.10]`) — illustrating how
this module's valuation framework directly incorporates the macro
literacy built across Modules 05-06.

## US Market Example
DCF models for mature, cash-generative US companies (like large,
established consumer staples or industrial companies with predictable
cash flows) tend to be more reliable and less sensitive to terminal value
assumptions than DCF models for high-growth, less predictable companies
(like early-stage technology companies), since a larger share of the
mature company's value sits in the more confidently-forecastable explicit
period rather than the highly uncertain terminal value.

## Historical Example
Analysts attempting to build DCF valuations for **dot-com era** companies
(`[08.06]`, `[09.01]`, `[09.02]`) with no current profits and highly
speculative future growth assumptions faced enormous terminal value
sensitivity — small changes in assumed long-term growth rates produced
wildly different valuations, a historical illustration of DCF's genuine
limitation when applied to businesses with highly uncertain long-term
cash flow trajectories, reinforcing why DCF works best for more
predictable, mature businesses.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional valuation analysts almost always build DCF models with
**sensitivity tables** — explicitly showing how the resulting valuation
changes across a range of different WACC and terminal growth rate
assumptions — rather than presenting a single point estimate, directly
acknowledging and communicating the model's inherent input sensitivity
rather than presenting false precision.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors attempting DCF analysis for the first time often
underestimate how dramatically small changes in growth or discount rate
assumptions can swing the final valuation — building the habit of testing
multiple reasonable assumption scenarios (rather than relying on a single
set of inputs) meaningfully improves the analytical honesty and usefulness
of a DIY DCF exercise.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts emphasize that DCF's greatest value often lies not
in producing a single "correct" number, but in **forcing explicit,
examinable assumptions** about growth, margins, and risk — the discipline
of building a DCF model makes an analyst's implicit assumptions visible
and challengeable, which is itself valuable regardless of how precisely
the final number matches reality.

## Common Mistakes
- Presenting a single DCF output as a precise, "correct" valuation without
  acknowledging the wide range produced by reasonable alternative
  assumptions.
- Underestimating terminal value's outsized influence on the total
  valuation result.
- Applying DCF to highly unpredictable, early-stage businesses where the
  underlying cash flow forecasting is little more than speculation.

## Misconceptions
- **"DCF produces an objective, precise 'true' value."** As the
  sensitivity table practice and dot-com era examples illustrate, DCF
  outputs are highly sensitive to input assumptions and should be treated
  as a reasoned range, not a precise, objective figure.
- **"Terminal value is a minor, secondary component of a DCF.**" It
  frequently represents the majority of total estimated value — small
  changes to terminal assumptions can dramatically swing the entire
  valuation.
- **"DCF works equally well for any company, regardless of predictability
  of future cash flows."** DCF is most reliable for mature, predictable
  businesses; it becomes increasingly speculative and less reliable for
  early-stage, highly uncertain businesses.

## Key Takeaways
- DCF estimates intrinsic value by discounting projected future Free Cash
  Flow (and Terminal Value) back to present value using WACC.
- Terminal value typically represents the majority of total DCF value,
  making its assumptions disproportionately influential.
- DCF works best for mature, predictable businesses and becomes
  increasingly unreliable for highly speculative, early-stage companies.
- Professional practice uses sensitivity tables to communicate the range
  of outcomes under different reasonable assumptions, not a single point
  estimate.

## Summary
The Discounted Cash Flow model — projecting future Free Cash Flow,
discounting it using WACC, and adding a Terminal Value — represents the
most theoretically direct valuation approach, estimating intrinsic value
from a business's own expected cash generation rather than peer
comparison, while carrying genuine, significant sensitivity to input
assumptions (especially terminal value) that professional practice
addresses through sensitivity analysis rather than false precision. This
sets up the next lesson's related, dividend-specific valuation approach —
the Dividend Discount Model.

## Quiz

   b) Intrinsic value from projected future Free Cash Flow, discounted to present value

   a) It's always a minor, negligible component
   b) It typically represents the majority of total estimated value
   c) It's never calculated in practice
   d) It only matters for bonds

4. Why do professional analysts use sensitivity tables with DCF models?
   a) To present a single, precise, objective number
   b) To communicate the range of outcomes under different reasonable assumptions
   c) Sensitivity tables are never used in practice
   d) To avoid calculating WACC

5. True or False: DCF works equally well for highly speculative,
   early-stage companies as it does for mature, predictable businesses.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — DCF estimates intrinsic value from projected, discounted future Free Cash Flow.
2. **b)** — WACC is the Weighted Average Cost of Capital, used as the DCF discount rate.
3. **b)** — Terminal Value typically represents the majority of total DCF value.
4. **b)** — Sensitivity tables communicate the range of outcomes under different assumptions, avoiding false precision.
5. **b) False** — DCF is most reliable for mature, predictable businesses and becomes increasingly speculative for early-stage companies.
