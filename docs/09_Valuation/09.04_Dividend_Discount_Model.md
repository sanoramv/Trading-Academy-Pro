## Core Concepts

### Definition
The **Dividend Discount Model (DDM)** estimates a stock's intrinsic value
as the present value of all its expected future dividend payments,
applying the same discounting logic as DCF (`[09.03]`) but specifically to
dividends rather than total free cash flow. The most commonly used
simplified version is the **Gordon Growth Model**:
**Value = Next Year's Expected Dividend / (Cost of Equity − Dividend
Growth Rate)**, which assumes dividends grow at a constant rate forever —
a significant simplifying assumption best suited to mature, stable
dividend-paying companies.

### Intuition
DDM rests on the premise that a stock's ultimate value to a long-term
holder comes specifically from the cash dividends it actually pays out
over time (as opposed to DCF's broader view of all cash generated by the
business, whether distributed or reinvested). This makes DDM
conceptually cleanest for companies with a long, stable, and predictable
history of dividend payments — and considerably less useful for companies
that pay no dividends or have unpredictable payout policies, where DCF
(`[09.03]`) is the more appropriate tool.

### Mental Model
```
GORDON GROWTH MODEL:

Value = D1 / (r - g)

  D1 = Expected dividend NEXT year
  r  = Cost of equity (required return, similar concept to
       WACC but specifically for equity holders)
  g  = Constant expected long-term dividend growth rate

  KEY CONSTRAINT: g must be LESS than r, or the formula
  produces a nonsensical (negative or infinite) result --
  a company literally cannot grow its dividend faster than
  its cost of equity forever.
```

### Visual Explanation
DDM is most reliable for mature companies with a long, stable dividend
history and becomes increasingly unreliable (or simply inapplicable) for
companies with volatile, absent, or unpredictable dividend policies.

### Worked Example
**Hypothetical Example.** Plugging real numbers into the Gordon Growth
Model formula from the Mental Model above:

```
ASSUMPTIONS
  D1 (next year's expected dividend):    Rs 20 per share
  r  (cost of equity):                   12%
  g  (constant long-term dividend
      growth rate):                       6%

CALCULATION
  Value = D1 / (r - g)
        = 20 / (0.12 - 0.06)
        = 20 / 0.06
        = Rs 333.33 per share
```

If the current market price of this stock is, say, Rs 280, the Gordon
Growth Model suggests it may be undervalued relative to this dividend-based
estimate — though as with any single-model output, this should be treated
as one data point among several (`[09.02]`'s relative valuation,
`[09.03]`'s DCF), not a precise, standalone verdict.

**Why the constraint matters, concretely:** if the same company's assumed
growth rate crept up to `g = 11%` (still less than `r = 12%`, but only
just), the formula becomes `20 / (0.12 - 0.11) = 20 / 0.01 = Rs 2,000` per
share — a six-fold jump in estimated value from a 5-percentage-point
change in one assumption. And if `g` reached or exceeded `r = 12%`
entirely, the denominator would hit zero or go negative, producing an
infinite or nonsensical result. This is exactly why the Mental Model above
flags `g < r` as a hard constraint, not a minor technical footnote — small
changes in the growth assumption swing the output dramatically as `g`
approaches `r`.

## Real World Analogy
The Dividend Discount Model is like valuing a rental property purely based
on the actual rent checks you expect to receive over time, ignoring any
value from the property's potential appreciation or the landlord's
decision to reinvest rental income into renovations rather than
distributing it — a reasonable approach if you're specifically interested
in cash income, but incomplete if the landlord is deliberately withholding
distributions to reinvest in ways that build genuine, if undistributed,
value.

## Indian Market Example
Indian **public sector undertakings (PSUs)** and certain mature private
companies with long-standing, stable dividend payout policies (such as
some established FMCG or utility companies) are commonly analyzed using
DDM-style approaches by income-focused Indian investors specifically
interested in dividend yield as a primary investment consideration,
distinct from the growth-focused, DCF-oriented analysis more common for
high-growth Indian sectors like technology or new-age consumer internet
companies.

## US Market Example
US **"Dividend Aristocrat"** companies — those with a long history
(typically 25+ consecutive years) of increasing dividend payments — are
frequently analyzed using DDM-style approaches, given their unusually
predictable, stable dividend growth track records that make the Gordon
Growth Model's constant-growth assumption considerably more reasonable
than it would be for a typical company with a shorter or less consistent
dividend history.

## Historical Example
**Myron Gordon's** development of the Gordon Growth Model in the late
1950s/early 1960s built on earlier dividend valuation concepts to create
the widely-taught simplified formula still used today — a historical
example of academic financial theory becoming a genuinely practical,
enduring tool still directly applied by analysts over six decades later,
illustrating the model's conceptual staying power despite its
simplifying assumptions.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional income-focused funds (dividend-focused equity funds,
insurance company portfolios prioritizing stable income) rely
substantially on DDM-style frameworks as a core part of their security
selection and valuation process, given their specific mandate emphasis on
sustainable, growing dividend income rather than total return alone.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors focused on building dividend income (a common retirement-
oriented investing strategy) benefit directly from understanding DDM's
logic, since it directly formalizes the intuitive "how much is this
dividend stream worth to me" question underlying dividend-focused
investing strategies.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts often use **multi-stage DDM variants** — rather than
assuming a single constant growth rate forever (the simplified Gordon
Growth Model), a multi-stage model might assume a higher growth rate for
an initial period (e.g., 5-10 years) before transitioning to a lower,
more sustainable long-term growth rate — a more realistic, if more
complex, refinement of the basic model covered in this lesson.

## Common Mistakes
- Applying DDM to companies with no or minimal dividend history, where DCF
  would be far more appropriate.
- Using an assumed growth rate (g) that exceeds or approaches the cost of
  equity (r), producing an unreliable or nonsensical result.
- Assuming a single constant growth rate is realistic indefinitely, rather
  than considering multi-stage growth assumptions for companies whose
  growth is likely to slow over time.

## Misconceptions
- **"DDM and DCF are interchangeable and always produce similar
  results."** They can diverge significantly for companies that retain
  substantial cash rather than distributing it as dividends — DCF values
  all cash generation, while DDM only values what's actually paid out.
- **"Every company should be valued using DDM since dividends are what
  investors ultimately receive."** Many high-growth companies deliberately
  retain and reinvest cash rather than paying dividends, precisely because
  reinvestment is expected to create more value than distribution — DDM
  would significantly undervalue such companies by ignoring this
  reinvested value.
- **"The Gordon Growth Model's constant growth assumption is realistic
  for any company."** It's a meaningful simplification best suited to
  genuinely mature, stable companies — multi-stage variants are more
  appropriate for companies with changing growth trajectories.

## Key Takeaways
- DDM estimates intrinsic value as the present value of expected future
  dividends, using the Gordon Growth Model as its simplified core formula.
- DDM is most appropriate for mature, stable dividend-paying companies;
  DCF is more appropriate for companies with minimal or unpredictable
  dividends.
- The assumed growth rate must be realistically less than the cost of
  equity, or the model produces unreliable results.
- Multi-stage DDM variants offer more realistic refinements for companies
  with changing growth trajectories over time.

## Summary
The Dividend Discount Model — valuing a stock as the present value of its
expected future dividend stream, via the Gordon Growth Model's simplified
formula — provides a conceptually clean, historically enduring valuation
tool specifically suited to mature, stable dividend-paying companies like
US Dividend Aristocrats or established Indian PSUs, while remaining
considerably less useful for high-growth companies that retain rather
than distribute cash, where DCF (`[09.03]`) is the more appropriate tool.
This completes the module's two core intrinsic valuation methodologies,
setting up the next lesson's focus on the distinct analytical approaches
required for growth versus value stocks.

## Quiz
1. What does the Dividend Discount Model estimate?
   a) A company's total revenue
   b) A stock's intrinsic value as the present value of expected future dividends
   c) A company's tax liability
   d) Currency exchange rates

2. What is the Gordon Growth Model formula?
   a) Revenue minus Expenses
   b) D1 / (r − g)
   c) Assets minus Liabilities
   d) Price divided by EPS

3. When is DDM most appropriate to use?
   a) For companies with no dividend history at all
   b) For mature, stable companies with a long, predictable dividend history
   c) Only for technology startups
   d) Never appropriate in any context

4. What happens if the assumed growth rate (g) equals or exceeds the cost
   of equity (r) in the Gordon Growth Model?
   a) The formula still works perfectly
   b) The formula produces a nonsensical (negative or infinite) result
   c) Nothing changes
   d) It only affects DCF, not DDM

5. True or False: DDM and DCF always produce identical valuation results
   for any company.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — DDM estimates intrinsic value as the present value of expected future dividends.
2. **b)** — The Gordon Growth Model formula is D1 / (r − g).
3. **b)** — DDM is most appropriate for mature, stable dividend-paying companies.
4. **b)** — A growth rate equal to or exceeding the cost of equity produces a nonsensical result.
5. **b) False** — DDM and DCF can diverge significantly for companies that retain rather than distribute cash.
