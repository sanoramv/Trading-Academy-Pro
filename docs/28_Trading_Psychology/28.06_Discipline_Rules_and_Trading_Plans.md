## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **trading plan** is a comprehensive, written document specifying
every aspect of a trader's approach — which strategies to trade (Module
24), entry/exit criteria, risk management rules (`[25.08]`'s rulebook,
incorporated directly), position sizing formulas (Module 26), and
specific psychological circuit breakers (this module) for managing
tilt, FOMO, and cognitive biases. **Discipline**, in this context, is
the practiced ability to consistently execute a written plan despite
the psychological pressures `[28.02]`-`[28.05]` described — treated
throughout professional trading education as a trainable skill, not a
fixed personality trait some traders simply "have" and others don't.

### Intuition
Every bias and pattern this module has covered — fear/greed
(`[28.02]`), confirmation/recency/loss aversion (`[28.03]`), tilt/
revenge trading (`[28.04]`), FOMO (`[28.05]`) — shares a common
vulnerability: they operate most powerfully in the absence of a
specific, pre-committed rule to fall back on. A written trading plan,
consulted and followed *before* emotional pressure arises, removes the
need to make sound judgment calls under exactly the conditions where
judgment is least reliable — directly closing `[28.01]`'s knowing-doing gap.

## Real World Analogy
A trading plan is like a ship captain's complete standing orders
(`[25.08]`'s analogy, now extended to cover the full voyage, not just
storm conditions) — covering not just what to do during a crisis, but
the entire operating procedure for the whole journey, written and
agreed upon calmly in port before departure, so the crew has a
consistent, trusted reference regardless of how calm or chaotic conditions become underway.

## Historical Example
The written trading plan as a formal discipline draws directly from the
combined body of work referenced throughout this academy — Alexander
Elder's structured trading records, Van Tharp's position sizing and
expectancy frameworks, and Mark Douglas's psychological discipline
writing — all converging, from the 1990s-2000s onward, on the same
core recommendation: a complete, written plan consulted consistently,
rather than relying on memory, intuition, or in-the-moment judgment alone.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional trading operations formalize this exact structure at
scale — written strategy mandates, risk policies, position limits, and
even formal psychological/behavioral training programs for traders —
essentially building the same complete trading plan this lesson
describes, but with organizational enforcement mechanisms individual
retail traders must instead self-impose through personal discipline.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders who have worked through this entire academy now possess
every component needed for a complete trading plan — the discipline
challenge, per `[28.01]`'s knowing-doing gap, is actually writing it
down, consulting it consistently, and resisting the pull to deviate
under the specific pressures `[28.02]`-`[28.05]` described.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders often describe their trading plan as a living
document that becomes genuinely valuable only through consistent use
over time — the plan's benefit compounds as a trader builds the habit
of consulting it before every decision, rather than treating it as a
one-time document written once and then set aside.

## Common Mistakes
- Writing a trading plan once and never actually consulting it during
  live trading.
- Writing vague, general rules ("manage risk well," "don't overtrade")
  rather than specific, checkable criteria.
- Treating discipline as a fixed trait ("I'm just not a disciplined
  person") rather than a practiced, trainable skill.

## Misconceptions
- **"Having a trading plan in my head is sufficient — I don't need to
  write it down."** Written, pre-committed rules meaningfully improve
  adherence under live pressure compared to relying on memory alone
  (`[25.08]`'s and this lesson's shared core principle).
- **"Discipline is an innate trait some traders have and others simply
  lack."** Professional trading education consistently treats
  discipline as a practiced, trainable skill, built through consistent,
  deliberate use of written rules over time.
- **"A trading plan only needs to cover strategy and risk, not
  psychology."** This module has shown that psychological factors are
  frequently the actual cause of deviation from otherwise sound
  strategy/risk rules — a complete plan must address both.

## Key Takeaways
- A complete trading plan integrates strategy (Module 24), risk
  management (`[25.08]`), position sizing (Module 26), and specific
  psychological circuit breakers (this module) into one written document.
- Written, pre-committed rules, consulted consistently before every
  trade, meaningfully improve adherence compared to in-the-moment judgment.
- Discipline is a practiced, trainable skill — not a fixed personality trait.
- The plan should be treated as a living document, reviewed and revised
  based on journaled evidence (`[24.12]`), not written once and abandoned.
