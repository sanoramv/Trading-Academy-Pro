## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Confirmation bias** is the tendency to seek out, notice, and
remember information that confirms an existing belief or position,
while discounting or ignoring contradicting information — a trader
holding a long position may disproportionately notice bullish news and
dismiss bearish signals. **Recency bias** is the tendency to weight
recent events more heavily than earlier ones when assessing patterns or
probabilities — a trader may judge a strategy's viability primarily by
its last few trades rather than its full journaled sample (`[24.12]`).
**Loss aversion**, formally documented by Daniel Kahneman and Amos
Tversky's Prospect Theory (1979), is the well-established tendency for
losses to be felt psychologically more intensely than equivalent gains
are felt positively — commonly estimated at roughly twice the emotional
weight, though the precise ratio varies by study and context.

### Intuition
Each bias distorts a specific part of the trading decision process:
confirmation bias distorts *information gathering* (only seeing what
supports the existing view), recency bias distorts *pattern
recognition* (overweighting the most recent, most emotionally salient
data), and loss aversion distorts *risk-taking behavior itself*
(making losses feel disproportionately painful, which can drive both
excessive caution after wins and excessive risk-taking to avoid
realizing a loss).

### Mental Model
```
THREE BIASES, THREE DISTINCT DISTORTIONS:

  CONFIRMATION BIAS distorts: INFORMATION GATHERING
    "I hold a long position, so I notice bullish news and
     dismiss bearish news, even when both are equally valid"

  RECENCY BIAS distorts: PATTERN RECOGNITION
    "My last 3 trades lost, so I believe my strategy 'stopped
     working' -- ignoring the FULL 200-trade journaled sample, [24.12]"

  LOSS AVERSION distorts: RISK-TAKING BEHAVIOR
    "A loss FEELS roughly TWICE as painful as an equivalent
     gain feels pleasurable" (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979)
    -> drives HOLDING losing positions too long (avoiding
       REALIZING the loss) and SELLING winning positions too
       early (locking in the pleasurable gain before it can reverse)
```

### Visual Explanation
Prospect Theory's classic value function graph shows a curve that is
steeper for losses than for equivalent-sized gains — visually
demonstrating loss aversion's asymmetry: the psychological "pain" from
a ₹10,000 loss registers more intensely than the psychological
"pleasure" from an equivalent ₹10,000 gain.

## Real World Analogy
These three biases are like a photographer who only takes pictures that
match a preconceived idea of the scene (confirmation bias), judges the
entire day's weather based only on the last five minutes (recency
bias), and feels far more upset about one blurry photo than pleased
about five sharp ones (loss aversion) — each bias distorts a different
part of how the photographer perceives and reacts to the same underlying reality.

## Indian Market Example
A trader holding a long position in an Indian IT stock ahead of
quarterly earnings actively seeks out bullish analyst commentary while
scrolling past bearish notes (confirmation bias); after three losing
trades on a Nifty options strategy, concludes the strategy "doesn't
work" despite a 150-trade journaled sample showing solid long-run
expectancy (recency bias); and holds a losing position well past its
planned stop-loss specifically to avoid realizing the loss, while having
exited a separate winning position early out of anxiety about giving
back the gain (loss aversion). **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## US Market Example
A trader who bought a stock based on a specific thesis continues
reading only news sources and analyst reports confirming that thesis
even as contradicting signals emerge (confirmation bias); judges a
systematic strategy's viability primarily by its performance over the
last month rather than its full multi-year backtest (recency bias); and
sells a winning position immediately after a small gain out of fear of
losing it, while holding a losing position far past its planned exit
point (loss aversion). **Hypothetical Example** for illustration only.

## Historical Example
Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's 1979 paper "Prospect Theory: An
Analysis of Decision under Risk," published in Econometrica, formally
established loss aversion and became one of the most influential papers
in behavioral economics, directly contributing to Kahneman's 2002 Nobel
Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences (Tversky, who died in 1996, was
ineligible posthumously) — the foundational work underlying this
lesson's loss aversion discussion and much of modern behavioral finance.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional trading desks build systematic checks specifically
designed to counter these biases at scale — mandatory disconfirming-
evidence reviews before major position changes (countering confirmation
bias), performance evaluation over full, multi-year track records
rather than recent months alone (countering recency bias), and
automated stop-loss/take-profit execution removing discretionary,
loss-averse decision-making in the moment.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders lack these institutional structural safeguards and must
build personal awareness and countermeasures manually — explicitly
seeking disconfirming evidence, reviewing full journaled samples
(`[24.12]`) rather than recent trades alone, and pre-committing to exit
rules (`[25.08]`) specifically to remove in-the-moment, loss-averse discretion.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders and coaches often recommend specific, concrete
countermeasures for each bias: for confirmation bias, deliberately
seeking out the strongest counterargument to a current position; for
recency bias, reviewing full rolling-window statistics rather than
memory alone; for loss aversion, using automated or pre-committed exit
orders rather than manual, in-the-moment exit decisions.

## Common Mistakes
- Seeking only news/analysis that confirms an existing position, while
  actively avoiding or dismissing disconfirming information.
- Judging a strategy's viability by its last few trades rather than a
  statistically meaningful, full journaled sample (`[24.12]`).
- Holding losing positions well past a planned stop specifically to
  avoid realizing the loss, while exiting winning positions prematurely.

## Misconceptions
- **"These biases only affect inexperienced or unsophisticated
  traders."** They are well-documented, broadly human cognitive
  patterns (Kahneman & Tversky's Nobel-winning research) affecting even
  highly experienced, sophisticated decision-makers.
- **"Being aware of a bias is sufficient to eliminate its effect."**
  As `[28.01]`'s knowing-doing gap established, awareness alone
  frequently doesn't prevent the bias's influence without concrete,
  practical countermeasures.
- **"Loss aversion means avoiding all losses is possible or desirable."**
  Losses are a normal, expected part of any trading strategy
  (`[24.01]`) — loss aversion describes a psychological distortion in
  how losses are *felt*, not a reason losses can or should be entirely avoided.

## Key Takeaways
- Confirmation bias distorts information gathering; recency bias
  distorts pattern recognition; loss aversion distorts risk-taking behavior.
- Loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979 Prospect Theory) shows losses
  are felt roughly twice as intensely as equivalent gains — a
  foundational finding in behavioral economics.
- Each bias has specific, practical countermeasures: seeking
  disconfirming evidence, reviewing full journaled samples, and using
  pre-committed exit rules.
- These biases affect experienced and inexperienced traders alike — not
  solely a beginner's problem.

## Summary
Confirmation bias, recency bias, and loss aversion each distort a
different part of the trading decision process — information gathering,
pattern recognition, and risk-taking behavior respectively — with loss
aversion's asymmetric psychological weighting of losses versus gains
formally established by Kahneman and Tversky's Nobel-winning 1979
Prospect Theory research. The next lesson turns to revenge trading and
tilt — a specific, often severe behavioral pattern that emerges when
loss aversion and the fear/greed cycle (`[28.02]`) combine after a
painful loss.

## Quiz
1. What does confirmation bias distort?
   a) Risk-taking behavior  b) Information gathering  c) Order execution speed  d) Margin requirements

2. What does recency bias cause a trader to overweight?
   a) The full historical record  b) Recent events over the full journaled sample  c) Fundamental data only  d) Nothing specific

3. What does loss aversion describe?
   a) Avoiding all risk  b) Losses being felt more intensely than equivalent gains  c) A stop-loss method  d) A margin call

4. Who formally established loss aversion in a landmark 1979 paper?
   a) Markowitz and Sharpe  b) Kahneman and Tversky  c) Van Tharp  d) Kelly and Thorp

5. True or False: Awareness of a cognitive bias is generally sufficient to eliminate its effect on decision-making.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Information gathering.
2. **b)** — Recent events over the full journaled sample.
3. **b)** — Losses being felt more intensely than equivalent gains, per Prospect Theory.
4. **b)** — Kahneman and Tversky, in their 1979 Prospect Theory paper.
5. **b) False** — Awareness alone frequently doesn't prevent a bias's influence without concrete countermeasures.
