## Core Concepts

### Definition
An **asset class** is a group of financial instruments that share similar
characteristics — how they generate return, how they behave in the economy,
and how they're regulated and traded. The five major asset classes covered
in this academy are:

1. **Equity** — ownership claims in a business (stocks/shares).
2. **Debt/Fixed Income** — loans made to a borrower in exchange for interest (bonds).
3. **Currency (FX)** — claims on one country's money relative to another's.
4. **Commodities** — physical goods (gold, oil, agricultural products) or claims on them.
5. **Derivatives** — contracts whose value is *derived* from another asset (futures, options).

### Intuition
Each asset class exists because different participants in the economy have
different needs. A company that wants permanent capital without repayment
obligations issues **equity**. A company or government that wants capital
but is willing to pay it back with interest issues **debt**. Anyone doing
international trade needs to exchange **currency**. Producers and consumers
of physical goods need to manage price risk in **commodities**. And anyone
needing to hedge or speculate on a future price without owning the
underlying asset today uses **derivatives**.

### Mental Model
Think of asset classes as different "flavors" of risk and return, sitting on
a rough spectrum:

```
LOWER RISK/RETURN                                          HIGHER RISK/RETURN
        |                                                              |
Government    Corporate    Currency    Commodities    Equity    Derivatives
  Bonds         Bonds        (FX)                                 (Leveraged)
```

This is a rough generalization — a highly leveraged derivative on a
government bond can be riskier than an unleveraged equity position — but it
is a useful starting mental map before nuance is added in later modules.

### Visual Explanation
Each asset class can be understood by asking: *what is the claim, who
issues it, and how does the holder earn a return?*

```

        |

        +--> To hedge/speculate on a future price without owning it today --> DERIVATIVES
```

## Real World Analogy
Think of a business as a house being built.
- **Equity** investors are like co-owners of the house — they get a share of
  its value (and any rental income) but are paid last if the house is sold
  in a distressed sale, and their upside is unlimited if the house
  appreciates.
- **Debt** investors are like the bank that gave a mortgage — they get fixed
  interest payments regardless of how much the house appreciates, but they
  get paid *before* the owners if the house is sold in distress.
- **Currency** is like the exchange rate you'd need if the house were being
  bought by a foreign buyer — it affects the deal even though it's not the
  house itself.
- **Commodities** are like the bricks, cement, and steel that went into
  building the house — physical inputs with their own supply/demand-driven
  prices.
- **Derivatives** are like a contract to buy the house at today's price
  six months from now, without owning it yet — its value moves with the
  house's price but you never had to buy the house itself.

## Indian Market Example
- **Equity:** A share of **Infosys** traded on the NSE.
- **Debt:** A **Government of India bond (G-Sec)** or a corporate bond
  issued by an NBFC, traded over-the-counter or on exchange platforms like
  NSE's debt segment.
- **Currency:** The **USD/INR** exchange rate, which determines how many
  rupees one US dollar buys — critical for Indian importers, exporters, and
  IT companies earning dollar revenue.
- **Commodities:** **Gold** traded on MCX (Multi Commodity Exchange), deeply
  culturally significant in India as both an investment and a store of
  wealth.
- **Derivatives:** A **Nifty 50 futures contract**, which derives its value
  from the Nifty 50 index without requiring ownership of all 50 underlying
  stocks.

## US Market Example
- **Equity:** A share of **Microsoft** traded on NASDAQ.
- **Debt:** A **US Treasury bond**, considered one of the safest debt
  instruments in the world, or a corporate bond issued by a company like
  Apple.
- **Currency:** The **US Dollar Index (DXY)**, which measures the dollar's
  value against a basket of major currencies.
- **Commodities:** **Crude oil (WTI)** traded on the NYMEX, a benchmark for
  global energy prices.
- **Derivatives:** An **S&P 500 futures contract (E-mini)**, widely used by
  institutions to hedge or gain exposure to the broad US equity market.

## Historical Example
The **2013 "Taper Tantrum"** illustrates how these asset classes interact.
When the US Federal Reserve signaled in May 2013 that it might slow
("taper") its bond-buying program, US bond yields rose sharply (debt
market), the US dollar strengthened broadly (currency market), emerging
market currencies including the Indian rupee fell sharply (the INR dropped
from around ₹55–56/USD to over ₹68/USD by August 2013), Indian equities sold
off (equity market), and Indian policymakers had to raise interest rates
defensively — a single macro announcement rippled through every asset class
simultaneously. This is why later modules (05, 06) teach macroeconomics
before deep-diving into any single asset class: they don't move in
isolation.

## Institutional Perspective
Large institutions build portfolios *across* asset classes deliberately —
this is called **asset allocation** (covered in depth in Module 27,
Portfolio Management). A pension fund might hold a mix of equities (for
growth), bonds (for stability and income), commodities (for inflation
protection), and use derivatives (for hedging), because each asset class
tends to behave differently in different economic environments, smoothing
overall portfolio returns.

## Retail Perspective
Retail investors, especially beginners, often concentrate entirely in one
asset class — usually equities — without understanding how debt, currency,
and commodity markets are silently influencing the stock prices they're
watching. A retail trader who doesn't understand that rising bond yields
often pressure equity valuations (Module 06, 09) will be confused by moves
that professionals see coming from the debt market.

## Professional Insights
Professional macro traders often say "everything is connected through
interest rates and currency." A change in central bank policy (Module 06)
ripples through bonds, then currencies, then equities, then commodities —
usually in that rough order, though not always. Understanding the *sequence*
and *interconnection* of asset classes is a hallmark of institutional-level
market thinking, versus retail-level thinking that treats each asset class
as an isolated silo.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating equities as the only "real" market and ignoring bonds, currencies,
  and commodities — even though bond and currency markets are, by notional
  value, far larger than equity markets globally.
- Not realizing that derivatives aren't a separate "asset" so much as a tool
  built on top of the other four asset classes.
- Assuming all asset classes move independently, missing the macro linkages
  (interest rates, currency, inflation) that connect them.

## Misconceptions
- **"Commodities are only for professional traders."** Retail investors in
  India routinely hold gold (physically or via Gold ETFs/Sovereign Gold
  Bonds) as a commodity investment without realizing they're participating
  in the commodities asset class.
- **"Derivatives are always extremely risky."** Derivatives are a tool — a
  Nifty futures contract can be used to *reduce* risk (hedging) just as
  easily as to *increase* it (speculation with leverage); the risk profile
  depends on how the tool is used, covered fully in Modules 20–21.
- **"Currency markets don't affect me if I only trade Indian stocks."** A
  falling rupee affects the input costs and profitability of Indian
  companies that import raw materials, and affects the earnings (in INR
  terms) of Indian IT companies that earn in USD — currency moves are never
  fully isolated from equity prices.

## Key Takeaways
- The five major asset classes are Equity, Debt, Currency, Commodities, and
  Derivatives — each solving a different economic need.
- Each has a distinct claim structure (what you own) and return mechanism
  (how you earn).
- Asset classes are interconnected through macroeconomic forces, especially
  interest rates and currency movements.
- Institutions deliberately allocate across asset classes; retail investors
  often accidentally concentrate in just one.

## Summary
An asset class is a category of financial instrument defined by what claim
it represents and how it generates a return. Equity, debt, currency,
commodities, and derivatives each meet a different economic need, carry
different risk/return characteristics, and are linked to each other through
macroeconomic forces like interest rates and currency movements. This
academy will devote entire modules to each (Modules 07-09 for equity/
fundamentals, 20 for futures, 21-23 for options/derivatives), but
understanding how they fit together as one system starts here.

## Quiz
1. Which asset class represents fractional ownership in a company?
   a) Debt  b) Equity  c) Currency  d) Commodities

2. A Government of India bond belongs to which asset class?
   a) Equity  b) Debt  c) Derivatives  d) Currency

3. What happened to the Indian rupee during the 2013 Taper Tantrum?
   a) It strengthened significantly
   b) It weakened sharply, from roughly ₹55–56/USD to over ₹68/USD
   c) It remained completely unchanged
   d) India stopped using the rupee temporarily

4. Which statement about derivatives is most accurate?
   a) They are always used for pure speculation
   b) They can be used for hedging or speculation depending on the user's intent
   c) They are a sixth, entirely separate asset class unrelated to the other four
   d) They are illegal in India

5. Why do professional macro traders emphasize interest rates when analyzing
   asset classes?
   a) Interest rates only affect bonds
   b) Interest rate changes ripple through bonds, currencies, equities, and commodities in sequence
   c) Interest rates are set by individual companies
   d) Interest rates have no effect on currencies

## Answers
1. **b)** — Equity represents fractional company ownership.
2. **b)** — A government bond is a debt instrument (a loan to the government).
3. **b)** — The INR fell sharply as capital flowed out of emerging markets toward the strengthening dollar.
4. **b)** — Derivatives are tools; their risk profile depends on whether they're used to hedge or speculate.
5. **b)** — Interest rate changes are a central transmission mechanism connecting all major asset classes.
