## Core Concepts

### Definition
An **order** is an instruction sent to the exchange (via a broker) to buy or
sell a security under specific conditions. The **order type** determines how
strictly the price is controlled versus how strongly execution is
guaranteed:

- **Market Order** — execute immediately at the best available current
  price. Guarantees execution, not price.
- **Limit Order** — execute only at a specified price or better. Guarantees
  price, not execution (it may never fill if the market doesn't reach your
  price).
- **Stop-Loss Order (SL)** — becomes a limit order once a trigger price is
  hit, used to cap losses or protect gains.
- **Stop-Loss Market Order (SL-M)** — becomes a market order once a trigger
  price is hit, prioritizing execution certainty over price certainty at
  that point.
- **Bracket Order (BO)** — a single order that combines an entry with both a
  target (profit booking) and a stop-loss automatically, common on Indian
  intraday trading platforms.
- **Cover Order (CO)** — a market/limit order automatically paired with a
  compulsory stop-loss order, typically offering higher intraday leverage in
  exchange for mandatory risk definition.

### Intuition
Every order type is really answering one question: **"What matters more to
me right now — getting filled for certain, or getting my exact price?"**
A market order says "I don't care about the exact price, just get me in/out
now." A limit order says "I care about the exact price more than I care
about whether this fills at all." Stop-loss orders exist because most
traders don't want to watch the screen every second — they want the
system to automatically protect them (or lock in gains) once price reaches
a level they've predetermined.

### Mental Model
```
                  PRICE CONTROL <-----------------> EXECUTION CERTAINTY
Limit Order       (You control exact price)          (May never fill)
Market Order      (No price control)                 (Fills almost certainly)
SL Order          (Price control after trigger)       (May not fill if price gaps past your limit)
SL-M Order        (No price control after trigger)    (Fills almost certainly once triggered)
```

### Visual Explanation
Stop-loss orders sit dormant until a **trigger price** is touched, at which
point they convert into either a limit order (SL) or a market order (SL-M).

### ASCII Diagram
```
Example: You bought Reliance at 2,850. You want to limit your loss to 2,820.

  SL ORDER (Stop-Loss Limit):
  Trigger price: 2,820   Limit price: 2,818
  --------------------------------------------------
  Price falls to 2,820 --> order activates as a LIMIT
  sell order at 2,818 --> fills ONLY if buyers are
  available at 2,818 or better. In a fast crash, price
  may skip past 2,818 entirely, leaving your order
  UNFILLED (risk: no execution).

  SL-M ORDER (Stop-Loss Market):
  Trigger price: 2,820
  --------------------------------------------------
  Price falls to 2,820 --> order activates as a MARKET
  sell order --> fills IMMEDIATELY at the best available
  price, even if that's now 2,805 in a fast-moving market
  (risk: price/slippage uncertainty, but guaranteed exit).
```

## Real World Analogy
A **market order** is like walking into a taxi stand and taking the first
available cab regardless of the fare shown — you're guaranteed a ride, but
not a specific price. A **limit order** is like telling a cab company "only
send a cab if the fare is under ₹200" — you might wait a long time, or the
ride might never happen if no cab agrees to that fare. A **stop-loss order**
is like telling a friend "if I don't call you by 9 PM, assume something's
wrong and act" — a safety mechanism that activates automatically once a
condition is met, without you needing to be watching constantly.

## Indian Market Example
On platforms like **Zerodha Kite** or other Indian discount brokers, an
intraday trader buying Nifty futures might use a **Bracket Order (BO)** to
simultaneously set: an entry price, a target price (e.g., 50 points above
entry), and a stop-loss price (e.g., 25 points below entry) — all in one
order ticket. If the target is hit, the position auto-closes with a profit;
if the stop-loss is hit, it auto-closes with a defined, limited loss. This
is a very common tool for retail intraday traders in India specifically
because it enforces discipline (a pre-defined exit on both sides)
automatically.

## US Market Example
On US platforms like **Interactive Brokers** or **Thinkorswim**, traders
frequently use a **Stop-Loss Market order combined with a Take-Profit Limit
order** (sometimes called an OCO — "One Cancels the Other" bracket) around
an Apple or Tesla equity position, so that whichever level is hit first
(the stop-loss or the target) automatically cancels the other order,
preventing an accidental double-execution.

## Historical Example
During the **May 6, 2010 "Flash Crash"** in US markets, the Dow Jones
Industrial Average fell nearly 1,000 points intraday within minutes before
sharply recovering, partly attributed to a large sell algorithm interacting
poorly with high-frequency trading liquidity. Many traders who had placed
**stop-loss market orders** (SL-M equivalent) got filled at extremely poor,
temporarily distorted prices during the plunge, because their orders
guaranteed execution but not price — a real-world illustration of the
exact trade-off this lesson teaches. This event led to the introduction of
circuit breakers and "limit up-limit down" mechanisms in US markets to
reduce such extreme, momentary price dislocations.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutions rarely use simple market orders for large sizes, because a
large market order can move the price significantly against them
(**market impact**, covered further in Module 29). Instead, they use
sophisticated execution algorithms (TWAP — Time-Weighted Average Price,
VWAP — Volume-Weighted Average Price) that break a large order into many
smaller pieces over time to minimize price impact, effectively blending
market and limit order logic programmatically.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders often misuse market orders in illiquid stocks (where the
gap between the best bid and best ask can be wide), resulting in poor fill
prices — a mistake called "paying the spread" unnecessarily. Retail traders
also sometimes set stop-losses too tight relative to a stock's normal
volatility, getting stopped out by routine price noise rather than an
actual trend reversal — a topic developed further in `[25.03]` Stop-Loss
Placement Methodology.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders think about **slippage** — the difference between the
expected execution price and the actual filled price — as a real,
measurable trading cost, especially for market and SL-M orders in fast or
illiquid conditions. They also distinguish between a stop-loss as a **mental
stop** (a price level you've committed to act on manually) versus a
**hard stop** (an actual resting order on the exchange) — each has
trade-offs around execution certainty versus information leakage (a visible
resting stop-loss order can, in theory, be seen and targeted by other
participants in some market structures, a concept explored further in
Module 18, Smart Money Concepts).

## Common Mistakes
- Using a market order in a low-liquidity/small-cap stock, resulting in a
  much worse fill price than expected due to a wide bid-ask spread.
- Setting a stop-loss (SL) limit order instead of an SL-M order in fast-
  moving conditions, risking the order never filling if price gaps past the
  limit price.
- Forgetting that a limit order might never execute at all if the market
  simply never reaches the specified price, leaving the trader unintentionally
  unhedged or out of a position they meant to have.

## Misconceptions
- **"A stop-loss order guarantees I'll lose exactly the amount I planned."**
  Only true for SL-M orders in normal conditions — during gaps or fast
  moves (like the 2010 Flash Crash), actual losses can exceed the planned
  stop-loss level due to slippage.
- **"Market orders are always bad."** Market orders are appropriate and
  often necessary when execution certainty matters more than price
  precision — e.g., exiting a position urgently in a fast-moving, liquid
  market.
- **"Bracket and Cover orders remove all risk."** They automate risk
  *definition*, not risk *elimination* — losses (and slippage) are still
  possible, especially around gaps.

## Key Takeaways
- Market orders prioritize execution certainty; limit orders prioritize
  price certainty — you can't maximize both simultaneously.
- SL orders convert to limit orders at trigger; SL-M orders convert to
  market orders at trigger — choose based on whether price or execution
  certainty matters more in that specific trade.
- Bracket and Cover orders (common on Indian platforms) automate entry,
  target, and stop-loss in a single order ticket.
- Slippage is a real cost, especially in illiquid stocks or fast-moving
  markets — the 2010 Flash Crash is a stark historical example.

## Summary
Order types exist to let traders express exactly what matters most to them
in a given moment: getting filled for certain (market orders) or getting a
specific price (limit orders), with stop-loss variants automating exits once
a trigger price is reached. Indian platforms add Bracket and Cover orders
that bundle entry, target, and stop-loss together. Understanding these
trade-offs — and their failure modes, like slippage during fast markets — is
essential before placing your first live order.

## Quiz
1. What does a market order prioritize?
   a) Exact price  b) Execution certainty  c) Lowest brokerage fee  d) Dividend eligibility

2. What happens to an SL-M order once the trigger price is hit?
   a) It becomes a limit order
   b) It becomes a market order
   c) It cancels automatically
   d) Nothing happens until manually confirmed

3. What is a Bracket Order (BO) commonly used for on Indian trading
   platforms?
   a) Only for long-term investing
   b) Bundling entry, target, and stop-loss into a single order
   c) Buying mutual funds
   d) Currency conversion

4. What real risk did the 2010 Flash Crash illustrate about stop-loss market
   orders?
   a) They always execute at the exact stop price
   b) They can experience significant slippage in fast-moving markets
   c) They are illegal in the US
   d) They only apply to bonds

5. True or False: A limit order is guaranteed to execute as long as you're
   patient.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Market orders prioritize getting filled, not a specific price.
2. **b)** — SL-M becomes a market order at trigger, prioritizing execution certainty.
3. **b)** — Bracket Orders bundle entry, target, and stop-loss for Indian intraday trading.
4. **b)** — The Flash Crash showed stop-loss/market orders can fill far from expected prices during extreme volatility.
5. **b) False** — A limit order may never execute if the market never reaches the specified price.
