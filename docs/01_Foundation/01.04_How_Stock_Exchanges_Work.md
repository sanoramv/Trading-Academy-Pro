## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **stock exchange** is a regulated, centralized venue (physical or
electronic) where buyers and sellers of listed securities meet to trade at
transparent, publicly quoted prices, under rules enforced by a market
regulator. Its core functions are: **listing** (admitting companies whose
shares can be traded), **trading** (matching buy and sell orders),
**price discovery** (continuously updating the "fair" price based on
participant behavior), and **surveillance** (detecting manipulation or
unfair practices).

### Intuition
Before organized exchanges existed, buying a share of a company meant
finding the company's other shareholders individually and negotiating a
private sale — slow, opaque, and risky (how would you know you weren't
being cheated on price?). An exchange solves this by bringing *all* buyers
and sellers of a given stock into one place (physically, historically; now
almost entirely electronic) so that the best available price is always
visible and any participant can transact against it immediately.

### Mental Model
Think of a stock exchange as a **continuous, transparent auction** running
throughout the trading day for every listed stock simultaneously. At any
moment, there's a highest price someone is willing to pay (**bid**) and a
lowest price someone is willing to sell at (**ask/offer**). When a new order
matches an existing one, a trade happens instantly and the price is
recorded and broadcast to everyone.

### Visual Explanation
Modern exchanges run on an electronic **order matching engine** that
maintains an **order book** — a live, ranked list of all buy and sell orders
for a stock, matching them by price-time priority (best price first; if tied,
earliest order first).

### ASCII Diagram
```
                     ORDER BOOK: RELIANCE INDUSTRIES
      BUY ORDERS (BIDS)          |      SELL ORDERS (ASKS)
  Qty      Price                 |      Price      Qty
  500    @ 2,845.00  <-- best bid|best ask -->  2,845.50  @  300
  1200   @ 2,844.50               |      2,846.00  @  800
  300    @ 2,844.00               |      2,846.50  @  450
  ...                             |      ...

  If a new BUY order arrives for 300 shares @ 2,845.50 (matching the
  best ask), the exchange's matching engine executes the trade
  INSTANTLY at 2,845.50, and the order book updates in real time.
```

```
[Company wants to raise capital / provide liquidity to existing owners]
```

## Real World Analogy
A stock exchange works like a large, well-run **fish market** with strict
rules: every seller must display their fish's quality and price openly (no
hidden deals), every buyer can see every seller's price at once, an
official referee (the exchange/regulator) ensures no one lies about the fish
they're selling, and the moment a buyer and seller agree on a price, that
price is announced to the entire market so everyone knows what fish is
currently trading for. Without this open structure, every transaction would
require private negotiation and trust-building from scratch.

## Indian Market Example
The **National Stock Exchange (NSE)**, launched in 1992, was India's first
fully electronic exchange, and it fundamentally transformed Indian markets
by replacing the open-outcry (physically shouting orders on a trading
floor) system used by the BSE at the time. NSE introduced a **fully
automated, screen-based trading system** that allowed participants anywhere
in India to see the same order book and trade at the same transparent price
— a huge leap in fairness and access compared to the old floor-based system,
where proximity to the trading floor mattered. Today, both NSE and BSE are
fully electronic, and NSE is the larger of the two by trading volume.

## US Market Example
The **New York Stock Exchange (NYSE)** historically operated a **hybrid
model** — a physical trading floor with "specialists" (later called
Designated Market Makers) who helped match orders for specific stocks,
combined with electronic trading. Today, NYSE trading is overwhelmingly
electronic, though it retains a symbolic physical floor. **NASDAQ**, in
contrast, was built from inception in 1971 as a purely electronic,
dealer-based market with no physical trading floor, making it one of the
earliest fully electronic exchanges in the world — a structure NSE would
later adopt for the Indian market in 1992.

## Historical Example
Before NSE's 1992 launch, the **Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE)** operated using
an open-outcry system where brokers physically shouted and used hand
signals on a trading floor to execute trades. This system was slow,
favored brokers physically present on the floor (creating information and
access asymmetry), and was harder to regulate for fairness. NSE's
electronic, anonymous, screen-based order matching system — introduced
partly in response to the 1992 Harshad Mehta securities scam (covered in
depth in `[31.03]`), which exposed weaknesses in the old settlement and
oversight systems — set a new standard that BSE and exchanges worldwide
eventually adopted.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutions care deeply about **exchange market microstructure** — the
precise rules of how orders match, what order types are available, how fast
the matching engine is (latency), and how much information about pending
orders is visible before execution. Large institutions often use
sophisticated execution algorithms (Module 29) specifically designed around
a given exchange's matching rules to minimize the market impact of large
orders.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders mostly interact with the exchange indirectly, through a
broker's app, and rarely think about the matching engine underneath.
However, understanding that "the market price" is really just "the last
matched trade price in a continuously updating order book" — not a fixed,
externally-set number — is foundational to understanding why prices can
move quickly and why large orders can move prices against a trader (a
concept fully developed as "liquidity" in Module 02).

## Professional Insights
Professionals distinguish exchanges from **over-the-counter (OTC) markets**.
An exchange is centralized, with all orders visible to a matching engine and
prices publicly quoted (like NSE, BSE, NYSE for stocks). An OTC market has
no single centralized venue — trades happen directly between two
counterparties (often banks), as is common in large parts of the global
currency and bond markets. Professionals choose venues based on the
liquidity, transparency, and counterparty risk trade-offs each offers.

## Common Mistakes
- Believing "the stock price" is set by the exchange or company, rather than
  emerging from the continuous matching of buy and sell orders from
  participants.
- Not realizing that different exchanges (NSE vs BSE, NYSE vs NASDAQ) for the
  *same* stock can briefly show slightly different prices due to
  independent order books, even though arbitrage keeps them very close.
- Assuming all trading happens on an exchange — large portions of bond and
  currency markets trade OTC, off-exchange.

## Misconceptions
- **"The exchange makes money when stock prices go up."** Exchanges
  primarily earn revenue from transaction fees, listing fees, and data
  services — regardless of whether prices rise or fall, since their role is
  facilitating trades, not taking a market view.
- **"NSE and BSE are competitors that trade completely different stocks."**
  Most major Indian stocks are dual-listed and tradeable on both exchanges;
  NSE simply has much higher trading volume for most securities.
- **"A stock exchange guarantees you won't lose money."** An exchange
  guarantees fair, transparent, and orderly trading and settlement — it does
  not guarantee investment outcomes.

## Key Takeaways
- A stock exchange's core jobs are listing, matching orders, price
  discovery, and surveillance.
- Modern exchanges (NSE, NYSE, NASDAQ) run on electronic matching engines
  using an order book with price-time priority.
- NSE's 1992 launch electronified Indian markets, partly in response to
  weaknesses exposed by the 1992 Harshad Mehta scam.
- Not all markets are exchange-based — large parts of bond/currency markets
  are OTC.

## Summary
A stock exchange is the regulated, transparent venue where buy and sell
orders for listed securities are matched, producing the continuously
updating "market price" through an electronic order book. NSE and BSE serve
this function in India; NYSE and NASDAQ serve it in the US. Understanding
that price emerges from real-time order matching — not from a company or
exchange "setting" a price — is essential before moving into how orders and
participants actually behave, covered in the next lessons of this module.

## Quiz
1. What determines the "market price" of a stock at any moment?
   a) The company's CEO
   b) The exchange regulator directly
   c) The last matched trade from the continuously updating order book
   d) The government

2. What system did BSE use before exchanges became electronic?
   a) Fully automated matching from day one
   b) Open-outcry floor trading
   c) Mail-in paper orders only
   d) Auction once per week

3. What historical event was a catalyst for NSE's more transparent,
   electronic model?
   a) The 2008 Global Financial Crisis
   b) The 1992 Harshad Mehta securities scam
   c) The COVID-19 pandemic
   d) India's independence in 1947

4. Which of these best describes an OTC (over-the-counter) market?
   a) A centralized exchange with a public order book
   b) Trades happening directly between counterparties without a centralized venue
   c) A market only for government use
   d) A market that only trades on weekends

5. True or False: NSE and BSE trade completely different, non-overlapping
   sets of stocks.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **c)** — Price emerges from the last matched trade in the live order book, not from an external authority.
2. **b)** — BSE historically used physical open-outcry trading before going electronic.
3. **b)** — The 1992 scam exposed settlement/oversight weaknesses that NSE's electronic model helped address.
4. **b)** — OTC markets lack a centralized matching venue; trades are direct between counterparties.
5. **b) False** — Most major stocks are dual-listed on both NSE and BSE.
