# [01.06] Primary Market vs Secondary Market

> Module: Foundation · Lesson 6 of 10 · Estimated time: 30 minutes

## Learning Objectives
- Distinguish the primary market from the secondary market.
- Explain the IPO process at a beginner level.
- Understand why secondary market trading doesn't directly give companies more capital.
- Identify Indian and US examples of primary market issuances.

## Prerequisites
- `[01.01]` What Is a Financial Market
- `[01.04]` How Stock Exchanges Work

## Core Concepts

### Definition
The **primary market** is where new securities are created and sold by an
issuer (a company or government) directly to investors for the first time,
raising fresh capital for the issuer. An **IPO (Initial Public Offering)**
is the most well-known example.

The **secondary market** is where previously issued securities are traded
between investors — the issuer is not a party to these trades and receives
no new capital from them. This is what most people mean when they say "the
stock market" day to day (NSE, BSE, NYSE, NASDAQ trading).

### Intuition
Imagine a company selling its own shares for the first time — like a bakery
selling its very first batch of "ownership certificates" directly to
customers to raise money for a new oven. That direct sale is the primary
market. Once those customers own certificates, they might resell them to
each other at a coffee shop, and the bakery isn't involved in — or paid for
— those resales at all. That resale marketplace is the secondary market.

### Mental Model
```
PRIMARY MARKET: Issuer <---> First Investor   (Issuer RECEIVES capital)
SECONDARY MARKET: Investor A <---> Investor B (Issuer receives NOTHING further)
```
Everything you see moving on a stock ticker throughout a normal trading day
is secondary market activity — the company already received its capital
(if any) at the IPO stage, sometimes years or decades earlier.

### Visual Explanation
The lifecycle of a share moves from creation (primary) to endless resale
(secondary), with the issuing company only involved at the creation stage
(and, rarely, in subsequent capital raises like FPOs/rights issues).

### ASCII Diagram
```
   STEP 1: PRIMARY MARKET (happens once, or rarely, per issuance)

   [Company] --issues new shares--> [IPO Investors]
       ^                                  |
       |                                  |
   Receives capital <--------- pays purchase price


   STEP 2: SECONDARY MARKET (happens continuously, forever after)

   [Investor A] <====== resells shares ======> [Investor B]
       (Company is NOT a party to this trade and receives no capital)
                        |
                        v
              Price moves based on
              ongoing supply & demand
```

### Flowchart
```
[Company needs capital]
        |
        v
[Decides to go public via IPO / FPO --> PRIMARY MARKET]
        |
        v
[Shares allotted to IPO investors, capital raised]
        |
        v
[Shares get listed on an exchange]
        |
        v
[Shares trade continuously between investors --> SECONDARY MARKET]
        |
        v
[Company's day-to-day share price moves have NO direct cash effect
 on the company itself, unless it issues MORE shares later]
```

## Real World Analogy
Buying a concert ticket directly from the event organizer (primary market)
is different from buying that same ticket later from someone reselling it
on a resale site (secondary market). The event organizer only gets paid
during the initial sale — all the reselling afterward, no matter how many
times the ticket changes hands or at what price, generates zero additional
revenue for the organizer. This is exactly how a company's shares work:
after the IPO, the company doesn't get richer or poorer as the stock price
moves in day-to-day secondary trading (though it does affect things like the
company's ability to raise capital cheaply in the future, and executive
compensation tied to stock price).

## Indian Market Example
When **Zomato** conducted its IPO in July 2021, the company raised
approximately ₹9,000 crore in fresh capital for its business through a
fresh issue of new shares sold directly to investors (the primary market
transaction), alongside a separate ₹375 crore Offer for Sale (OFS) by
existing shareholder Info Edge (India) Ltd. — proceeds from the OFS portion
went to Info Edge, not Zomato, bringing the total issue size to ₹9,375
crore. From the day
Zomato's shares began trading on NSE/BSE (listing day) onward, every
subsequent trade — whether the stock rose or fell — was a secondary market
transaction between investors; Zomato as a company received no further cash
from any of that day-to-day trading.

## US Market Example
When **Airbnb** conducted its IPO in December 2020, it raised billions of
dollars in fresh capital directly through the primary market offering.
After listing on NASDAQ, all subsequent trading of Airbnb shares — the
price swings reported daily in financial news — has been secondary market
activity between investors, generating no additional direct capital for
Airbnb itself (barring any future secondary offerings).

## Historical Example
**Coal India's IPO in 2010** was, at the time, one of India's largest ever,
raising approximately ₹15,200 crore for the Government of India (which was
divesting a portion of its stake) — a clear primary market transaction
transferring capital from investors to the selling shareholder. In the
years since, Coal India's shares have traded continuously on the secondary
market, with price movements driven by earnings, coal demand, government
policy, and broader market sentiment — none of which sends additional
capital back to the original 2010 transaction's parties directly.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutions participate heavily in the primary market through mechanisms
like **anchor investor allocations** in Indian IPOs (large institutional
investors who commit to an IPO before it opens to the general public,
lending credibility and price stability) and **book-building** processes
(where institutional demand helps determine the final IPO price). In the
secondary market, institutions are typically the largest source of daily
trading volume, alongside market makers.

### Retail Perspective
Retail investors most commonly encounter the primary market through IPO
applications (via UPI-based ASBA in India, or brokerage IPO platforms in the
US) and interact with the secondary market every time they buy or sell an
already-listed stock through their broker. A common beginner confusion is
believing that buying shares in the secondary market somehow funds the
company further — it does not, beyond the historical fact that the company
once raised capital when those shares were first created.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts distinguish IPO-stage risk (uncertain pricing, often
limited historical public financial data, potential for significant
first-day volatility) from established secondary-market risk (more
historical data available, but subject to broader market and
company-specific risk factors). Many professional investors explicitly avoid
buying IPOs on listing day due to the historically higher volatility and
frequent post-listing price corrections once initial hype fades — a pattern
seen across many high-profile IPOs in both Indian and US markets.

## Common Mistakes
- Believing a rising secondary market stock price means the company is
  "getting richer" in real time — it isn't, unless it issues more shares.
- Applying for every IPO without evaluating the business, purely because "it's
  new" or "everyone is talking about it."
- Not understanding that a **Follow-on Public Offer (FPO)** or **Qualified
  Institutional Placement (QIP)** are also primary market transactions
  (additional capital raises after the initial IPO), distinct from ordinary
  secondary trading.

## Misconceptions
- **"IPOs always make money for retail investors."** IPO listing-day pops
  are common but not guaranteed, and many IPOs (in both India and the US)
  have traded below their issue price for extended periods after listing.
- **"The secondary market doesn't matter to companies at all."** A healthy,
  liquid secondary market matters greatly to companies because it makes
  their stock more attractive for future primary market capital raises and
  affects things like the cost of raising future capital and use of stock
  for employee compensation/M&A.
- **"Buying a stock in the secondary market supports the company's
  operations financially."** Only primary market transactions transfer
  capital to the company; buying in the secondary market transfers money to
  the previous shareholder who sold to you, not to the company.

## Key Takeaways
- Primary market = new securities created, capital goes to the issuer.
- Secondary market = existing securities traded between investors, no new
  capital to the issuer.
- IPOs, FPOs, and QIPs are primary market events; everyday stock market
  trading is secondary market activity.
- IPO investing carries distinct risks (pricing uncertainty, volatility)
  compared to investing in already-listed, established secondary market
  securities.

## Summary
The primary market is where companies raise capital by creating and selling
new securities directly to investors (most visibly through IPOs); the
secondary market is where those securities then trade continuously between
investors, with the issuing company no longer a party to the transaction.
Confusing the two leads to misunderstanding both how companies actually get
funded and what your day-to-day stock trading does (and doesn't) do for the
underlying business.

## Revision Notes
- Primary market: issuer ↔ investor, capital flows to issuer. Happens via
  IPO/FPO/QIP.
- Secondary market: investor ↔ investor, no capital flows to issuer.
  Happens continuously on exchanges.
- Zomato IPO (2021): ₹9,375 crore raised. Coal India IPO (2010): ~₹15,200
  crore raised — both primary market events.
- IPOs carry distinct pricing/volatility risk vs. established secondary
  market stocks.

## Quiz
1. In which market does a company receive fresh capital directly from investors?
   a) Secondary market  b) Primary market  c) Neither  d) Both equally

2. What happens to a company's cash position when its stock price rises 5%
   in ordinary secondary market trading?
   a) The company's cash increases by 5% of market cap
   b) Nothing directly — no new capital changes hands with the company
   c) The company must issue a dividend immediately
   d) The stock exchange pays the company

3. What is an IPO?
   a) A type of secondary market order
   b) A company's Initial Public Offering, selling new shares to raise capital
   c) An index tracking product
   d) A government bond auction

4. Which of these is also a primary market transaction, besides an IPO?
   a) A retail investor selling shares to another retail investor
   b) A Qualified Institutional Placement (QIP)
   c) A limit order on NSE
   d) A stop-loss order execution

5. True or False: Buying shares in the secondary market sends money
   directly to the company whose shares you bought.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — The primary market is where the issuer directly receives capital from investors.
2. **b)** — Secondary market price moves don't directly change the company's cash position.
3. **b)** — An IPO is a company's initial sale of new shares to the public to raise capital.
4. **b)** — A QIP is another primary market capital-raising mechanism, alongside IPOs and FPOs.
5. **b) False** — Secondary market purchases pay the previous shareholder, not the company.
