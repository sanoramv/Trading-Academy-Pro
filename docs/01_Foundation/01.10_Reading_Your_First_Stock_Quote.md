## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **stock quote** is a real-time (or near real-time) snapshot of a
security's trading data, typically including: **Last Traded Price (LTP)**,
**Bid** (highest current buy price) and **Ask/Offer** (lowest current sell
price), **Volume** (number of shares traded), **Open Interest (OI)** (for
derivatives — number of outstanding contract positions), the **day's
high/low range**, and the **52-week high/low range**.

### Intuition
A quote is a compressed summary of everything happening in a stock's order
book (from `[01.04]`) at a glance. Instead of watching the full, constantly
updating order book, most traders check a handful of key numbers that tell
them: where is the price right now, how actively is it trading, how liquid
is it, and how does today compare to its recent history.

### Mental Model
Group quote fields into three questions:
1. **"Where is the price?"** → LTP, Bid, Ask
2. **"How active/liquid is trading?"** → Volume, Bid-Ask Spread, Open Interest (for F&O)
3. **"How does this compare to recent history?"** → Day Range, 52-Week Range

### Visual Explanation
A typical quote screen arranges these fields in a standard layout across
virtually every Indian and US broker platform.

## Real World Analogy
Reading a stock quote is like reading a car's dashboard while driving: the
speedometer (LTP) tells you your current speed, the fuel gauge (Volume)
tells you how much activity/energy is being used, and the trip odometer
alongside the total odometer (Day Range vs. 52-Week Range) tells you both
your recent short trip and your long-term journey context — all necessary
information glanced at together, not read one at a time in isolation.

## Indian Market Example
On the **NSE India website** or any Indian broker app, searching for
**"NIFTY 50"** shows the index's LTP, day's change, day range, and — because
Nifty is heavily traded via derivatives — a separate options chain showing
Open Interest by strike price, which traders use to gauge where large
positions are concentrated (a topic developed fully in `[21.11]` Option
Chain Analysis). A tight bid-ask spread of just a few paise on a heavily
traded stock like **Reliance Industries** signals high liquidity, whereas a
small-cap stock might show a spread of ₹2–5 or more on a similarly priced
stock, signaling thinner liquidity.

## US Market Example
On a US platform showing **Apple Inc. (AAPL)**, the quote typically
displays LTP, bid/ask (often with exchange-specific NBBO — National Best
Bid and Offer — aggregating quotes across multiple US exchanges and market
makers), day range, 52-week range, and volume, alongside **after-hours
trading** data — a feature especially relevant around US earnings
announcements, which are commonly released after regular market close, when
significant price moves can occur in the after-hours session before the
next full trading day begins.

## Historical Example
During periods of extreme volatility — such as March 2020's COVID crash —
bid-ask spreads across many markets, including both Indian and US equities,
widened significantly compared to normal conditions, as market makers
(from `[01.05]`) reduced their willingness to provide tight liquidity amid
extreme uncertainty. Traders who didn't check the bid-ask spread before
placing market orders during this period sometimes experienced significantly
worse fill prices than expected — a direct, practical consequence of not
reading the full quote, not just the LTP.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutions look well beyond the basic quote screen to **Level 2 / market
depth data** — showing multiple levels of the order book (not just the best
bid/ask, but the next several price levels and their sizes), which gives a
much richer picture of where liquidity is concentrated and how a large
order might move the price. Retail platforms increasingly offer basic
market depth views too (often called "5-depth" or "20-depth" market watch
on Indian platforms).

## Retail Perspective
New retail traders often fixate exclusively on the LTP and its color
(green/red), ignoring volume, bid-ask spread, and range data entirely. This
is a natural starting point, but this lesson's goal is to build the habit of
scanning the *entire* quote, not just the headline number, before making any
decision.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders treat a widening bid-ask spread as a warning sign of
either reduced liquidity or increased uncertainty (often both), and adjust
their order type choice accordingly (favoring limit orders over market
orders when spreads widen, per `[01.07]`). They also compare current
volume to a stock's average volume (not just today's raw number) to judge
whether unusual participation is occurring — a raw volume number alone,
without context, is not very informative.

## Common Mistakes
- Looking only at LTP color (green/red) and ignoring volume, spread, and
  range context entirely.
- Placing a market order without checking the bid-ask spread first,
  especially in a less liquid stock.
- Confusing Open Interest (a derivatives-specific concept, `[20.03]`,
  `[21.11]`) with regular equity trading volume — they measure different
  things.

## Misconceptions
- **"A stock's LTP is the price I'll definitely get if I buy right now."**
  The LTP is the *last* traded price, which can differ from the price
  you'll actually receive, especially if you use a market order in a
  fast-moving or illiquid stock (per `[01.07]`, `[01.08]`).
- **"High volume always means the stock is a good buy."** High volume
  indicates high activity/interest, not direction or quality — a stock can
  have very high volume while falling sharply, just as easily as while
  rising.
- **"52-week high/low tells you a stock's true value."** It only tells you
  its recent trading range, not whether it's fundamentally cheap or
  expensive — that requires fundamental analysis, covered starting Module
  07.

## Key Takeaways
- A stock quote compresses the order book into a handful of key numbers:
  LTP, Bid/Ask, Volume, Day Range, 52-Week Range, and (for derivatives) OI.
- Always check the bid-ask spread before choosing an order type, especially
  in less liquid securities.
- Volume and range numbers need context (compared to averages/history) to
  be meaningful, not read in isolation.
- This lesson completes Module 01 — you can now independently read a live
  quote and place an informed order type.

## Summary
A stock quote is your compressed window into a security's live order book,
combining price (LTP, Bid/Ask), activity (Volume, Open Interest for
derivatives), and context (Day Range, 52-Week Range) into a single glance.
Reading the full quote — not just the LTP — is a foundational habit that
directly informs which order type to use and how confident you should be in
your expected execution price. With this, Module 01's foundation is
complete: you understand what a market is, who trades in it, how exchanges
and infrastructure work, how orders are placed and executed, and how to read
the resulting live data. Module 02 builds directly on this foundation to
teach market structure.

## Revision Notes
- LTP = Last Traded Price. Bid = best buy price. Ask/Offer = best sell price.
- Bid-Ask Spread width signals liquidity (tight = liquid, wide = illiquid).
- Volume = shares traded; OI = outstanding derivative contract positions
  (different concepts).
- Day Range and 52-Week Range provide short- and long-term price context.
- Always check the full quote, not just LTP color, before choosing an order type.

## Quiz
1. What does "Bid" represent in a stock quote?
   a) The last traded price
   b) The highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay
   c) The lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept
   d) The company's total market value

2. What does a widening bid-ask spread typically signal?
   a) Increasing liquidity
   b) Decreasing liquidity and/or rising uncertainty
   c) A guaranteed price increase
   d) Nothing significant

3. What is Open Interest (OI)?
   a) The number of shares traded today
   b) The total number of outstanding derivative contract positions
   c) The company's total outstanding shares
   d) The dividend yield

4. Why is checking volume relative to its average more useful than checking
   raw volume alone?
   a) It isn't — raw volume is always sufficient
   b) Raw volume alone lacks context for judging whether activity is unusual
   c) Average volume is illegal to disclose
   d) Raw volume never changes

5. True or False: The 52-week high/low tells you whether a stock is
   fundamentally undervalued or overvalued.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Bid is the best (highest) price a buyer is currently willing to pay.
2. **b)** — Wider spreads typically indicate lower liquidity and/or higher uncertainty.
3. **b)** — Open Interest measures outstanding derivative contract positions, distinct from volume.
4. **b)** — Context (vs. average) is needed to judge whether current volume is actually unusual.
5. **b) False** — It only reflects recent trading range, not fundamental valuation.
