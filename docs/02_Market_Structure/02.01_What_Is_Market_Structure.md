## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Market structure** is the observable pattern formed by a sequence of
swing highs and swing lows on a price chart, which reveals whether a market
is trending up, trending down, or moving sideways (ranging), and where key
decision points (support/resistance) sit. It is the raw skeleton of price
behavior — before any indicator, pattern name, or trading-school-specific
vocabulary is layered on top.

### Intuition
Every price chart, no matter the instrument or timeframe, is fundamentally
just a sequence of "price went up to here, then came back down to here,
then went up further, then came back down to a higher point than before."
Market structure is simply the discipline of tracking that sequence
consciously instead of just glancing at whether the current candle is green
or red. Once you can read structure, indicators become optional
confirmation tools rather than a substitute for understanding price itself.

### Mental Model
All price action, at the most basic level, reduces to three possible
structural states:

```
UPTREND:    Higher Highs (HH) + Higher Lows (HL)
DOWNTREND:  Lower Highs (LH) + Lower Lows (LL)
RANGE:      Roughly equal highs and lows, oscillating between two levels
```

Everything in later modules — Wyckoff phases, SMC order blocks, ICT kill
zones, classical chart patterns — is a more detailed description layered on
top of these three basic states, not a replacement for them.

### Visual Explanation
Structure is best seen by connecting a chart's swing highs and swing lows
in sequence and observing whether each new high/low is higher, lower, or
roughly equal to the prior one.

## Real World Analogy
Reading market structure is like watching a staircase being built in real
time. In an uptrend, each new step is built higher than the last, and even
though the stair-builder occasionally steps back down slightly between
steps, they never step back down as far as the previous step's base — the
staircase keeps climbing overall. In a downtrend, it's the reverse — a
staircase heading down, with each landing lower than the last. In a range,
imagine the builder walking back and forth on a single flat floor, never
committing to building upward or downward.

## Indian Market Example
Looking at the **Nifty 50 index** chart from roughly early 2020 (post-COVID
crash low around 7,511 in March 2020) through late 2021 (peak around 18,600
in October 2021), the index displayed a clear, extended uptrend structure —
a sequence of higher highs and higher lows over many months, punctuated by
normal pullbacks that never broke below the prior swing low's structure
until the broader trend eventually matured and structure shifted into a
range/correction phase in 2022.

## US Market Example
The **S&P 500** during 2022 offers a clear downtrend structure example:
from its January 2022 high near 4,818 to its October 2022 low near 3,491,
the index formed a sequence of lower highs and lower lows, driven by the US
Federal Reserve's aggressive interest rate hiking cycle (a macro driver
covered in Module 06) — a textbook downtrend structure that any trader,
regardless of which specific trading school they follow, could identify
using nothing more than swing high/low sequencing.

## Historical Example
The **Sensex's multi-decade chart** from India's 1991 economic
liberalization onward shows all three structural states occurring
repeatedly at different points: extended uptrend structure through much of
the 2003–2007 period, a sharp downtrend structure during the 2008 Global
Financial Crisis (Sensex falling from roughly 21,000 in January 2008 to
around 8,000 by late 2008/early 2009), and extended multi-year range-bound
structure during periods like 2010–2013. No single structural state persists
forever — markets cycle through all three continuously, which is precisely
why structure must be read fresh on every chart, not assumed from memory of
past behavior.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutions build their entire trading and risk frameworks around
structural context — a trend-following fund will only take long positions
when structure confirms an uptrend, and a mean-reversion fund will only fade
extremes when structure confirms a range. Institutional risk desks also use
structure to define invalidation levels for entire portfolios of positions,
since a confirmed break of major structure (covered in `[02.05]`) often
triggers systematic reassessment of a fund's broader market view.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders very commonly skip structure analysis entirely and jump
straight to indicators (RSI, MACD) or patterns (head and shoulders,
triangles) without first establishing the basic structural context those
tools are supposed to operate within. This is like trying to read a
paragraph's meaning by memorizing individual letters without first
understanding that letters form words, and words form sentences — the
tools work far better once structure is understood as the foundational
layer beneath them.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders often say "structure first, everything else second."
Before applying any indicator or pattern, professionals ask: is this
instrument trending or ranging right now, on the timeframe I care about? The
answer fundamentally changes which trading strategies (Module 24) are even
appropriate — trend-following strategies underperform in ranges, and
mean-reversion strategies underperform in strong trends, so misreading
structure is often the root cause of a strategy "not working," rather than
the strategy itself being flawed.

## Common Mistakes
- Jumping straight to indicators or patterns without first identifying the
  basic structural state (uptrend, downtrend, range).
- Assuming a single timeframe's structure applies to all timeframes (a stock
  can be in an uptrend on the daily chart while in a downtrend on the
  1-hour chart — covered fully in `[02.08]`).
- Declaring a trend based on just two swing points, without enough
  confirmed structure to distinguish a real trend from noise.

## Misconceptions
- **"Market structure is a specific trading strategy/school of thought."**
  It is not owned by any single methodology — Wyckoff, SMC, ICT, and
  classical technical analysis all describe the same underlying structural
  reality using different vocabularies layered on top.
- **"You need special software to read market structure."** Market
  structure can be read on any basic candlestick or line chart by eye, once
  you know what to look for — no special indicator is required, though tools
  can assist.
- **"A market is always clearly trending or clearly ranging."** Markets
  frequently sit in ambiguous, transitional structural states — recognizing
  genuine ambiguity (and reducing position size or standing aside
  accordingly) is itself an important skill, covered further in Module 25.

## Key Takeaways
- Market structure is the sequence of swing highs and swing lows that
  reveals whether a market is trending up, trending down, or ranging.
- The three basic structural states — uptrend, downtrend, range — underlie
  every more advanced framework taught later in this academy.
- Structure should be read before applying any indicator or pattern, not
  after.
- No structural state is permanent; markets cycle continuously between all
  three over time.

## Summary
Market structure is the foundational skill of reading a sequence of swing
highs and swing lows to determine whether a market is trending up, trending
down, or ranging — the shared skeleton beneath every more advanced
price-reading framework this academy will teach later, from classical
technical analysis to Wyckoff to Smart Money Concepts to ICT. Establishing
this structural context before applying any tool or indicator is the single
most important habit this module will build, starting with the precise
definitions of higher highs/higher lows and lower highs/lower lows in the
next lesson.

## Quiz
1. What defines an uptrend in market structure terms?
   a) Rising volume only
   b) Higher highs and higher lows
   c) A green candle today
   d) High Open Interest

2. What defines a range in market structure terms?
   a) Consistently higher highs and higher lows
   b) Consistently lower highs and lower lows
   c) Roughly equal highs and lows oscillating between two levels
   d) Any day with low volume

3. Which of these best describes the relationship between market structure
   and tools like Wyckoff, SMC, or ICT?
   a) They are unrelated concepts
   b) Structure is the shared foundation; these are more detailed frameworks built on top of it
   c) Market structure was invented by ICT
   d) Only Wyckoff uses market structure

4. What happened to the S&P 500's structure during 2022?
   a) It formed a clear uptrend of higher highs and higher lows
   b) It formed a downtrend of lower highs and lower lows, driven partly by Fed rate hikes
   c) It stayed perfectly flat all year
   d) It doubled in value

5. True or False: A market's structural state (trend/range) is permanent
   once established.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Higher highs and higher lows define an uptrend structurally.
2. **c)** — A range shows roughly equal highs and lows oscillating between two levels.
3. **b)** — Market structure is the shared foundation; other frameworks add detail on top of it.
4. **b)** — The S&P 500 formed a downtrend in 2022 amid aggressive Fed rate hikes.
5. **b) False** — Structural states cycle continuously; none persist forever.
