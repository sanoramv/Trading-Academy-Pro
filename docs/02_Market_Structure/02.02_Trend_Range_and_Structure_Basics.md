## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **trend** is a sustained directional move in price, characterized by a
consistent sequence of higher highs/higher lows (uptrend) or lower
highs/lower lows (downtrend), that persists across multiple swings — not
just one or two. A **range** is a period where price oscillates between a
relatively stable upper boundary (resistance) and lower boundary (support)
without making sustained progress in either direction. The core working
principle professional traders use is: **"the trend/range is assumed to be
in force until price structure proves otherwise."**

### Intuition
Markets don't announce "I am now trending" or "I am now ranging" — traders
infer the current state from the same swing-high/swing-low evidence
introduced in `[02.01]`, updated continuously as new price bars form. This
means your assessment of structure is always provisional — a working
hypothesis you update, not a fixed fact you declare once and never revisit.

### Mental Model
Think of "structure until proven otherwise" like a legal presumption of
innocence: you assume the existing trend or range continues **unless and
until** a specific structural event proves it has ended (covered fully as
Break of Structure / Change of Character in `[02.05]`). This prevents two
common errors: assuming every trend is over the moment there's a single
pullback, and assuming every range will hold forever.

### Visual Explanation
Both trends and ranges tend to move through three recognizable phases:
**initiation**, **continuation**, and **exhaustion/transition**.

## Real World Analogy
A trend is like a river flowing consistently downhill — it may briefly pool
or eddy in spots, but it keeps moving in one overall direction until it
hits a genuinely new obstacle (a dam, a change in terrain). A range is like
a lake — water moves within it, sometimes choppy, sometimes calm, but it
isn't going anywhere until something changes the terrain enough to let it
drain out (a breakout) or a new inflow changes its level. Assuming "this is
a lake" the moment you see a single wave, or assuming "this is a flowing
river" the moment you see a single ripple, are both premature judgments —
you need to observe the pattern over enough time to be confident.

## Indian Market Example
**Bank Nifty** frequently displays clean multi-week range structures
between major macro catalysts (like RBI policy announcements or major
earnings seasons for large banks such as HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, and SBI),
oscillating between a defined support and resistance band, before a
catalyst triggers a breakout into a new trend phase — a pattern many
Indian derivatives traders specifically watch for, since range-bound Bank
Nifty behavior favors different strategies (like options selling, Module
24.09) than trending behavior does.

## US Market Example
The **Nasdaq Composite** during 2023 illustrates a trend initiation and
continuation phase clearly: after a prolonged 2022 downtrend, the index
found a low in late December 2022/early January 2023 and began forming
higher highs and higher lows through the year, substantially driven by
renewed enthusiasm around artificial intelligence-related companies
(notably Nvidia) — a textbook example of a trend's initiation phase
following an extended prior downtrend/basing structure.

## Historical Example
The **Sensex's 2010–2013 period** is a well-documented multi-year range
example: after the sharp 2008–2009 crash and recovery, the index spent
roughly three years oscillating in a broad range (roughly 15,000–21,000)
without decisively breaking into a new sustained trend, reflecting a period
of macro uncertainty (including persistent inflation concerns and global
growth worries) before eventually breaking out into a strong uptrend from
2013–2014 onward, coinciding with improving macro sentiment. This
multi-year example illustrates that ranges can persist far longer than
beginners often expect — patience and structural confirmation matter more
than guessing when a range will end.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional trend-following strategies (Module 24.02) are specifically
designed to underperform during range-bound periods and outperform during
strong trends — sophisticated trend-following funds openly acknowledge this
trade-off and size their risk accordingly, rather than trying to predict
exactly when a range will transition into a trend. Institutional range
strategies (like some options-selling and market-making approaches) do the
opposite, profiting from range persistence and losing when unexpected
breakouts occur.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders very commonly apply trend-following techniques (like moving
average crossovers) during range-bound markets, generating repeated false
signals and losses — a classic case of using the right tool in the wrong
structural context, rather than the tool itself being broken. Recognizing
"we are currently in a range, not a trend" before choosing a strategy
prevents much of this frustration.

## Professional Insights
Professionals often quantify "how strong is this trend" using the
steepness and consistency of the HH/HL (or LH/LL) sequence, and the
shallowness of pullbacks relative to the prior impulse move — a strong
trend shows shallow pullbacks (30–50% retracement of the prior swing,
connecting to Fibonacci concepts in `[10.09]`), while a weakening trend
shows increasingly deep pullbacks (approaching or exceeding 61.8–100%
retracement), often the earliest visual warning sign of a transition into
range or reversal.

## Common Mistakes
- Declaring a trend "over" after a single normal pullback, without waiting
  for actual structural confirmation (`[02.05]`).
- Assuming a range will persist forever and being caught off guard by a
  strong breakout with no plan in place.
- Applying trend-following tools in range conditions, or range/mean-
  reversion tools in strong trend conditions.

## Misconceptions
- **"A pullback within a trend means the trend has ended."** Normal,
  healthy trends always include pullbacks — the key structural question is
  whether the pullback holds above the prior swing low (in an uptrend) or
  breaks it, not whether a pullback occurs at all.
- **"Ranges are low-opportunity, boring markets to avoid entirely."** Ranges
  offer real, well-defined opportunities (buying support, selling
  resistance, or selling options premium) for traders whose strategies are
  designed for range conditions.
- **"You can predict exactly when a range will break into a trend."**
  Structure tells you the current state and gives probabilistic clues
  (like narrowing ranges often preceding bigger moves), but exact breakout
  timing is not reliably predictable — risk management (Module 25) exists
  precisely because of this uncertainty.

## Key Takeaways
- Trend and range are working hypotheses, assumed valid until structure
  proves otherwise — not permanent labels.
- Both trends and ranges move through initiation, continuation, and
  exhaustion/transition phases.
- Pullback depth relative to the prior impulse move is an early clue to
  trend strength or weakening.
- Match your strategy type (trend-following vs. range/mean-reversion) to
  the current structural state, not the other way around.

## Summary
Trends and ranges are both provisional, evidence-based assessments —
"structure until proven otherwise" — that move through recognizable
initiation, continuation, and exhaustion phases. Reading pullback depth and
range persistence gives early clues about a structure's health, but exact
transition timing is never fully predictable, which is why matching your
strategy type to the current structural state (rather than forcing a
trend-following approach onto a range, or vice versa) is one of the most
practically valuable skills this module builds — setting up the precise
definitions of higher highs/lows in the next lesson.

## Quiz
1. What does "structure until proven otherwise" mean?
   a) Structure changes every candle
   b) Assume the current trend/range persists until a specific structural event proves otherwise
   c) Structure is irrelevant once established
   d) Only institutions can assess structure

2. What are the three phases a trend typically passes through?
   a) Buy, hold, sell
   b) Initiation, continuation, exhaustion/transition
   c) Open, high, low
   d) Morning, afternoon, evening

3. What is an early visual clue that a trend may be weakening?
   a) Pullbacks becoming shallower
   b) Pullbacks becoming progressively deeper
   c) Volume disappearing entirely
   d) The chart turning a different color

4. What multi-year period exemplifies a range-bound Sensex market?
   a) 2003–2007
   b) 2010–2013
   c) 2020–2021
   d) 1991–1995

5. True or False: A single pullback within an uptrend automatically means
   the uptrend has ended.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — It's a working assumption maintained until specific structural evidence disproves it.
2. **b)** — Initiation, continuation, and exhaustion/transition are the three recognizable phases.
3. **b)** — Progressively deeper pullbacks relative to the prior impulse move are an early weakening signal.
4. **b)** — Sensex traded in a broad multi-year range roughly from 2010–2013.
5. **b) False** — Normal pullbacks are expected within healthy trends; only a structural break invalidates the trend.
