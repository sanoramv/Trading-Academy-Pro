# [23.01] What Is Volatility — Historical vs Implied

## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Volatility** is a statistical measure of how much an asset's price
fluctuates over a given period, typically expressed as an annualized
standard deviation of returns. **Historical volatility** (also called
realized volatility) measures how much the underlying *actually* moved
over a past period, calculated directly from price data. **Implied
volatility (IV)** is the volatility figure that, when input into an
option pricing model (`[22.01]`), produces the option's currently
observed market premium — it is *reverse-engineered* from price, and
represents the market's collective, forward-looking expectation of
future movement, not a measurement of movement that has already happened.

### Intuition
Historical volatility looks backward: "how much did this actually
move?" Implied volatility looks forward: "how much does the market,
collectively, expect this to move?" These are related but distinct —
IV can run higher or lower than recent historical volatility depending
on upcoming known events, current sentiment, and how much uncertainty
option buyers are collectively willing to pay to hedge or speculate on.

### Mental Model
```
HISTORICAL VOLATILITY:
  Calculated FROM PAST PRICE DATA (a backward-looking measurement)
  Answers: "How much DID this move?"

IMPLIED VOLATILITY:
  Reverse-engineered FROM CURRENT OPTION PREMIUM using a pricing model
  Answers: "How much does the market EXPECT this to move?"

  Premium = f(Underlying, Strike, Time, INTEREST RATE, VOLATILITY)
  Given every other input is observable/known, solving the formula
  BACKWARD for the volatility input that matches the observed
  market premium yields Implied Volatility.

VOLATILITY RISK PREMIUM:
  On average, over long periods, Implied Volatility has tended to run
  HIGHER than the volatility subsequently realized -- a persistent,
  though not universal or guaranteed, historical pattern.
```

### Visual Explanation
Picture two lines on the same chart: historical volatility, a jagged
line tracking the underlying's actual recent movement, and implied
volatility, a separate line derived purely from option prices — the two
often move together broadly but can diverge sharply, particularly
ahead of known events when IV rises in anticipation while historical
volatility (measuring only the past) hasn't yet reflected anything.

### Worked Example
**Hypothetical Example.** The Definition above describes volatility as
"an annualized standard deviation of returns" — here is exactly what that
means, calculated by hand from a short series of daily returns:

```
ASSUMPTIONS -- 6 daily returns (%) for a hypothetical stock:
  Day 1: +1.0%   Day 2: -0.5%   Day 3: +0.8%
  Day 4: -1.2%   Day 5: +0.3%   Day 6: -0.4%

STEP 1 -- Calculate the mean (average) daily return
  Mean = (1.0 - 0.5 + 0.8 - 1.2 + 0.3 - 0.4) / 6 = 0.0 / 6 = 0.0%

STEP 2 -- Calculate each day's deviation from the mean, then square it
  Day 1:  1.0 - 0.0 =  1.0   ->  1.0^2 = 1.00
  Day 2: -0.5 - 0.0 = -0.5   -> -0.5^2 = 0.25
  Day 3:  0.8 - 0.0 =  0.8   ->  0.8^2 = 0.64
  Day 4: -1.2 - 0.0 = -1.2   -> -1.2^2 = 1.44
  Day 5:  0.3 - 0.0 =  0.3   ->  0.3^2 = 0.09
  Day 6: -0.4 - 0.0 = -0.4   -> -0.4^2 = 0.16
  Sum of squared deviations = 3.58

STEP 3 -- Variance = average of the squared deviations
  Variance = 3.58 / 6 = 0.597 (in percent-squared units)

STEP 4 -- Daily standard deviation = square root of variance
  Daily SD = sqrt(0.597) = 0.77%

STEP 5 -- Annualize (standard convention: multiply by sqrt of the
          number of trading days in a year, ~252)
  Annualized Historical Volatility = 0.77% x sqrt(252)
                                    = 0.77% x 15.87
                                    =~ 12.2%
```

That 12.2% figure is exactly the kind of number the Indian Market Example
below refers to when it says "Nifty's historical volatility over a calm
month might sit around 12% annualized" — this is the actual arithmetic
behind that statement, not a separately-sourced figure. The same five
steps apply to any real return series; the only thing that changes with
more data (5-10 days here vs. the 252 trading days a real annual figure
would use) is how stable and representative the resulting number is.

## Real World Analogy
Historical volatility is like reading a car's odometer and trip log to
see how erratically it was driven last month; implied volatility is
like the price a rental car insurance company charges *today* based on
how erratically they expect the car to be driven *next* month — the
insurer's price reflects their forward-looking assessment, which may or
may not match how the car actually behaved historically.

## Indian Market Example
Nifty's historical volatility over a calm month might sit around 12%
annualized, while India VIX (implied volatility, `[23.02]` next lesson)
might read 14–15% ahead of a Union Budget (`[03.10]`) — the gap
reflecting the market pricing in extra expected movement around the
announcement, even though the *recent past* (historical volatility) was
comparatively calm. **Hypothetical/illustrative figures.**

## US Market Example
The VIX (US, `[23.03]` next lesson) frequently trades above the S&P
500's trailing historical volatility, particularly during periods of
market complacency, when option sellers are willing to sell insurance
relatively cheaply but still command a premium over pure historical
realized movement — the volatility risk premium in action.

## Historical Example
Academic and industry research studying VIX versus subsequently
realized S&P 500 volatility over multi-decade periods has repeatedly
found that implied volatility has, on average, exceeded the volatility
that was subsequently realized — a documented, persistent phenomenon
(the volatility risk premium) that underlies much of institutional
options-selling activity (`[22.04]`), though the premium is not
guaranteed in any single period and can invert sharply during genuine
market stress, as in 2008 and March 2020.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional volatility desks explicitly trade the *spread* between
implied and their own forecast of realized volatility — buying
volatility when they believe IV understates likely future movement,
selling when they believe it overstates it, treating the historical-
vs-implied gap as their core trading signal rather than a side note.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders benefit from checking both figures before entering an
options trade: buying options when IV is already elevated relative to
recent historical volatility risks paying for movement the market has
already substantially priced in (`[21.12]`, `[22.05]`'s IV crush).

## Professional Insights
Professional traders note that implied volatility is inherently
model-dependent — it's only as accurate as the pricing model (typically
Black-Scholes-family) used to reverse-engineer it, and different models
or assumptions can produce slightly different IV readings for the same
observed premium.

## Common Mistakes
- Using historical volatility alone to judge whether an option is
  "cheap" or "expensive," ignoring what IV — the actual premium driver
  — currently reflects.
- Assuming IV is a precise forecast rather than the market's current,
  revisable collective expectation.
- Forgetting that IV, not historical volatility, is what directly
  determines Vega's effect on premium (`[22.05]`).

## Misconceptions
- **"Historical volatility and implied volatility are the same thing
  measured differently."** They answer fundamentally different
  questions — past actual movement versus current forward-looking
  expectation — and frequently diverge.
- **"High implied volatility means the underlying will definitely move
  a lot."** It means the market is pricing in a *higher expectation* of
  movement — the actual outcome can still be calm.
- **"The volatility risk premium guarantees profit for option sellers."**
  It is a historical average tendency, not a guarantee in any specific
  period — genuine tail events can and do invert it sharply.

## Key Takeaways
- Historical volatility: backward-looking, measured directly from past
  price data.
- Implied volatility: forward-looking, reverse-engineered from current
  option premium via a pricing model.
- IV, not historical volatility, directly drives Vega's effect on
  premium (`[22.05]`).
- The volatility risk premium: IV has, on average, historically run
  above subsequently realized volatility — a persistent but not
  guaranteed pattern.

## Summary
Historical volatility measures how much an underlying actually moved in
the past, while implied volatility — reverse-engineered from current
option prices — represents the market's forward-looking expectation of
future movement, and it is IV, not historical volatility, that directly
drives option premium through Vega. Having distinguished the two, the
next two lessons examine implied volatility's most widely watched
real-world measures: the India VIX and the US VIX.

   a) A fee charged by brokers  b) The tendency for implied volatility to historically run above subsequently realized volatility  c) A tax on options  d) A margin requirement

   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — Actual past price movement.
2. **b)** — Reverse-engineered from current option premium using a pricing model.
3. **b)** — Implied volatility.
4. **b)** — The historical tendency for implied volatility to run above subsequently realized volatility.
5. **b) False** — High IV reflects a higher market expectation, not a guarantee of actual movement.
