## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Volatility skew** describes the pattern where implied volatility
(`[23.01]`) differs systematically across strikes at the same expiry,
rather than being uniform. For equity indices like Nifty and the S&P
500, the typical pattern is a **downside skew** (sometimes called
"skew" specifically): OTM puts (lower strikes) carry higher IV than OTM
calls (higher strikes) at equal distance from the current price. A
**volatility smile** describes a more symmetric pattern — both OTM puts
and OTM calls carrying higher IV than the ATM strike, common in some
currency and commodity markets, resembling a smile shape when plotted.

### Intuition
If markets priced every strike with identical implied volatility
(a flat line), Black-Scholes' simplifying assumptions would hold
perfectly — but real markets don't behave that way. Equity index
options exhibit skew because the market persistently prices in a
greater perceived risk of sharp *downside* moves (crashes) than
sharp *upside* moves (melt-ups) — demand for downside protection (puts)
tends to run structurally higher than demand for OTM call speculation,
pushing put IV higher.


### Visual Explanation
Reading down an option chain's IV column (`[21.11]`) from low to high
strikes on an equity index typically shows IV declining as strikes
rise — a downward-sloping "skew" rather than a symmetric "smile" —
directly visible without needing any chart, just by comparing the IV
figures across the chain.

## Real World Analogy
Volatility skew resembles how flood insurance costs more than an
equivalent-probability drought insurance policy in a region prone to
sudden, severe floods but only gradual droughts — insurers (option
market participants) price the *sudden, severe* downside risk (puts)
at a premium over the comparatively gradual, less panic-inducing upside
scenario (calls), even if the pure statistical distance from "normal"
is similar for both.

## Indian Market Example
On a typical NSE Nifty option chain, an OTM put roughly 400 points below
the current level frequently shows meaningfully higher IV than an OTM
call roughly 400 points above — reflecting Indian equity index skew
consistent with the global equity pattern, and a factor traders
constructing iron condors (`[21.10]`) account for when the put and call
sides don't naturally offer symmetric premium at equidistant strikes.
**Hypothetical/illustrative characterization.**

## US Market Example
S&P 500 (SPX) options have exhibited a persistent downside skew since
at least the 1987 crash — a structural shift in how the market prices
tail risk that predates and postdates the specific historical episode
that is widely credited with entrenching it into standard US index
options pricing behavior.

## Historical Example
The pronounced equity index skew pattern is widely attributed by
options historians to the aftermath of the October 1987 "Black Monday"
crash, when the S&P 500 fell over 20% in a single session — an event
that permanently altered how the market priced the probability of
sudden, severe downside moves, embedding a lasting skew into equity
index option pricing that persists in both US and Indian index options
markets today.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional volatility desks actively trade skew itself as a distinct
strategy — for example, selling relatively overpriced OTM puts against
buying relatively underpriced OTM calls (a "risk reversal") — treating
the *shape* of the volatility curve, not just its overall level, as a
tradeable signal independent of Vega (`[22.05]`) alone.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders constructing spreads or iron condors (`[21.08]`,
`[21.10]`) should check IV across the specific strikes being used
rather than assuming symmetric pricing — skew means a put spread and an
otherwise-equidistant call spread on the same underlying will often
collect meaningfully different net credit, purely from this IV pattern.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders track how skew *itself* changes over time — a
steepening skew (put IV rising relative to call IV) often signals
rising hedging demand or fear even before the overall VIX/India VIX
level moves dramatically, making skew a potentially earlier or more
granular signal than the aggregate volatility index alone.

## Common Mistakes
- Assuming all strikes on an option chain carry roughly the same IV,
  leading to mispriced expectations for spread/strangle net credit.
- Constructing a "symmetric" iron condor (`[21.10]`) by distance from
  ATM without checking that the actual IV — and thus premium — is
  rarely symmetric due to skew.
- Confusing skew (a general downward slope) with smile (a symmetric
  U-shape) — the two describe different market structures.

## Misconceptions
- **"IV is the same across every strike for a given expiry."** Skew
  and smile patterns are the norm, not flat, uniform IV.
- **"Skew and smile are the same thing."** Skew is a directional slope
  (typically put IV > call IV for equities); smile is a symmetric
  U-shape more common in other asset classes.
- **"Skew is a temporary anomaly that will eventually disappear."** It
  has persisted as a structural feature of equity index options pricing
  since 1987, not a fleeting mispricing.

## Key Takeaways
- Volatility skew: IV differs systematically across strikes — for
  equity indices, typically OTM puts carry higher IV than OTM calls.
- Volatility smile: a more symmetric pattern, both OTM wings elevated
  versus ATM — more common in currency/commodity markets.
- Equity index skew is widely attributed to the 1987 Black Monday crash
  permanently altering downside-risk pricing.
- Skew directly affects spread/strangle/iron condor net credit — always
  check actual chain IV, not just strike distance from ATM.

## Summary
Volatility skew — the tendency for OTM puts to carry higher implied
volatility than equidistant OTM calls in equity index options — reflects
a persistent, structural market pricing of downside tail risk that
traces back to the 1987 crash, distinct from the more symmetric
volatility smile seen in other asset classes, and directly affects how
multi-leg strategies from Module 21 price in practice. The next lesson
turns to volatility regimes and mean reversion — how implied volatility
itself tends to cluster into calm and turbulent periods over time.

## Quiz
1. What is volatility skew?
   a) IV is identical across all strikes  b) IV differs systematically across strikes  c) A type of candlestick pattern  d) A margin requirement

2. For equity indices, which typically carries higher IV?
   a) OTM calls  b) OTM puts  c) They're always equal  d) ATM options always highest

3. What historical event is widely credited with entrenching equity index skew?
   a) 2008 GFC  b) The 1987 Black Monday crash  c) Dot-com bubble  d) COVID crash

4. How does skew affect an iron condor's construction?
   a) It has no effect  b) Put and call spreads at equidistant strikes often collect different net credit  c) It eliminates all risk  d) It only affects futures

5. True or False: Volatility skew and volatility smile describe the same pattern.
   a) True
   b) False

## Answers
1. **b)** — IV differs systematically across strikes.
2. **b)** — OTM puts typically carry higher IV for equity indices.
3. **b)** — The October 1987 Black Monday crash.
4. **b)** — Put and call spreads at equidistant strikes often collect different net credit due to skew.
5. **b) False** — Skew is a directional slope; smile is a symmetric U-shape — different patterns.
