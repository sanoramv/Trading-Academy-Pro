## Core Concepts

### Definition
A **futures contract** is a standardized, legally binding agreement,
traded on a regulated exchange, to buy or sell a specific quantity of an
underlying asset (a stock, an index, a commodity, a currency) at a
predetermined price, on a predetermined future date. Unlike simply
buying a stock in the spot/cash market (`[01.03]`), a futures contract
does not require full upfront payment of the contract's total value —
instead, traders post a fraction of the contract value as **margin**
(`[20.04]`, previewed), creating inherent leverage that magnifies both
potential gains and potential losses.

### Intuition
A futures contract is fundamentally a *commitment about the future*,
not an immediate transfer of ownership — buying a futures contract
doesn't mean you own the underlying asset today; it means you've agreed
to a specific price for a transaction that will settle later (or, more
commonly for index/stock futures, that gets cash-settled based on price
differences without physical delivery ever occurring).

### Visual Explanation
Unlike a private, customized agreement between two specific parties (a
forward contract, `[20.02]`), a futures contract's standardized terms
(lot size, expiry date) are fixed by the exchange itself, and the
contract trades continuously on the exchange throughout its life — any
trader can buy or sell the contract at the current market price at any
time before expiry, without needing to find a specific counterparty.

## Real World Analogy
A futures contract is like agreeing today to buy a specific quantity of
wheat from a farmer at a fixed price for delivery three months from now
— you don't pay the full amount today, and you don't receive the wheat
today; you've simply locked in a price for a future transaction, and the
value of that agreement itself fluctuates in the meantime based on how
wheat's actual expected future price changes.

## Indian Market Example
Indian traders access Nifty 50 and Bank Nifty index futures (`[03.08]`)
through the NSE's F&O segment — a trader buying one lot of Nifty futures
posts only a fraction of the contract's total notional value as margin,
creating leverage that can amplify returns (or losses) considerably
beyond what an equivalent cash-market Nifty ETF investment would produce.

## US Market Example
US traders access broad index exposure through CME's E-mini S&P 500
futures (ES) — a heavily traded, highly liquid contract used by both
institutional hedgers and individual speculators, illustrating futures'
dual role as both a risk-management tool (`[20.08]`) and a speculative
instrument (`[20.09]`).

## Historical Example
Modern futures trading traces its organized origins to 19th-century
agricultural commodity markets (the Chicago Board of Trade, founded
1848, closely connected to `[15.01]`'s Market Profile origins at CBOT
decades later) — farmers and grain merchants used early futures-like
agreements to manage price uncertainty between planting and harvest,
the same core hedging function futures continue to serve today.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional investors use index futures extensively for portfolio-level
hedging and efficient market exposure — futures allow gaining or reducing
broad market exposure quickly and with lower transaction costs than
trading the full underlying basket of individual securities directly.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders new to futures should specifically internalize that
leverage — the defining structural feature distinguishing futures from
simple spot-market stock ownership — magnifies losses just as much as
gains, making proper risk management (Module 21, previewed) considerably
more critical than in unleveraged spot-market trading.

## Professional Insights
Professional futures traders emphasize understanding the complete
contract specification (`[20.03]`) — lot size, tick size, expiry
mechanics — before ever placing a trade, since miscalculating position
sizing due to unfamiliarity with a contract's specific multiplier can
lead to dramatically larger real-money exposure than a trader intended.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating a futures contract like simple stock ownership, without
  appreciating the leverage and margin mechanics involved.
- Not understanding a specific contract's exact lot size and multiplier
  before sizing a position.
- Confusing futures with forwards, missing the key standardization and
  exchange-traded distinctions covered fully in `[20.02]`.

## Misconceptions
- **"Buying a futures contract means you immediately own the underlying
  asset."** It represents a commitment about a future transaction, not
  immediate ownership — most index futures settle in cash, without any
  physical delivery ever occurring.
- **"Futures require paying the full contract value upfront."** Only a
  fraction (margin) is required upfront, creating inherent leverage.
- **"Futures contracts are customized agreements between two specific
  parties."** They are standardized, exchange-traded instruments — the
  key distinction from forward contracts, covered in `[20.02]`.

## Key Takeaways
- A futures contract is a standardized, exchange-traded agreement to buy
  or sell an underlying asset at a predetermined price on a future date.
- Only a fraction of the contract's value (margin) is required upfront,
  creating leverage.
- Positions are marked-to-market daily, with gains/losses flowing
  through the margin account.
- Most index futures cash-settle at expiry, without physical delivery.

## Summary
A futures contract — a standardized, exchange-traded, leveraged
agreement to transact an underlying asset at a future date — represents
this academy's first formal derivatives instrument, building on
`[03.08]`'s earlier F&O overview with full mechanical detail. This sets
up the next lesson's direct comparison of futures against forwards and
spot transactions, clarifying exactly what standardization and exchange-
trading add to the basic futures concept.

2. **b)** — A fraction of contract value posted upfront, creating leverage.
3. **b)** — Daily settlement of gains/losses through the margin account.
4. **b)** — No, most cash-settle at expiry.
5. **b) False** — It represents a commitment about a future transaction, not immediate ownership.
