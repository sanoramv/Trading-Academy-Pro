## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Initial margin** is the upfront deposit (typically 10-15% of a
contract's notional value for index futures, though this varies and is
set by the exchange's risk framework, SPAN margin methodology in India)
required to open a futures position. **Maintenance margin** is the
minimum account balance that must be maintained — if losses erode the
account below this threshold, a **margin call** requires the trader to
deposit additional funds or have the position forcibly closed. **Mark-
to-market (MTM)** is the daily process of settling gains and losses
based on each day's closing price — unlike spot positions, futures P&L
is realized (credited or debited to the trading account) every single
trading day, not only when the position is eventually closed. **Leverage**
is the resulting ratio between total notional position value and the
actual capital (margin) required — since margin represents only a
fraction of notional value, percentage gains and losses on the margin
capital are magnified considerably relative to the underlying's actual
percentage price movement.

### Intuition
Because only a fraction of a position's total value is required as
margin, even a modest percentage move in the underlying asset translates
into a considerably larger percentage change relative to the trader's
actual invested capital — this is the core mechanism making futures both
more capital-efficient and meaningfully riskier than equivalent
unleveraged spot-market positions.

### Mental Model
```
LEVERAGE CALCULATION EXAMPLE (illustrative figures)

  Notional value of 1 lot = Rs 6,00,000
  Initial margin required = Rs 90,000 (15% of notional)
  Effective LEVERAGE = 6,00,000 / 90,000 = ~6.7x

  If the underlying moves +2%:
    Notional value gain = 2% x 6,00,000 = Rs 12,000
    Return ON MARGIN CAPITAL = 12,000 / 90,000 = ~13.3%
    (a 2% underlying move becomes a ~13.3% return on the
     ACTUAL capital deployed -- leverage MAGNIFIES both
     gains AND losses by roughly this same ratio)
```

### Visual Explanation
Mark-to-market means a futures trader experiences the full economic
reality of daily price swings immediately, credited or debited to their
margin account each day — unlike a long-term spot investor who might
mentally treat unrealized losses as "not real yet," a futures trader's
margin account balance directly, immediately reflects every day's
gains and losses.

### ASCII Diagram
```
   MARK-TO-MARKET DAILY CYCLE

  Day 1: Buy 1 lot at 24,000. Post initial margin.
         Account balance = Initial margin

  Day 2: Price closes at 24,100 (+100 points)
         MTM gain = 100 x lot size, CREDITED to account
         Account balance = Initial margin + MTM gain

  Day 3: Price closes at 23,950 (-150 points from Day 2)
         MTM loss = 150 x lot size, DEBITED from account
         Account balance = previous balance - MTM loss
         IF balance falls below MAINTENANCE MARGIN ->
         MARGIN CALL issued -- deposit more funds or
         position gets forcibly closed
```

## Real World Analogy
Leverage in futures is like buying a house with a mortgage rather than
paying cash — a relatively small down payment (margin) controls a much
larger total asset value (notional value), meaning even a modest
percentage change in the house's value translates into a considerably
larger percentage change relative to your actual invested down payment,
for better (property appreciation) or worse (property decline,
potentially even exceeding your down payment amount).

## Indian Market Example
Indian Nifty futures traders post SPAN margin (a standardized risk-based
margin calculation methodology used across Indian exchanges) when opening
a position — if a sharp adverse move erodes the account below maintenance
margin during a volatile trading session, the trader's broker issues a
margin call, typically requiring same-day or next-day fund deposit to
avoid automatic position liquidation.

## US Market Example
US futures traders on CME-listed contracts similarly post initial margin
set by the exchange's risk methodology (SPAN or similar frameworks) —
during periods of unusually high volatility, exchanges can and do raise
margin requirements intraday, sometimes triggering margin calls even for
positions that haven't moved as much as a trader might expect, purely due
to the margin requirement itself increasing.

## Historical Example
The margin and mark-to-market system, combined with the clearinghouse
structure (`[20.02]`), represents futures markets' core risk-management
innovation — this daily settlement mechanism specifically prevents the
kind of catastrophic, accumulated, unrealized-loss scenarios that
historically contributed to counterparty defaults in less-regulated
forward-contract markets.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional risk managers specifically monitor margin utilization
across their entire derivatives portfolio in real time, maintaining
substantial capital buffers above minimum maintenance margin requirements
specifically to avoid forced, poorly-timed liquidations during volatile
periods — a risk-management discipline retail traders should
specifically emulate at appropriate scale.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders new to futures should specifically avoid using maximum
available leverage simply because the exchange permits it — maintaining
meaningfully more capital than the bare minimum margin requirement
provides crucial buffer against margin calls during ordinary market
volatility, a discipline this academy will develop further in Module
21's risk management coverage.

## Professional Insights
Professional traders emphasize that leverage is fundamentally a
double-edged, symmetric tool — it magnifies losses exactly as
proportionally as it magnifies gains, meaning undisciplined use of high
leverage is a leading cause of rapid, catastrophic account losses among
inexperienced futures traders, a pattern professional risk management
practices are specifically designed to prevent.

## Common Mistakes
- Using maximum available leverage without appreciating how proportionally
  it magnifies potential losses, not just potential gains.
- Maintaining account balances too close to the maintenance margin
  threshold, leaving no buffer against ordinary volatility.
- Not understanding that mark-to-market means losses are realized daily,
  not merely "on paper" until a position is eventually closed.

## Misconceptions
- **"Leverage only magnifies gains, not losses."** It magnifies both
  proportionally and symmetrically — this is leverage's defining,
  double-edged characteristic.
- **"Unrealized futures losses aren't 'real' until the position is
  closed, similar to how some spot investors think about paper
  losses."** Mark-to-market means futures losses are realized (debited
  from the account) daily, not merely theoretical until closure.
- **"Margin requirements are fixed and never change."** Exchanges can
  and do adjust margin requirements, sometimes intraday during periods of
  unusually high volatility.

## Key Takeaways
- Initial margin is the upfront deposit required to open a position;
  maintenance margin is the minimum balance that must be sustained.
- Mark-to-market realizes gains/losses daily, not only at position
  closure.
- Leverage magnifies percentage returns on actual capital deployed,
  proportionally for both gains and losses.
- Maintaining capital buffers above minimum margin requirements is a
  crucial risk-management discipline.

## Summary
Margin, mark-to-market, and leverage together define futures trading's
core risk-and-reward mechanics — a relatively small upfront deposit
controls a much larger notional position, with daily mark-to-market
realizing gains and losses immediately, and leverage symmetrically
magnifying both potential outcomes relative to actual capital deployed.
This sets up the next lesson's treatment of basis, contango, and
backwardation — the specific relationship between futures prices and
the underlying spot price.
