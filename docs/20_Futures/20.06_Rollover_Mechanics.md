## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Rollover** is the process of closing a futures position in the
expiring (near-month) contract and simultaneously opening an equivalent
position in the next month's contract, allowing a trader to maintain
continuous market exposure beyond a single contract's expiry date. Indian
equity and index F&O contracts follow a **monthly expiry cycle**, with
three contract months (near, next, and far month) available for trading
simultaneously at any given time, and the near-month contract expiring on
the exchange's current designated expiry day each month (last Tuesday as
of September 2025, previously last Thursday — verify the current
convention, `[20.03]`).
As the near-month contract approaches expiry, liquidity typically shifts
toward the next-month contract, and traders wishing to maintain their
position must actively execute the rollover before (or exactly at)
expiry.

### Intuition
Because futures contracts have a finite life (unlike spot-market stock
ownership, which can be held indefinitely), any trader wanting continuous
exposure beyond a single expiry must actively "roll" their position
forward — this isn't automatic; it requires deliberately closing the
expiring contract and opening a new position in the next contract month,
incurring transaction costs and being directly affected by any basis
difference between the two contract months (`[20.05]`).

### Visual Explanation
Rollover activity concentrates heavily in the days immediately preceding
expiry (particularly on and around expiry day itself), and exchanges
publish "rollover percentage" data — the proportion of open interest in
the expiring contract that has been rolled to the next month, versus
allowed to expire — which traders use as a sentiment indicator,
independent of price action alone.

## Real World Analogy
Rollover is like renewing a short-term lease before it expires if you
want to keep living in the same type of apartment — rather than the
lease automatically continuing indefinitely, you must actively sign a
new agreement (potentially at a different rent, analogous to basis
difference) before the old one runs out, or you'll need to vacate
(equivalent to letting the futures position expire without rolling).

## Indian Market Example
Indian Nifty futures traders wanting to maintain a long position beyond
the current month's expiry (last Tuesday as of September 2025, previously
last Thursday) must actively roll their
position to the next month's contract, typically executing this in the
final week before expiry when next-month liquidity has become
sufficiently robust — many platforms offer a combined "rollover" order
type that executes both legs (closing near month, opening next month)
together.

## US Market Example
US futures traders, particularly in commodity and index futures,
similarly must actively roll positions before expiry — CME and other
exchanges publish detailed open-interest data by contract month, which
traders monitor to gauge when liquidity has sufficiently shifted to the
next contract for efficient rollover execution.

## Historical Example
The concept of "rollover percentage" as a sentiment indicator gained
particular prominence in Indian derivatives market commentary, where
financial media routinely report monthly Nifty/Bank Nifty rollover
figures compared to historical averages as one input among many for
assessing prevailing market positioning and sentiment ahead of each
expiry.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional traders managing large positions plan rollover execution
carefully to minimize market impact — often spreading rollover
transactions across several days before expiry rather than executing
the entire position's rollover in a single transaction, to avoid moving
the market against themselves during the less liquid near-expiry window.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders should specifically plan their rollover timing in advance
rather than waiting until the very last trading day, when near-month
liquidity can thin considerably and bid-ask spreads may widen —
proactive rollover planning avoids unnecessary transaction cost and
execution risk.

## Professional Insights
Professional analysts specifically use rollover percentage data as one
input (among several) for gauging market conviction — unusually high
rollover percentages combined with a specific directional price bias can
suggest strong conviction behind that prevailing trend, though this
indicator, like others in this academy's toolkit, should be combined
with confirmation rather than used in isolation.

## Common Mistakes
- Waiting until the very last trading day to execute rollover, incurring
  potentially wider spreads and reduced liquidity.
- Ignoring basis differences between contract months when calculating
  rollover cost/benefit.
- Treating rollover percentage data as a standalone, sufficient signal
  without additional confirmation.

## Misconceptions
- **"Futures positions automatically continue indefinitely without any
  action required."** Rollover requires active, deliberate execution —
  positions do not automatically continue past expiry.
- **"Rollover has no cost or benefit associated with it."** Basis
  differences between contract months (`[20.05]`) directly translate
  into rollover cost or benefit, in addition to transaction costs.
- **"Rollover percentage alone is a sufficient, standalone trading
  signal."** It functions best as one input among several, combined with
  price action and other confirmation tools.

## Key Takeaways
- Rollover is the active process of closing an expiring near-month
  position and opening an equivalent next-month position.
- Indian F&O follows a monthly expiry cycle, with three contract months
  trading simultaneously and near-month expiring on the last Tuesday as
  of September 2025 (previously last Thursday — verify the current day).
- Rollover cost/benefit is directly determined by basis differences
  between contract months (`[20.05]`).
- Exchange-published rollover percentage data serves as an additional
  sentiment indicator, best combined with other confirmation.

## Summary
Rollover mechanics — the active process of transitioning a futures
position from an expiring near-month contract to the next contract
month — require deliberate execution, direct attention to basis
differences (`[20.05]`) affecting cost/benefit, and careful timing to
avoid reduced near-expiry liquidity. This sets up the next lesson's
treatment of index futures versus stock futures — comparing the two
primary categories of Indian equity-linked futures contracts covered
throughout this module.
