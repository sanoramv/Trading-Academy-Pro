## Core Concepts

### Definition
**Basis** is the difference between a futures contract's price and the
underlying asset's current spot price (Basis = Futures Price − Spot
Price). When futures trade at a **premium** to spot (positive basis),
the market is in **contango** — the more common condition for most
financial futures, reflecting the cost of carrying a position forward
(financing costs, dividends foregone, storage for commodities). When
futures trade at a **discount** to spot (negative basis), the market is
in **backwardation** — less common for equity index futures, but
frequent in certain commodity markets, often reflecting near-term supply
tightness or unusually high current demand for immediate delivery.

### Intuition
Basis exists because a futures price isn't simply "today's price
projected forward unchanged" — it reflects the net cost (or benefit) of
holding the underlying asset until the future expiry date, primarily
driven by financing costs (the "cost of carry") minus any income the
underlying generates (like dividends) during that holding period; as
expiry approaches, this cost-of-carry period shrinks toward zero,
causing basis to naturally converge toward zero as well.

### Mental Model
```
BASIS = FUTURES PRICE - SPOT PRICE

CONTANGO (basis POSITIVE, futures > spot):
  Reflects COST OF CARRY (financing cost minus dividend
  yield) -- the MORE COMMON condition for equity index
  futures, since financing cost typically exceeds
  dividend yield

BACKWARDATION (basis NEGATIVE, futures < spot):
  Futures trade BELOW spot -- can occur when dividend
  yield exceeds financing cost, or in commodities during
  near-term supply tightness/high immediate demand

BASIS CONVERGENCE: as expiry approaches, the remaining
"cost of carry" period shrinks -> basis converges toward
ZERO -> at expiry, futures price = spot price (by
definition, since final settlement is based on spot)
```

### Visual Explanation
Basis convergence is not optional or probabilistic — it is a structural
certainty, since a futures contract's final settlement price at expiry
is directly determined by (or, for cash-settled contracts, directly
equals) the underlying spot price at that time, meaning any existing
basis must mathematically shrink to zero by the time expiry actually
arrives.

## Real World Analogy
Basis is like the difference between a concert ticket's price today for
an event happening in six months versus buying admission on the actual
event day — the advance ticket might cost slightly more (reflecting the
seller's cost of holding inventory and financing risk over those six
months, similar to contango) or, in unusual cases, slightly less (if the
seller urgently needs cash now, similar to backwardation) — but as the
event date approaches, this price difference naturally shrinks toward
zero, since there's less and less time remaining for that cost-of-carry
difference to matter.

## Indian Market Example
Indian Nifty futures typically trade in mild contango during normal
market conditions, reflecting financing costs generally exceeding the
Nifty 50's dividend yield — traders specifically monitor this
basis/premium level, since an unusually wide premium can signal
elevated bullish speculative positioning, while a narrowing or negative
basis can signal bearish sentiment or unusual market stress.

## US Market Example
US index futures similarly trade predominantly in contango under normal
conditions, though commodity futures markets (crude oil, natural gas)
frequently shift between contango and backwardation based on near-term
supply/demand dynamics — the 2020 crude oil futures episode, where
extreme oversupply and storage constraints drove unusual pricing
dynamics, is a widely-referenced historical example of dramatic
commodity-market backwardation/contango shifts.

## Historical Example
The theoretical cost-of-carry model explaining basis, contango, and
backwardation was formalized within financial economics literature
during the mid-20th century, building on earlier commodity-market
observations dating back to futures markets' 19th-century origins
(`[20.01]`) — providing a rigorous, mathematical foundation for a
phenomenon commodity traders had empirically observed for considerably
longer.

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional traders specifically monitor basis levels as a sentiment
and positioning indicator — an unusually elevated contango premium in
index futures can suggest excessive speculative long positioning,
information institutional risk desks incorporate into their broader
market assessment alongside other indicators covered throughout this
academy.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders should specifically understand basis behavior before
their first rollover experience (`[20.06]`) — a position held into
expiry or rolled to the next contract month will be directly affected by
basis convergence and any difference in basis levels between contract
months, a cost (or occasionally benefit) worth understanding in advance.

## Professional Insights
Professional arbitrage desks specifically monitor basis for pricing
inefficiencies — if futures trade at a basis level meaningfully
inconsistent with the theoretical cost-of-carry model (too wide or too
narrow relative to financing costs and dividend expectations), arbitrage
opportunities can emerge, and professional trading activity tends to
correct such mispricings relatively quickly.

## Common Mistakes
- Assuming futures prices should always exactly equal the spot price,
  without understanding basis's cost-of-carry foundation.
- Ignoring basis differences between contract months when planning a
  rollover, potentially incurring unexpected costs.
- Not recognizing basis convergence as a structural certainty rather
  than merely a probable tendency.

## Misconceptions
- **"Futures prices should always exactly match spot prices."** Basis
  (the difference between them) reflects genuine cost-of-carry economics
  and is a normal, expected feature of futures pricing, not a market
  inefficiency.
- **"Contango and backwardation are randomly occurring, unpredictable
  conditions."** They reflect systematic economic factors (financing
  costs, dividend yields, supply/demand for immediate delivery) that can
  be reasoned about, even though their exact magnitude can vary.
- **"Basis convergence is merely a probable tendency, not certain."** It
  is a structural mathematical certainty, since final settlement directly
  ties the futures price to the spot price at expiry.

## Key Takeaways
- Basis = Futures Price − Spot Price; contango is positive basis
  (futures premium), backwardation is negative basis (futures discount).
- Contango typically reflects cost of carry (financing costs minus
  dividend yield); backwardation can reflect high dividend yield or,
  in commodities, near-term supply tightness.
- Basis converges to zero as expiry approaches — a structural certainty,
  not a mere tendency.
- Basis behavior directly affects rollover cost/benefit, covered fully
  in `[20.06]`.

## Summary
Basis — the difference between futures and spot prices — reflects
cost-of-carry economics, manifesting as contango (futures premium,
typical for equity index futures) or backwardation (futures discount),
and structurally converges to zero as expiry approaches since final
settlement ties futures price directly to spot price. This sets up the
next lesson's treatment of rollover mechanics — the practical process of
transitioning a position from an expiring contract to the next contract
month, directly affected by the basis dynamics covered here.
