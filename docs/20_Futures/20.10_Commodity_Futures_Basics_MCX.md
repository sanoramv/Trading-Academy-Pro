## Core Concepts

### Definition
The **Multi Commodity Exchange of India (MCX)** is India's primary
commodity derivatives exchange, offering futures contracts on precious
metals (gold, silver), base metals (copper, zinc, aluminum), energy
(crude oil, natural gas), and select agricultural commodities. While
sharing the core futures mechanics already covered throughout this
module (`[20.01]`-`[20.09]`) — margin, mark-to-market, leverage, expiry
cycles — commodity futures introduce **physical delivery** as a
practically relevant consideration for many contracts (unlike cash-
settled index futures, `[20.07]`), along with commodity-specific price
drivers connecting directly to `[06.08]`'s commodity cycle discussion.

### Intuition
Commodity futures apply the identical structural framework this module
has built — standardized contracts, margin and leverage, mark-to-market,
expiry and rollover — to a fundamentally different underlying asset
class, where physical, real-world supply and demand factors (weather,
geopolitical supply disruptions, industrial demand cycles) play a more
direct, immediate role in price discovery than the equity-market factors
(earnings, macro sentiment) driving index and stock futures.

### Visual Explanation
Unlike Indian equity index futures, which always cash-settle
(`[20.07]`), many MCX commodity contracts offer (or require, depending
on the specific contract and trader's position) physical delivery
options — traders who don't intend to take or make physical delivery
must specifically close or roll their position before the delivery
notice period begins, a consideration requiring more active attention
than cash-settled index futures.

## Real World Analogy
Commodity futures are like applying the same standardized "future
delivery agreement" concept already covered for stock indices to
physical, tangible goods — the contractual mechanics (margin, daily
settlement, standardized lot sizes) work identically, but the underlying
"cargo" being priced is a physical commodity subject to real-world
supply disruptions (a mine closure, a poor harvest, a pipeline outage)
rather than a company's abstract financial performance.

## Indian Market Example
Indian traders and businesses use MCX gold futures both for speculative
purposes and for genuine hedging — jewelers and bullion dealers
specifically use gold futures to hedge inventory price risk, while retail
speculative traders use the same contracts to express views on gold
price direction, often influenced by rupee-dollar exchange rate movements
(`[04.04]`) given gold's dollar-denominated global pricing.

## US Market Example
US commodity futures trading, centered on exchanges like CME Group
(which includes NYMEX for energy and COMEX for metals), similarly serves
both genuine commercial hedging needs (airlines hedging fuel costs, food
companies hedging grain costs) and substantial speculative trading
volume, illustrating the same dual hedging/speculation purpose this
module has developed throughout.

## Historical Example
Commodity futures represent the historically original application of
futures markets — predating financial futures (stock index futures,
which only emerged considerably later in the 20th century) by many
decades, tracing back to the same 19th-century Chicago Board of Trade
agricultural origins already referenced throughout this module
(`[20.01]`).

## Institutional Perspective
Institutional commodity trading desks specifically combine this module's
technical futures mechanics with deep fundamental analysis of physical
supply/demand factors (`[06.08]`) — unlike equity index futures, where
broad macro and market sentiment dominate, commodity futures pricing
often requires genuine expertise in the specific physical market's
real-world supply chain dynamics.

## Retail Perspective
Retail traders new to commodity futures should specifically recognize
that while the mechanical framework (margin, leverage, expiry) transfers
directly from this module's equity/index futures coverage, the
fundamental analysis required (weather patterns, geopolitical supply
disruptions, industrial demand cycles) is meaningfully different from
equity analysis, requiring dedicated additional study before active
commodity speculation.

## Professional Insights
Professional commodity traders emphasize paying particularly close
attention to delivery notice periods and specific contract delivery
mechanics — unlike index futures' straightforward cash settlement,
inadvertently holding a commodity futures position into physical
delivery obligations without proper preparation can create genuinely
significant, unexpected logistical and financial complications.

## Common Mistakes
- Assuming all commodity futures cash-settle like index futures, without
  checking specific contract delivery mechanics.
- Applying equity-market fundamental analysis frameworks directly to
  commodities without recognizing meaningfully different price drivers.
- Not accounting for delivery notice periods when planning to close or
  roll a position.

## Misconceptions
- **"All futures contracts, including commodities, cash-settle like
  index futures."** Many commodity contracts offer or require physical
  delivery — always verify the specific contract's settlement mechanics.
- **"Commodity futures respond to the same fundamental drivers as equity
  index futures."** Commodity prices are often driven by physical
  supply/demand factors (weather, geopolitical supply, industrial
  demand) meaningfully distinct from equity-market drivers.
- **"The core futures mechanics (margin, leverage, mark-to-market) don't
  apply to commodities."** They apply identically — commodities differ
  primarily in underlying asset characteristics and delivery mechanics,
  not core contractual structure.

## Key Takeaways
- MCX is India's primary commodity derivatives exchange, covering
  precious metals, base metals, energy, and select agricultural
  commodities.
- Commodity futures share this module's core mechanics (margin,
  mark-to-market, leverage, expiry) but introduce physical delivery as a
  practically relevant consideration for many contracts.
- Commodity price drivers (weather, geopolitical supply, industrial
  demand) differ meaningfully from equity-market drivers.
- Always verify a specific commodity contract's delivery mechanics and
  notice period before holding a position toward expiry.

   a) Dividends  b) Physical delivery  c) Stock splits  d) Earnings reports

   a) Company earnings  b) Physical supply/demand factors (weather, geopolitics, industrial demand)  c) Stock buybacks  d) Dividend policy

   b) False

3. **b)** — Physical delivery.
4. **b)** — Physical supply/demand factors.
